## T1 Navigation & IA labels

**Consumer nav is four items, and only one is the product** `[observed]`

`Medical library` · `Help` · `App` · `Partner with Ada` · language selector

The symptom assessment — the thing the hero sells — **is not in the nav.** It is reachable only from the hero CTA. The persistent navigation offers reading material, support, the app store and a B2B off-ramp. This is deliberate: Ada's nav is what you browse, and the assessment is what you *start*, once, on purpose.

**`Partner with Ada`** routes to a different domain (`about.ada.com`) with its own IA (`Our solutions` → `Care utilization` / `Patient finding`, `Research and publications`, `News`, `Contact us`). The consumer and enterprise registers are cleanly separated by domain, and the clinical-evidence content lives on the enterprise side. A worried layperson on ada.com sees almost none of the accuracy claims.

**Help centre — five topics, gerund- and possessive-led** `[observed]`

| Topic (verbatim) | Register |
|---|---|
| `My data Privacy and Security` | First-person possessive |
| `Managing my Account` | Gerund + first-person possessive |
| `Using The Ada App` | Gerund |
| `Ada as a Company` | Third-person, about the org |
| `Signing Up & Getting Started` | Paired gerunds |

Three of five use **`my`** — `My data`, `Managing my Account`. Ada's help IA speaks in the user's voice about the user's possessions, where Wise's speaks in gerunds about activities. Note the inconsistent capitalisation (`My data Privacy and Security`, `Using The Ada App`) — sentence case and title case are mixed within single labels. A visible content-ops gap.

**Medical Library IA is an A–Z letter index** `[observed]` with ~600 entries under `A` through `Z`, mixing condition names (`Acute Appendicitis`), symptom pages (`Abdominal Pain`), biomarker pages (`HDL Cholesterol`), hormone pages (`Testosterone`), sub-guides (`COVID-19 Guide`, `Thyroid Guide`, `Cardiovascular Disease Guide`, `Micronutrients Guide`, `Sexual Health Guide`, `Hormones Guide`, `Biomarkers Guide`) and a large `Signs of …` family. Alphabetical-only. **There is no symptom-first or body-region entry point** — the user must already know the word. For a product whose entire thesis is that laypeople cannot name their condition, the library IA contradicts the assessment's premise.

**The `Signs of …` family** `[observed]` is the exception and the interesting one: `Signs of Stroke`, `Signs of Depression`, `Signs of Miscarriage`, `Signs of a Concussion`, `Signs of Herpes`, `Signs of Kidney Problems`, `Signs of Postpartum Depression`, `Signs of Panic Attack`, `Signs of burnout`, `Signs of Autism`, `Signs of Ear Infection`, `Signs of Hemorrhoids`, `Signs of Shingles: Herpes Zoster`. These live outside `/conditions/` at bare root paths (`ada.com/signs-of-stroke/`). This is the **worried-searcher** content tier — phrased as the query a frightened person types, not as the clinical noun.

**Footer** `[observed]`: `For partners` · `Legal` (`Imprint`, `Privacy Policy`, `Cookie Policy`, `Terms & Conditions`, `Consent Settings`) · `Find us on` · `Contact us`. Plus two certification badges rendered as images on **every page**: `ISO 27001`, `EN ISO 13485`. Persistent visual trust signalling at the foot of a symptom-checker — the compliance artefact used as reassurance furniture.

## T2 Value proposition & headline patterns

**Hero — first-person self-description** `[observed]`

> `I'm Ada, an AI-powered symptom assessment optimized by clinicians.`

The single most consequential sentence in this file. Four moves in fifteen words:

1. **`I'm Ada`** — the product speaks as itself. Not "Ada is", not "Meet Ada". A named first-person agent, which is what makes a branching interrogation feel like a conversation rather than a form.
2. **`an AI-powered symptom assessment`** — it declares its own mechanism unprompted. Not "health companion", not "doctor in your pocket". The AI disclosure is in the first sentence, not buried.
3. **`symptom assessment`** — the category noun, chosen over "symptom checker" here even though "symptom checker" is used everywhere else on the site (see T13).
4. **`optimized by clinicians`** — the human-oversight claim, in a past participle that positions clinicians as having *tuned* the AI rather than supervising each output. Precise and carefully limited: it does not say "reviewed by a doctor" or "doctor-approved".

The whole proposition is: *an AI that says it is an AI, shaped by humans, that assesses rather than diagnoses.* Every word of the hedging strategy is already present in the H1.

**CTA under the hero** `[observed]`: `Start symptom assessment`, prefixed with an AI icon (`ai.svg`). Verb-first, names the exact artefact.

**Meta description uses a different, warmer voice** `[observed]`: "Hi. We're Ada. Our symptom assessment helps people manage their health, and our enterprise solutions help our partners improve health and care." Note the switch from `I'm Ada` (page) to `We're Ada` (meta) — singular agent in the product, plural company in the search result. Two identities for two contexts.

**Four-pillar value prop on /app/** `[observed]` — adjective label + one-line mechanism:

| Pillar | Line |
|---|---|
| `Symptom checker` | "With our free symptom checker, built on AI and clinical evidence." |
| `Accurate` | "Clinical studies show Ada is the most accurate symptom checker app." |
| `Confidential` | "We protect your data in accordance with the highest data protection standards. Your data. Your decisions." |
| `Trusted` | "Ada is one of the most popular symptom checker apps. Rated 4.75/5 on Android and 4.8/5 on iOS." |

`Your data. Your decisions.` — two two-word sentences. Note the second one: the promise is not just data control but **decision** ownership, which is the same boundary move as the medical disclaimer, restated as a benefit.

**Scale numbers** `[observed]`: `15 million users` · `30 million symptom assessments completed` · `350,000 5-star ratings` · `7 product languages` · `50 in-house medical experts`. Ratings given to two decimal places on Android (`4.75/5`) and one on iOS (`4.8/5`) — an inconsistency, but a truthful-looking one.

**Medical Library headline** `[observed]`: `Good information leads to better health choices.` Sentence with a full stop. The library page repeats the thesis with a stronger verb: `The right health information leads to the right health actions.`

**Section header on /app/ repeated four times**: `For better health` `[observed]` — the same H2 above four different user testimonials. Four identical headings on one page is a template artefact, not a decision.

## T5 Form & field labels — symptom-assessment question design

**This is the priority section. Read the evidence markers carefully: nothing below is an observed question string.** The assessment was not started. What follows is what Ada *publishes about* its own question design, plus the structural constraints its own documents impose.

### What Ada states about how the questions work

**The mechanism, verbatim from T&Cs §4.1** `[observed]`:

> "Ada Assess is a software application that use custom probabilistic reasoning technology on a medical-expert-curated knowledge base to provide Users with health information relevant to medical findings they are currently experiencing."

Three content-design facts sit inside that sentence:

1. **`probabilistic reasoning`** — the branching is Bayesian. Each answer reweights a hypothesis set, which means **question order is not fixed** and the user cannot be shown a progress bar with a meaningful denominator. Any "question 4 of 12" would be a lie. This is the structural reason symptom checkers feel open-ended.
2. **`medical-expert-curated knowledge base`** — the question bank is hand-authored by clinicians, not generated. Every question is a content artefact someone wrote and reviewed.
3. **`medical findings they are currently experiencing`** — note `findings`, not `symptoms`. `Finding` is the clinical term covering both what the patient reports and what is observed. Ada's legal text uses the clinical noun; its consumer text uses `symptoms`. The register split is visible between documents.

**The output artefact is a defined term** `[observed]`, T&Cs §4.1:

> "At the end of the health assessment the User is provided with a report that lists such information as the conditions likely to be causing these findings, and actionable next steps (hereinafter referred to as the 'Assessment Report')."

Two content decisions in the definition: `conditions likely to be causing` (plural, probability-hedged, causal rather than diagnostic) and `actionable next steps` (the advice is bound to be *actionable*, i.e. every result must terminate in something the user can do).

### How severity is escalated — the one hard number Ada publishes

**`8 advice levels`** `[observed]`, from about.ada.com/medical-quality under the pillar `Advice safety`:

> "8 advice levels to offer safe and sensible guidance"

This is the single most useful disclosure for a content designer studying triage. Ada does not have a binary "see a doctor / don't" output — it has **an eight-step ladder of urgency**, and each rung is a distinct piece of authored copy with a distinct clinical threshold behind it. Ada does not publish the eight labels. **They are not reproduced here because they were not observed, and inventing them would be exactly the failure this corpus forbids.**

Two further claims bound the ladder `[observed]`:
- "Recommendations comparable to triage nurses" (cited to Morse, K. et al., J Med Internet Res, 2020)
- "97.9% of user safety and quality feedback addressed within 24 hours"

The second is an **operational** safety claim, not a copy claim, and it is the more telling one: it implies a live pipeline where users flag unsafe output and clinicians correct the knowledge base. The help article `What can I do if Ada missed something?` is the consumer-facing entrance to that pipeline, and it says so `[observed]`: "If Ada misses something significant, we have strict prompt review and correction protocols."

**Coverage claims that bear on question design** `[observed]`: "99% condition coverage including common, rare, high-risk obstetric, pediatric, and mental health conditions" and "33.3% more patients could have had their rare disease identified in their first documented clinical consultation" (cited to Ronicke, S. et al., Orphanet, 2019). The named inclusion of **obstetric, paediatric and mental health** as separately-flagged categories indicates three question sets with distinct safety handling.

### How questions are made answerable without medical knowledge

`[documented]` — Ada does not publish its authoring guidelines. What can be evidenced:

- **The user can propose vocabulary back to the system.** `How do I suggest a symptom?` is a standing help article `[observed]`. A symptom-input field that admits "I can't find my symptom" and routes it to the knowledge team is a question-design decision with a content surface attached.
- **Completion is measured and high.** "91% assessment completion for 1 client" and "97% of patients found Ada easy to use" (cited to Miller, S., Gilbert, et al., JMIR, 2020) `[observed]`. Completion rate is the proxy metric for whether laypeople can answer the questions.
- **The questions are explicitly multi-factor.** A user review reproduced on /app/ `[observed]`: "I really like how it takes to account a variety of factors when asking about symptoms." Reproduced verbatim by Ada with its own disclaimer attached (see T10).
- **The register of the published medical content is the best available proxy for the question register.** See T14 — the Medical Library writes `pain in the lower part of your belly or your back` rather than "lower abdominal or lumbar pain", and `tissue that resembles the lining of the uterus grows in other places` rather than "ectopic endometrial-like tissue". Same medical team, same lay-translation discipline. If the question bank is written by the people who wrote `belly`, the questions are written at that level.

### Result hedging

`[documented]`, from three converging sources:

- T&Cs §4.1: `the conditions likely to be causing these findings` — plural, probabilistic, causal.
- Help, `What can I do if Ada missed something?`: "Ada's information is **not a diagnosis** and shouldn't replace professional medical advice. It offers **possible explanations** for symptoms and **suggests care options**, but it's not a substitute for a doctor's diagnosis."
- T&Cs §9.1: "The information is made available by Ada **as is**, without warranty, guarantee or representation of any kind."

The hedging vocabulary is consistent across legal and support registers: `possible explanations`, `suggests`, `likely to be causing`, `care options`. **Never** `diagnosis`, `result`, `finding` (in consumer copy), or `you have`.

### Named in-app controls

`[documented]`, from help articles — the only real UI strings available:

| Path | Strings |
|---|---|
| Export an assessment | `Profiles` (bottom menu) → profile → `Health assessments` → assessment → menu (top-right) → share → generates a **PDF** |
| Privacy preferences | `⚙️ (Settings)` → `Privacy and notifications` → "Adjust the consent options according to your preferences. **The changes will be saved automatically.**" |

`Health assessments` is the plural noun for the stored artefacts. The auto-save note on the consent screen is a deliberate omission of a Save button on a consent surface — a choice with real consequences either way.

**Field labels, placeholders, validation and hint text: `[absent]`.** None reachable. This is the largest gap in the file and it is the gap that matters most for the benchmark.

## T7 Error, failure & recovery

**The flagship recovery article is about the product being wrong** `[observed]`:

> `What can I do if Ada missed something?`

Title analysis: first person, user's own question, and the verb is `missed` — an omission by the system, stated as the system's failure without euphemism. Not "inaccurate results" or "unexpected output". The body then does four things in order: states the review process, describes who does it ("Our Medical Knowledge Team, consisting of doctors from over 20 countries and various specialties"), names the correction protocol, and gives a concrete reporting route — "Provide your User ID or contact us using the email linked to your Ada account." It closes by restating the boundary (see T10).

**That is the reusable structure: acknowledge → name the humans → name the protocol → give the reporting handle → restate the limit.** Five moves, ~120 words, on a page about a medical AI getting something wrong.

**Other failure-shaped titles** `[observed]`:
- `I received an email that seems to be Phishing, what can I do?` — first-person, comma splice and all, covering a threat Ada does not control
- `What happens if my phone is lost or stolen?` — device-loss consequences for health data
- `I need help with the Ada App` — a catch-all
- `I'm trying to create an account but haven't received a verification email. What should I do?` is Clue's equivalent; Ada's nearest is the verification FAQ

**Negative finding.** `[absent]` There is no article addressing the emotionally hardest failure: what to do if the assessment result frightens you, or if it suggests a serious condition. Ada documents *system* failure (missed something, phishing, lost phone) thoroughly and documents **user distress not at all**. For the highest-stakes content problem in the corpus, the missing article is the one titled something like "Ada suggested something serious and I'm scared". No crisis signposting, no mental-health routing, no emergency-services guidance was found on any consumer help page inspected — the emergency instruction exists only inside the all-caps T&Cs block (T10), which is the least-read surface on the site.

## T10 Disclosures, legal & compliance — medical disclaimer, data use, consent

**Priority section. The strongest material in this file.**

### The boundary statement, and where it appears

Ada restates "this is not a diagnosis" in **four distinct registers on four different surfaces**. The repetition is the pattern.

**1. Legal register — T&Cs §1.3, in full capitals, as a standalone block** `[observed]`:

> "PLEASE NOTE THAT NEITHER ADA ASSESS, NOR OUR WEBSITE MAKE ANY MEDICAL DIAGNOSES. PLEASE SEEK THE ADVICE OF A MEDICAL PROFESSIONAL IF YOU ARE CONCERNED ABOUT YOUR HEALTH. IN CASE OF AN EMERGENCY, YOU SHOULD CONTACT THE EMERGENCY SERVICES IMMEDIATELY. ALWAYS CONSULT YOUR DOCTOR OR ANOTHER QUALIFIED HEALTH CARE PROVIDER IF YOU HAVE ANY QUESTIONS REGARDING ANY MEDICAL ISSUES. YOU SHOULD NEVER DISREGARD A DOCTOR'S PROFESSIONAL ADVICE OR CANCEL AN APPOINTMENT WITH A DOCTOR BECAUSE YOU ARE RELYING ON INFORMATION YOU HAVE READ OR HEARD ON OUR WEBSITE OR VIA ADA ASSESS."

Five sentences, escalating: no diagnoses → seek advice → **emergency services** → always consult → **never cancel an appointment**. That final clause is the one most products omit. Ada anticipates the specific harmful behaviour — a user reading a reassuring result and cancelling a real appointment — and prohibits it by name. Placed in §1, before the legal relationship is even established, not in a limitations section at the back.

**2. Support register — the help answer, opening with a bare "No."** `[observed]`:

> "No. Ada's here to help you navigate your health journey. Still, it's not intended to replace your doctor or the advice of a qualified healthcare practitioner and cannot provide a diagnosis."

The answer's first word is `No.` — one syllable, full stop, before any softening. Then the reassurance, then `Still,` as the pivot, then the limit restated with two verbs: `not intended to replace` and `cannot provide`. The second is absolute (capability), the first is conditional (intent). Both are needed and they do different work.

**3. Recovery register — inside the "Ada missed something" article** `[observed]`:

> "Remember, Ada's information is not a diagnosis and shouldn't replace professional medical advice. It offers possible explanations for symptoms and suggests care options, but it's not a substitute for a doctor's diagnosis."

`Remember,` — the disclaimer is re-served at the moment the user is most likely to have over-trusted the output. Placement is the whole point.

**4. Contractual register — T&Cs §4.3–4.5** `[observed]`, sentence case, escalating specificity:
- §4.3: "all information … are solely for information purposes. No information that is presented … constitutes a medical consultation or is intended to replace a medical consultation."
- §4.4: "does NOT provide diagnoses for medical conditions, nor do they prescribe how you are to treat any medical issues you may have. This can only be done by consulting a medical professional (such as a general practitioner)." — note `NOT` capitalised mid-sentence for emphasis, and the parenthetical concretising "medical professional" into "(such as a general practitioner)".
- §4.5: "you are responsible for whether or not you decide to consult a medical professional and/or seek medical advice."
- §9.2: "You should not take any action based on the information provided … without consulting a doctor or other medical professional first."

**The pattern to steal: the same boundary restated in four registers, each placed where its register's reader actually is.** Legal text for the contract, a one-word "No." for the searcher, "Remember," for the disappointed user, and a benefit-framed version (`Your data. Your decisions.`) for the browser.

**Weakness recorded honestly.** The boundary is *absent* from the hero and from the medical-library articles. `ada.com/conditions/endometriosis/` — a page that describes diagnosis, staging, and treatment options including drugs and surgery — carries **no medical disclaimer at all**. Its author line is `Written by Ada's Medical Knowledge Team`, it has five numbered citations to NHS/WHO/Endometriosis UK, and an `Updated on October 24, 2024 at 1:00 PM UTC` timestamp precise to the minute — but no "this is not medical advice" line anywhere on the page. The disclaimer discipline that is exemplary in the app and the T&Cs does not reach the editorial surface. This is the clearest gap in an otherwise strong disclosure system.

### Testimonial disclaimer — a small, excellent pattern

Under **every** review quote on /app/ `[observed]`:

> "These are unaltered publicly available third-party statements and not claims from Ada."

Twelve words doing three jobs: `unaltered` (no cherry-picking of sentences), `publicly available` (provenance), `not claims from Ada` (regulatory separation). On a medical device, a user saying "it got my rare disease right" is a performance claim Ada cannot make — so Ada quotes it and disowns it in the same breath. Repeated under all four testimonials rather than once at the block foot.

Worth noting the testimonials Ada chose: one includes the user's own caveat — "Don't expect it to replace professional medical diagnosis tho! This app basically guides me to realize when it's time to see a doctor." Ada surfaces a review that **states the product's limitation in the user's voice**. Selecting social proof that does the disclaimer's job is a sophisticated move.

### Data use and consent

**Third-party sharing, stated with an unusually clear distinction** `[observed]`:

> "Ada does not share your personal data with third parties without your explicit consent. Under no circumstances do we sell any data provided to us."

Then the part most products skip — separating *sharing* from *processing*:

> "Transferring data to our service providers is very different from transferring data to third parties for their purposes. When data is transferred (shared), service providers only have access to your data under the control of Ada. All service providers are bound by a data protection agreement and will only process data according to our instructions."

A **public list of service providers** is linked (`ada.com/service-providers/`). Named-subprocessor transparency on a consumer health app.

**Consent framed as revocable and non-obligatory** `[observed]`: "You are never obligated to provide consent for data sharing, and if you provide such consent, you can revoke it at any time." Followed by a four-step path to the control. The rights statement and the mechanism sit in the same answer — a pattern most privacy copy separates across pages.

**Contextual consent** `[observed]`: "When appropriate, we'll ask you whether you would like to share your data with our partners when we believe this would benefit you, for example, to show you available care options based on your reported symptoms." Just-in-time consent with the benefit named concretely, not a blanket onboarding checkbox.

**Anonymised-data licence, disclosed in the T&Cs** `[observed]`, §3.2: "You hereby grant us the perpetual, worldwide, transferable, and sublicensable right to use data, which we have anonymized in line with applicable data protection law, in any manner, whether currently known or unknown. You acknowledge and accept that we own all right, title and interest in and to any data derived by us from such anonymized data." `perpetual`, `sublicensable`, `in any manner, whether currently known or unknown`, and Ada **owns** the derived data. This is far broader than the consumer-facing privacy FAQ implies, and it is not summarised anywhere in the help centre. Recorded as an observed asymmetry between the two registers — not as an accusation.

**Other named disclosure topics** `[observed]`: `What are my GDPR privacy rights?` · `How is my data stored by Ada?` · `Are messages to Ada secure?` · `What precautions does Ada take to protect users' data?` · `Why do I need to create an account to conduct an assessment?`

**Age gate** `[observed]`, T&Cs §1.5: "You must be at least 13 years old … If you are under 18 years old, usage … requires the consent of your legal guardian." Two thresholds, stated in one sentence.

## T11 Help-centre architecture

Two levels: five topic cards → flat article lists. Small — roughly 30 articles total, against Wise's ~90 in one topic alone.

**Featured FAQs on the help home — six, in this order** `[observed]`:

1. `Can Ada replace my doctor?`
2. `How do I edit my health profile in Ada?`
3. `How do I export my assessment?`
4. `How do I verify my account via email?`
5. `Is my personal data shared with third parties?`
6. `You don't have any ads in the app. How do you make money?`

The ordering is the artefact. **Position 1 is the boundary question, not a task.** Positions 2–4 are mechanics. Positions 5–6 are trust. Ada leads its help centre with the question a nervous user is most likely to be asking themselves, and it is a question whose honest answer undermines the product's apparent promise. Answering it first, and answering it `No.`, is the strongest single IA decision in this file.

Q6's construction is also notable: a **statement plus a question** (`You don't have any ads in the app. How do you make money?`). The FAQ voices the user's observation before voicing their suspicion.

**Article-title grammar — four shapes** `[observed]`:

| Shape | Example |
|---|---|
| `Can Ada …?` | `Can Ada replace my doctor?` · `Can Ada give me a medical diagnosis?` · `Can Ada help my pets?` |
| `How do I …?` | `How do I export my assessment?` · `How do I suggest a symptom?` |
| `What …?` | `What can I do if Ada missed something?` · `What are my GDPR privacy rights?` · `What happens if my phone is lost or stolen?` |
| `I <state>` | `I need help with the Ada App` · `I received an email that seems to be Phishing, what can I do?` |

The `Can Ada …?` family is distinctive: **six of ~30 articles are capability-boundary questions.** Ada spends a fifth of its help centre on what it cannot do. Compare Wise, whose equivalent family is about the user's mistakes. Ada's is about the product's limits.

`Can Ada help my pets?` deserves a line. It is a real article, sitting between two medical-boundary articles. Someone asked, enough times to warrant content. It is also — quietly — a boundary article: it establishes that the scope is human medicine.

**Routing furniture** `[observed]`: `How can we help you?` → featured FAQs → `Helpful topics` (five icon cards) → `Still need help?` → `Contact support`. Every article ends with `Have more questions?` + `Submit a request`. Consistent, and human contact is last on both the hub and every leaf.

**Content-ops defect recorded** `[observed]`: `Can Ada replace my doctor?` and `Can Ada give me a medical diagnosis? / Can Ada diagnose medical conditions?` have **byte-identical body copy** — the same three paragraphs beginning "No. Ada's here to help you navigate your health journey." Two distinct questions, one answer, duplicated rather than cross-linked. The second title also contains a slash-separated double title (`Can Ada give me a medical diagnosis? / Can Ada diagnose medical conditions?`), which is two SEO variants fused into one user-facing label. Both are visible seams in the help-content pipeline on the most important answer Ada publishes.

## T12 FAQs

**Placement:** help-centre home (six, above), plus a per-article FAQ block at the foot of Medical Library entries.

**Medical Library FAQ pattern** `[observed]` — from the endometriosis article, `Endometriosis FAQs`, formatted `**Q:**` / `A:`:

| # | Question (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| 1 | Can you get pregnant with endometriosis? |
| 2 | Does endometriosis cause infertility? |
| 3 | Is endometriosis genetic? |
| 4 | Does endometriosis cause weight gain? |
| 5 | What is the main cause of endometriosis? |

Structural notes. Five questions, and **three of five are about fertility** — Q1 and Q2 are near-duplicates asked from opposite directions (can I / does it stop me). That duplication is deliberate: they are two different search queries and two different emotional states, and Ada answers both rather than merging them.

Q4 (`Does endometriosis cause weight gain?`) is the body-image question and its answer is a model of careful negation: "Endometriosis doesn't cause weight gain directly, although treatments for endometriosis can contribute to weight gain. Endometriosis also causes bloating, which can be mistaken for weight gain as well." Direct answer → the real mechanism → the perceptual confusion, in three clauses. It does not dismiss the observation; it relocates it.

Every answer is one to three sentences and every one hedges (`It's possible`, `Although the exact cause … remains uncertain`, `it's possible that there may be`, `is thought to be related to`). Note `it's possible that there may be a genetic factor` — a double hedge, which is over-hedged prose but under-claimed medicine.

**Three register splits worth flagging:**

1. **`symptom assessment` (home, T&Cs, CTA) vs `symptom checker` (/app/, titles, meta).** Not a slip — `assessment` is the regulated, careful noun and `checker` is the discoverable, searched one. The careful word is used where Ada is making a promise; the common word where Ada is being found. Defensible, but it means the product has two names.
2. **`findings` vs `symptoms`.** Clinical noun in the contract, lay noun in the product. The T&Cs sentence quoted in T5 uses both (`health information relevant to medical findings`) while every consumer surface says `symptoms`.
3. **Two device classes for two products.** `Ada Assess` = Class IIa under MDR (2017/745). `Ada Health App` = Class I under the older MDD (93/42/EEC). A user cannot tell from the consumer site which one they are using. The T&Cs page even carries a self-referential loop `[observed]`: §2.3 says "For the applicable terms and conditions for other Ada products, such as the Ada Health App please see Ada Health App Terms and Conditions" and links **back to the same page**. A broken cross-reference on the one paragraph that distinguishes two differently-regulated products.

## T14 Voice, tone & accessibility

**Person.** The product speaks in **first-person singular** (`I'm Ada`) exactly once, in the hero, and never again. Everywhere else the company speaks as `we` — "We're always working", "we have strict prompt review and correction protocols", "Our team of doctors". The user is `you` throughout. The one `I` is doing enormous work: it establishes a conversational partner before the user enters a branching interrogation, and it is then quietly retired.

**Register gradient — four clearly separated tiers** `[observed]`:

| Tier | Example | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Marketing | "Good information leads to better health choices." | Warm, short, aphoristic |
| Support | "No. Ada's here to help you navigate your health journey." | Contractions, direct, reassuring-then-limiting |
| Editorial | "pain in the lower part of your belly or your back" | Lay-translated clinical, second person |
| Legal | ALL-CAPS BLOCK / "NO WARRANTIES. USE … IS AT YOUR OWN RISK." | Maximal formality, capitals as emphasis |

The gradient is steeper than Wise's because the stakes are higher at the bottom. Contractions (`Ada's here`, `it's not`, `shouldn't`, `We're`) appear freely in support and marketing and **vanish entirely** in the T&Cs.

**Lay translation in editorial is the transferable craft.** `[observed]` From the endometriosis article:
- `tissue that resembles the lining of the uterus grows in other places, outside of the uterus` — rather than "ectopic endometrial-like tissue"
- `pain in the lower part of your belly or your back` — `belly`, not "abdomen"
- `period blood containing cells of the inner lining of the uterus flows back into the pelvic cavity` — then, and only then, the term: "This is called **retrograde menstruation**"
- `a procedure in which a small camera is inserted into the lower belly` — then "**laparoscopy**"

**The pattern: describe the mechanism in ordinary words first, then attach the clinical term as a label.** Never the reverse. `metaplasia`, `retrograde menstruation` and `laparoscopy` are all introduced this way. Where a term is unavoidable up front (`estrogen`, `endometriosis`) it is bolded rather than glossed.

**Bolding is used as a comprehension aid, heavily.** Key phrases within paragraphs are bolded so the article is skimmable at symptom level — `**Heavy bleeding during or between periods**`, `**Severe pain during your period**`. A frightened reader scanning for their own symptom can find it without reading prose.

**Hedging vocabulary, consistently applied** `[observed]`: `can vary`, `it's possible`, `may contribute`, `is thought to be`, `usually`, `commonly`, `in some cases`, `although further research is still needed`, `not fully understood`. Ada's editorial will say **"Endometriosis's causes are not fully understood"** in plain terms rather than papering over the uncertainty.

**Authorship and currency** `[observed]`, the trust template on every Medical Library article:
- `Written by Ada's Medical Knowledge Team` (linked to `/team/medical/`)
- `Updated on October 24, 2024 at 1:00 PM UTC` — **precise to the minute**
- Numbered inline footnote markers throughout body text, resolving to a citation list with source, year, title, link and `Accessed October 1, 2024`

Note the date discipline: the article is `Updated on October 24, 2024` but the sources are `Accessed October 1, 2024`. Two different dates, both shown, three weeks apart. That is honest — it tells the reader when the evidence was last checked *and* when the prose was last touched, and does not conflate them. **A minute-precision update timestamp plus per-source access dates is the strongest currency-signalling pattern in this batch.**

**Attribution contrast within the batch:** Ada attributes to a *team*, not a named individual. Flo and Clue both attribute to a named reviewer with post-nominals (`Lynae Brayboy, MD, FACOG`; `Sarah Toler, DNP, CNM`). Ada instead publishes team-level credentials elsewhere — "doctors from over 20 countries and various specialties", "50 in-house medical experts". **Institutional authority vs personal authority.** Ada's scales better and is less findable; Clue's is warmer and individually accountable. Worth naming as a deliberate fork rather than one being right.

**Accessibility content** `[observed]`
- `Skip navigation block` is first in DOM on every page. Note the label — `Skip navigation block`, not the conventional `Skip to content`. Names what is being skipped rather than the destination; arguably clearer, certainly non-standard.
- Alt text is mixed. Good, scene-level: `woman checking Ada medical library`, `A cyclist checking the Ada symptom checker app`, `a person using start Ada assessment on a phone`. Weak or absent: several `![]()` empty-alt images that appear to carry meaning; icon images with filename-derived alt (`Icon accurancy` — with the **typo present in the alt text itself**, `icon-n700-contribution-lines.svg`); and the two certification badges use `ISO 27001` / `EN ISO 13485` as alt, which is correct.
- Consent is a standing footer control (`Consent Settings`), not a one-shot banner — revocable by design, matching the stated policy.
- **`/symptom-assessment-ai/` renders essentially empty without JavaScript.** The regulated device has no non-JS fallback and no noscript message. Recorded as an observed accessibility and resilience defect.
- `Icon accurancy` (sic) as alt text on a page selling medical accuracy is a small but pointed QA finding.

**Negative findings, recorded honestly**
- Two names for one product (`symptom assessment` / `symptom checker`).
- Two device classifications, indistinguishable to the user, with a broken cross-reference between them.
- Identical body copy on the two most important help articles.
- No medical disclaimer on Medical Library articles despite treatment and staging content.
- No crisis, distress or emergency signposting on any consumer help surface; emergency instruction exists only in the all-caps T&Cs block.
- `Icon accurancy` typo in alt text.
- Mixed capitalisation in help topic labels.

## Transferable patterns

1. **Lead the help centre with the boundary question, and answer it "No."** `Can Ada replace my doctor?` in position 1, answered with a one-word sentence before any reassurance. For any product whose users may over-trust it — AI assistants, fraud scoring, eligibility checkers, BNPL affordability tools — putting the disempowering answer first is the trust-building move, not the trust-destroying one.
2. **Restate the same limit in four registers, each placed where that register's reader is.** All-caps in the contract; `No.` for the searcher; `Remember,` for the disappointed user; `Your data. Your decisions.` for the browser. Condition: the four versions must say the *same thing*, or the repetition reads as inconsistency rather than reinforcement.
3. **Prohibit the specific harmful behaviour by name.** "YOU SHOULD NEVER … CANCEL AN APPOINTMENT WITH A DOCTOR BECAUSE YOU ARE RELYING ON INFORMATION…". Generic "not a substitute for professional advice" does not stop the action; naming the action might. Transfers to any product where a reassuring output could cause a user to skip a real safeguard.
4. **Describe the mechanism in ordinary words, then attach the term.** `period blood … flows back into the pelvic cavity. This is called retrograde menstruation.` Never term-first. Applies to every jargon-heavy domain, finance included — describe what happens, then name it.
5. **Disown your own social proof.** "These are unaltered publicly available third-party statements and not claims from Ada", repeated under every quote. Where a user's claim would be non-compliant if you made it, quote it and separate yourself in the same sentence. Directly relevant to regulated-product testimonials.
6. **Publish a graded advice ladder rather than a binary.** `8 advice levels` means eight authored urgency copy-sets with eight clinical thresholds. Binary "act / don't act" outputs force every borderline case into the wrong bucket. Applies to risk scoring, fraud alerts and affordability warnings.
7. **Dual-date your content: updated-on to the minute, plus per-source access dates.** Shows evidence currency and prose currency separately, and refuses to let a light copy-edit imply a fresh evidence review.
8. **Ship a "we got it wrong" article with a reporting handle.** `What can I do if Ada missed something?` → acknowledge, name the humans, name the protocol, give the handle (`Provide your User ID`), restate the limit. Most products have no route for "your output was wrong" at all.

## Caveats & gaps

- **The assessment flow was not started and no health data was entered, by instruction and by judgement.** Every claim in T5 about question phrasing, escalation and result copy is `[documented]` from Ada's published descriptions. **No question string, advice level label, severity wording or result string is reproduced in this file, because none was observed.** The eight advice levels exist and are named as a count; their labels are not public and are not guessed here.
- **This is the file's central limitation and it is unavoidable without entering symptom data.** A future pass would need either an authenticated non-clinical walkthrough with synthetic input (which the brief correctly forbids), Ada's published clinical study appendices, or the enterprise documentation behind `about.ada.com` contact gating.
- `/symptom-assessment-ai/` is client-rendered and returned an empty shell; a browser-rendered fetch would confirm whether any pre-assessment consent or disclaimer interstitial exists before the first question. **That interstitial — if it exists — is the single highest-value missing artefact in this file.**
- Privacy Policy, Cookie Policy and Imprint were not opened. T10 relies on the T&Cs and the privacy help articles; the Policy itself (referenced as "section 3") may contain materially different framing.
- `ada.com/service-providers/` (the named-subprocessor list) was not fetched.
- One Medical Library article of ~600 was read. The template is consistent in its furniture but the FAQ sets and hedging density will vary by condition — particularly for the mental-health and oncology entries, where the safety copy is most likely to differ and where crisis signposting, if it exists anywhere, would most plausibly be found.
- The `Signs of …` family (a distinct content tier at root paths) was identified from the index but not opened.
- No in-app UI observed: field labels, validation, placeholders, progress indication, empty states, toasts and the consent interstitial are all `[absent]`.
- en-locale only. Ada ships seven product languages and ten site locales; the disclaimer wording in German (the manufacturer's home jurisdiction) may be the more authoritative version.
