## T1 Navigation & IA labels

**Primary nav is two words.** `[observed]`: `Learn` · `edX For Business`. Plus a promo banner with `Close site banner.` as the dismiss label.

That is the entire global nav. Everything — seven credential types, three degree levels, the catalogue — is either inside `Learn` (client-rendered, not captured) or reachable only via the footer. This is a notably thin nav for a catalogue of this complexity, and it pushes almost all IA load onto the footer and onto the help centre.

**Homepage explore tabs** `[observed]`: `Explore top courses` → `Artificial Intelligence` · `Computer Science` · `Data Science` · `Business` · `Healthcare`. Five subject tabs; each card carries the institution, the credential type, and (for programs) a course count — e.g. `MicroMasters` `6 courses`, `MicroBachelors` `4 courses`, `Certificate` `1 course`.

**The card label is where the credential taxonomy actually surfaces** `[observed]`. Card type labels seen on one homepage: `Executive Education` · `Certificate` · `Course` · `Masters` · `MicroMasters` · `MicroBachelors` · `Bachelors`. Seven type labels on one screen, with no legend and no ordering by commitment. See T13.

**Footer — seven blocks** `[observed]`

| Block | Contents |
|---|---|
| `Skills` | 10 skill links → `see more Courses` |
| `Professional Certificates` | `Harvard University's CS50` · `MicroMasters Programs` · `MicroBachelors Programs` · `XSeries Programs` · `Certificates in AI` · `Certificates in Project Management` · `Certificates in Data Analytics` · `Certificates in IT` → `see more Certificates` |
| `Online Degrees` | 8 degree links → three separate "see more" links: `see more Undergraduate Degrees` · `see more Graduate Degrees` · `see more Doctorate Degrees` |
| `Helpful Resources` | `Benefits of a Verified Certificate` · `How to Get Financial Assistance` · `How to Audit a Course` · five `Become a/an <role>` articles → `see more Resources` |
| `edx` | `Partner With Us` · `About` · `edX For Business` · `Affiliates` · `Open edX` · `2U Advisory Council` · `Careers` · `News` |
| `Connect` | `Idea Hub` · `Contact Us` · `Help Center` · `Security` · `Media Kit` |
| `Legal` | `Terms of Service & Honor Code` · `Privacy Policy` · `Cookie Policy` · `Accessibility Policy` · `Trademark Policy` · `Modern Slavery Statement` · `Sitemap` · `Your Privacy Choices` |

**Two defects in the footer.**

**One, a taxonomy error.** The block headed `Professional Certificates` contains `MicroMasters Programs`, `MicroBachelors Programs` and `XSeries Programs` — **none of which is a Professional Certificate.** edX's own help centre gives each of those four its own definitional article as a distinct program type (T11). The footer heading is the platform mislabelling its own credential classes on every page.

**Two, inconsistent footer variants across pages.** On the homepage and course pages the degree list reads `Bachelor's in Psychology` and `Master's in Statistics`; on the MicroBachelors hub the *same* links read just `Psychology` and `Statistics`, and that variant also adds `Certificates in Management`, `Certificates in Python`, `Professional Certificate Programs`, `Career Resource Network` and `Earn University Credit`. Also `see more Courses` on one page vs `see more Topics` on another, for the same destination. A shared footer with unsynchronised content models.

**The `Helpful Resources` block is doing real IA work**, and is the best thing in the footer. `Benefits of a Verified Certificate`, `How to Get Financial Assistance`, `How to Audit a Course` — three pages named after the three decisions a learner has to make before paying (*is the paid tier worth it / can I get help paying / can I not pay at all*), promoted to a persistent footer slot. Most platforms bury all three.

**Help-centre top level — eight categories, single nouns, no scope lines** `[observed]`

`Account Basics` · `Courses` · `Payments & Refunds` · `Certificates` · `Programs` · `Credit` · `Mobile` · `FAQ`

Compared to Wise's gerund-plus-scope-sentence pattern, this is object-model IA: these are the system's nouns, not the learner's activities, and none carries a description. `Credit` as a top-level peer of `Certificates` is the notable one — academic credit is treated as a *separate concern* from credentials, which is correct (they have different partners, different processes, different money) and which most platforms collapse. `FAQ` as an eighth sibling category alongside seven topical ones is the weak slot — a catch-all that duplicates the others.

**Help-centre section names (level 2) are gerund or noun phrases and much closer to the learner's task** `[observed]`. From `Courses`: `Enrolling in edX Courses or Programs` · `About Dates and Schedules` · `About Audit and Verified Courses` · `Accessing Course Content` · `Completing Assignments` · `Completing Essay Assignments` · `Taking Timed and Proctored Exams` · `Checking Your Course Progress` · `Participating in Course Discussions`.

Nine sections, seven of them verb-led gerunds. The `About X` construction is used for the two sections that are *conceptual* rather than procedural (`About Dates and Schedules`, `About Audit and Verified Courses`), which is a consistent and useful distinction between "how do I" and "what is".

## T2 Value proposition & headline patterns

**Homepage has no single hero headline.** `[observed]` The most hero-like block is:

> `Learn something new with edX`
> "edX helps learners around the world grow their skills and careers. Join our learning community today!"

Generic, and followed immediately by four numbers, which is where the actual positioning lives.

**Four-stat block** `[observed]`

| Figure | Claim |
|---|---|
| `100M` | `global learners, in nearly every industry, are upskilling with edX.` |
| `84%` | `of edX learners have seen professional growth after earning a certificate.` |
| `140K` | `professionals have had their lives changed through Executive Education.` |
| `42K` | `edX learners have already found their degree program this year.` |

**None of the four carries a footnote, source or date.** Compare Coursera, which footnotes every equivalent figure with a named report and a date range. Two further problems: `84%` is conditioned on *having earned a certificate* (a self-selected population) with no acknowledgement, and `have had their lives changed` is unfalsifiable copy sitting in a numeric-claim slot.

**A cross-page numeric contradiction** `[observed]`: the homepage says `100M` `global learners`; the verified-certificate page says edX "has connected over 86 million people across the globe with high-quality online education." A 14-million discrepancy between two live pages, neither dated.

**Section headers on the homepage are community- and access-framed rather than outcome-framed** `[observed]`:
`Discover more than a classroom — join a global community.` · `Empower every member of your team` · `Popular topics` · `Hear what other learners have to say` · `Register for a free account`

Four supporting lines under the community header, and they are the clearest statement of edX's actual proposition:
- "Access world-class education from institutions like Harvard, MIT, and Cambridge."
- "Learn from anywhere in the world with online courses, certificates, and degrees."
- "Connect with edX's global network of ambitious learners."
- "Find a range of programs covering diverse topics for all types of students."
- "Engage with expert-led curricula via flexible, hybrid, and 100% online options."

**The institutional-prestige lever is edX's differentiator and it is used consistently**: `Harvard`, `MIT`, `Cambridge`, `founded by Harvard and MIT`, `Ivy League institutions like Harvard`, `prestigious STEM-focused schools like MIT`, `edX courses are a badge of honor`. Where Coursera leads on employer recognition, edX leads on university provenance. Same category, opposite trust anchor.

**Credential hub pages use a definitional H2 as the first section** `[observed]`:
`What is a MicroBachelors® Program?` · `What is a verified certificate?` · `Why enroll in a MicroBachelors® Program?` · `Why purchase a verified certificate` · `How MicroBachelors® programs compare to traditional college courses` · `How to get credit for your MicroBachelors® Program` · `How to get started with a MicroBachelors® Program`

**Definition → motivation → comparison → mechanism → next step.** This is the house structure for every credential page, and it is the right one for a product whose primary comprehension risk is *what is this thing*. Note that the comparison section compares to the **off-platform alternative** (traditional college courses), not to sibling edX products — which is where the confusion actually is.

**`Key takeaways` as a section** `[observed]` on the verified-certificate page — three bullets at the top summarising the page before the page argues it. An explainer-article convention imported into a commercial landing page.

## T4 Onboarding & getting-started

`[observed]` — edX's pre-auth onboarding is **explanatory rather than interactive.** There is no intent selector, no goal picker, no path table. Instead there are three numbered-step blocks and a set of comparison tables.

**The three-step getting-started list** on the MicroBachelors hub is unusually honest about what it is asking:

1. `Consider your career goals and educational needs.`
2. `Determine what kind of program can help you achieve those goals.`
3. `Explore and enroll in a MicroBachelors Program.`

Steps 1 and 2 are **entirely the learner's own cognitive work**, with no tool offered to do it. It reads as a decision checklist rather than a product flow, and it sets up step 3 as the only action. Compare Coursera's `What brings you to Coursera today?` / `Choose your path`, which performs step 2 *for* the learner. edX names the work; Coursera does the work. Naming it is more candid and less useful.

**The comparison table is edX's real onboarding device**, and it appears in two forms.

**Form A — vs the off-platform alternative** `[observed]`, MicroBachelors hub, five rows:

| | `MicroBachelors® Programs` | `Traditional college courses` |
|---|---|---|
| `Course delivery format` | `Online` | `Often in-person` |
| `Admissions process` | `No application required` | `Admissions process typically required` |
| `Schedule` | `Flexible schedule` | `Fixed schedule` |
| `Cost` | `Pay by course` | `Higher tuition costs` |
| `College credit` | `Earn college credit` | `Earn college credit` |

The last row is the point of the whole table: **the two columns are identical on the row that matters.** Putting the parity row last, after four rows of differentiation, is a deliberate rhetorical sequence — differentiate on convenience, then equalise on the thing the learner actually fears losing. Note also `Higher tuition costs` is the only vague cell in the table, and it's the competitor's price.

**Form B — vs the free tier on the same platform** `[observed]`, verified-certificate page, four rows:

| | `Verified certificate` | `Free course` |
|---|---|---|
| `Graded assignments` | (yes) | (no) |
| `Access to content` | `Unlimited` | `Limited` |
| `Career value` | `Recognized credential to share with employers.` | `Explore course content before committing.` |
| `Certificate of completion` | (yes) | (no) |

`Career value` for the free tier is written as `Explore course content before committing.` — **the free tier's stated benefit is that it is a reversible decision.** That is a genuinely good piece of copy: it gives the non-paying learner a legitimate purpose rather than framing the free tier as a deficient version of the paid one.

**Form C — the definitive version, in the help centre** `[documented]`, seven rows, and the best of the three because it lists the *shared* rows too:

| `What's included?` | `Verified Track` | `Audit Track` |
|---|---|---|
| `Verified Certificates` | yes | no |
| `Graded Assessments and Exams` | yes | no |
| `Discussion forums` | yes | **yes** |
| `Videos` | yes | **yes** |
| `Readings` | yes | **yes** |
| `Practice (Ungraded) Assessments` | yes | **yes** |
| `Access to videos/readings after the course end date` | yes | no |

Four of seven rows are identical. A paywall comparison that shows the free tier winning (or tying) on the majority of rows is unusual and builds credibility for the three rows where it loses. The `Practice (Ungraded) Assessments` row is doing precise work: the parenthetical distinguishes it from the paid `Graded Assessments`, so the reader can see that the difference is *grading*, not *practice*.

**Course-level commitment badges: `[absent]` — not observable.** The course XDP body is client-rendered. edX's per-course `weeks` / `hours per week` / `Self-paced` / price badges could not be captured. What *is* documented is that the audit-track expiry is keyed to a figure called the **`estimated course length`**, which is "posted on the course introduction page in the catalog" — so the commitment figure exists and is load-bearing (it determines when free access dies), but was not directly observed.

**Program-level commitment figures, where stated in prose** `[observed]`:
- MicroBachelors: `A typical 6-credit program takes about 2-4 months to complete, though timelines vary by program.`
- Single course: "You can complete an individual course through edX for as little as $50."
- Professional Certificates: "which involve a course series and take 2-10 months to complete, start at around $500."
- Executive Education: "start at around $2,500 and take 6-20 weeks or more to complete."

Note the units: `2-4 months`, `2-10 months`, `6-20 weeks or more`. Three ranges, two unit systems, and `or more` appended to the most expensive one. Compared to Coursera's normalised `X months at 10 hours a week`, edX gives wall-clock ranges with no weekly rate — so the learner cannot tell whether `2-10 months` assumes two hours a week or twenty.

## T5 Form & field labels

Sparse pre-auth. `[observed]`

**Help-centre search block:**
> Heading: `Search for your answer below`
> Lede: `If you need further assistance you may submit a request to our support team on the article or search pages.`
> Then: `Popular search topics:` followed by three links

Two things. The heading is an **imperative that describes where the answer is** (`below`) rather than naming the field — a heading doing a label's job. And the lede pre-empts search failure *before the user has searched*, telling them the escalation route up front. That is the Wise `Trouble logging in?` move applied to search: name the fallback at the point of first attempt.

`Popular search topics:` then seeds three queries — `Apply for financial assistance`, `Get course help`, `Login issues` — phrased as **verb-object task names**, not as the article titles they link to. So the seeded query language is deliberately different from the destination title language (see T11 defect note).

**Identity-verification field labels** `[documented]`, inferable from article titles only: ID photo upload, webcam capture, account name matching. Actual labels not observed. `[absent]`

**Payment form** `[documented]`: one field is named in an article title — `What is the Security Code on the payment form?` So the field is labelled `Security Code`, and it generates enough confusion to warrant a dedicated article.

## T6 Status & state language — **unusually rich, and the strongest T6 in this batch**

edX has a genuinely large state vocabulary for courses, and the help IA exposes all of it. `[documented]` unless marked.

**Course lifecycle states**, each with its own article:
- **start / end** — `When does my course start and end?`, `When does the course start?`, `How long do I have to finish? When does the course end?`
- **`self-paced`** — `How do self-paced courses work?`
- **`archived`** — `What does archived mean?`. Consequence stated flatly in another article: "archived courses do not award certificates"
- **`expires`** — `How can I get access after a course expires?`, `How long can I access a course in the audit track before it expires?`
- **`session`** — `Error when selecting session`, `Select Session` button, `choose any available session of the course`, `How do I complete a course when multiple sessions are available?`

**Enrolment / track states:**
- `audit track` (free) vs `verified track` (paid) — with `How do I know if I'm enrolled in the verified track?` as a dedicated article, i.e. **your own track is not self-evident from the UI**
- `upgrade` as a state transition with its own section (`Upgrade to the Verified Track`) and its own deadline (`What is the deadline to upgrade to a verified certificate?`)
- `Why is there no option to upgrade?` — the *absence* of the transition, documented

**Progress and grading states:**
- `passed` — `How do I know if I passed?`
- `completed` — `How can my course be marked as completed?`
- `Due` — `Why does this assignment I completed show as Due?`
- `Course Completion` percentage — `Why doesn't Course Completion show 100%?`
- the **`green progress checkmark`** — `Why doesn't the green progress checkmark appear?`

**Those last three are the outstanding finding in this file.** edX ships three separate help articles for *the gap between the system's rendered state and the learner's lived reality*:

> `Why does this assignment I completed show as Due?`
> `Why doesn't Course Completion show 100%?`
> `Why doesn't the green progress checkmark appear?`

This is exactly the Wise `Why does it say my transfer's complete when the money hasn't arrived yet?` pattern, and edX has it three times over. Note especially that the third article is named after a **visual affordance** (`the green progress checkmark`) rather than a data state — edX has written a help article for a *colour*, because that is what the learner will search for. Naming the UI element by its appearance, not its function, is a findability decision most content teams resist and should not.

**Audit-expiry mechanics, stated precisely** `[documented]`:
- "The audit access counts from the date and time that you first enrolled in the course, or if you enrolled before the course started, it counts from the date and time that the course opened."
- "Once the audit access duration has passed, you'll lose access to the course and you will no longer be able to click **View Course** on your dashboard."

The second sentence describes the state change **in terms of the control that stops working**, not in terms of an internal flag. A learner whose audit period has lapsed does not see an error — they see a button that no longer responds — and edX documents it that way.

**Verification states** `[documented]`: `Why was my ID verification denied?`, `I am having technical difficulties with verification`, `How do I verify my identity after changing the name on my account?`

**Financial-assistance states** `[documented]`: application → decision → wait. `How long do I have to wait for my Financial Assistance decision?` names *waiting* as a state the learner is in, and answers it as a duration question.

## T7 Error, failure & recovery — **strong**

`[documented]` throughout, via help article titles, and the title grammar is consistent enough to read as a system.

**Three title shapes for failure, used consistently:**

| Shape | Examples |
|---|---|
| `Why can't / Why doesn't / Why is …?` — the blocked-action question | `Why can't I access the assignments and exams?` · `Why doesn't the Submit button work?` · `Why is my payment failing?` · `Why don't I see my course in the drop down when I apply for Financial Assistance?` · `Why was my ID verification denied?` · `Why is there no option to upgrade?` · `Why doesn't Course Completion show 100%?` · `Why doesn't the green progress checkmark appear?` |
| `I <have the problem>` — first person, present tense | `I am having technical difficulties with verification` · `I can't make a deadline; can I get an extension?` |
| `What if I …?` — the hypothetical-trouble question | `What if I am having login problems or forgot my password?` · `What happens if I need to stop taking a course?` · `What if I'm experiencing a problem in the mobile app?` |

The `Why doesn't the Submit button work?` title is worth isolating. It names **the specific control**, in the learner's words, with no attempt to diagnose in the title. A learner staring at a dead Submit button will type approximately that sentence. Contrast a conventional title like "Troubleshooting assignment submission".

`I can't make a deadline; can I get an extension?` is the best of the first-person set — it states the failure and the requested remedy in one title, with a semicolon, and it is the question a learner is embarrassed to ask. Placing it in `Completing Assignments` rather than burying it means the platform expects it.

**`What happens if I need to stop taking a course?`** — abandonment framed as a legitimate need (`need to stop`), not as failure or churn. No blame in the framing, and it surfaces as a *related article* on the refund page, which is exactly where someone quitting will be.

**Recovery is routed to a specific control, not to a general channel** `[documented]`. The refund article's escalation is: use `Select Session` → then `unenroll` → refund auto-generates. Only "If there are no sessions available, please contact edX support". So the human channel is gated behind a self-serve attempt, and the self-serve attempt is described as two named button presses.

**A candid negative signal edX publishes about itself** `[observed]`: the refund article's own helpfulness score is `1161 out of 3229 found this helpful` — roughly 36%. The audit-vs-verified article scores `11007 out of 11861` (~93%) and the certificate-types article `2104 out of 2505` (~84%). edX publishes all three raw. **The refund article is the platform's worst-rated major article and it is left visible.** That is both a content finding (refunds are where edX's explanation fails) and a practice worth admiring (the score isn't hidden).

## T9 Notifications & system messages

`[observed]` — pre-auth, limited to the site banner and the cookie/chat notice.

**Site banner** (MicroBachelors hub):
> `Save up to 30% this week only` · `30% off courses/programs` and `20% off executive education`. · `Close site banner.`

Two different discounts in one banner, differentiated by product class, with the percentages in the link text. `this week only` is the urgency device, and unlike Coursera's banner there is no countdown and no contradictory second timer. Rendered **twice** in the DOM (responsive variants), so a screen-reader user may hear it twice.

**Cookie / live-chat coupling notice** `[observed]`, on every help-centre page, and it is the most interesting message on the site:

> `To chat live with an edX Learner Support agent, the use of cookies is required. If you reject these cookies, you can still contact edX Learner Support by sending an email to info@edx.org.`
> `Accept Cookies` · `Reject Cookies`

This is a **consent notice that states the consequence of refusal and supplies the fallback in the same breath.** It does not say "we use cookies to improve your experience"; it says *this specific feature needs them, and here is what you get instead if you say no*. Two-option, no dark pattern, no pre-ticked anything, and the refusal path terminates in a working email address rather than a dead end.

That is a directly reusable pattern for any consent gate on a support channel, and it is the single best piece of consent copy in this batch.

**Transactional and re-engagement email copy** `[documented]` only: `How are edX certificates delivered?`, `Can I get a receipt for my enrollment fee?`. Not quoted. Push and reminder copy: `[absent]`, though `Where can I view my due dates?` and `How does the suggested schedule work?` imply a deadline-reminder system.

## T10 Disclosures, legal & compliance — **PRIORITY-adjacent, and the refund tables are the artefact**

### Refund windows — stated as a two-column eligibility table, twice `[observed]`

**edX Verified Certificate Track:**

| `When is the refund available?` | `When is the refund NOT available?` |
|---|---|
| `Up to 14 days after the Course Start Date` | `6 months after the Payment Date` |
| `Up to 14 days after the Payment Date` | |

**Program Bundles** — "If eligible, you can receive a refund for each course in your program bundle separately."

| `When is the refund available?` | `When is the refund NOT available?` |
|---|---|
| `If you've enrolled in a program course, up to 14 days after your enrollment or 14 days after the Course Start Date` | `6 months after the Payment Date` |
| `If you haven't enrolled in a program course, up to 6 months after your payment` | |

**This is the best-constructed refund disclosure in the batch, for four reasons.**

1. **It has a NOT column.** Most refund policies state only the window and leave the user to infer the exclusion. edX gives ineligibility its own column with its own heading, so the outer bound (`6 months after the Payment Date`) is as visible as the inner one.
2. **It names the two different clocks.** `Course Start Date` and `Payment Date` are capitalised as defined terms, and both windows are given, so a learner who paid before the course opened can work out which applies. The bundle table then adds a third clock (`your enrollment`).
3. **The bundle case splits on a condition the learner can check about themselves** — `If you've enrolled in a program course` vs `If you haven't` — with a materially different window (14 days vs 6 months). Bundle purchasers who haven't started get twelve times longer.
4. **The refund is self-serve and the mechanism is a side effect of a different action**: "you can automatically receive a refund if you unenroll from the course on your dashboard." No form, no request. The user does the thing they wanted to do (leave) and the money follows.

**The weakness**, and it is real: three routes are described on one page — verified track (self-serve via unenrol), program bundles (same, but you may first need to `Select Session`), and `Professional Education` ("you can contact edX support"). Plus `Boot Camps`, which are excluded entirely: "Refunds for Boot Camps are subject to the terms of your enrollment agreement for the applicable Boot Camp program." Four product classes, three mechanisms, one of which is "read your contract". And the page's own helpfulness score is ~36% (T7). The table is excellent; the surrounding routing is not.

Also note the policy hierarchy is disclosed: "You can find our edX refund policy in our Terms of Service" with a deep link to `#refund-policy`. The help article positions itself as the readable summary and points at the binding text — the Wise dual-format disclosure pattern, in miniature.

### Price and duration disclosure by credential tier `[observed]`

From the verified-certificate FAQ, the clearest single price/commitment statement edX publishes:

| Tier | Price | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Individual course | `for as little as $50` | not stated |
| `Professional certificates` | `start at around $500` | `2-10 months` |
| `cohort-based executive education programs` | `start at around $2,500` | `6-20 weeks or more` |
| `MicroBachelors` | `around $166 USD per credit` | `about 2-4 months` for a typical 6-credit program |

`around $166 USD per credit` is the standout pricing construction here. **It prices the credential in the unit the comparison will be made in** — a learner deciding between a MicroBachelors and a community-college class thinks in cost-per-credit, so edX quotes cost-per-credit rather than program price. It then supplies the multiplier separately ("Each completed program awards at least three college transfer credits, while some programs offer more"), so the learner computes their own total. Same philosophy as Coursera's rate-conditional duration, applied to money.

`at least three` is also worth noting — a **floor rather than a range**, which cannot be disappointed.

### Credit and admission expectations — the best expectation-setting copy in this batch `[observed]`

The MicroMasters admission article deserves quoting in full structure because the sequencing is the craft:

> Title: `Does earning a MicroMasters® credential guarantee admission to a university master's program?`
> Sentence 1: `Earning a MicroMasters® credential does not guarantee admission to a full master's program.`
> Sentence 2: `However, if you apply and are accepted into the on-campus or online program that is associated with your MicroMasters® program, the MicroMasters® credential will count toward the degree.`
> Sentence 3: routes the learner to the `Certificate & Credit Pathways` section on the program page "to read specific details about which institutions accept the credential for credit."

**Negative answer first, in the first sentence, with no softening preamble.** No "great question", no "it depends", no benefit restated before the bad news. Then the positive conditional. Then the route to the *specific* answer for the learner's own case. Three sentences, ~60 words, and a learner cannot come away with the wrong belief.

edX writes a whole family of these, and the titles are the point — each one names a hope the platform will not meet:

- `Does earning a MicroMasters® credential guarantee admission to a university master's program?`
- `Can the MicroMasters® credential be transferred to any school for credit?`
- `Will the MicroMasters® credential help me get a job or interview with the company that recognizes the program?`
- `Does enrolling in MicroBachelors mean I'm enrolled in a university?`
- `Will my university accept my edX coursework for credit?`
- `Can I receive college credit or credit hours for my course?`
- `Will my certificate include a grade and/or number of credit hours?`

**`Does enrolling in MicroBachelors mean I'm enrolled in a university?`** is the sharpest. It anticipates a specific, consequential false belief — that buying a university-branded credential makes you a student of that university — and names it as a question. A platform that sells university association writing the article that disclaims university enrolment is the compliance-UX equivalent of Wise leading with "not an FDIC-insured bank".

**Credit transfer is bounded by naming the process and the decider** `[observed]`: "To apply those credits toward a bachelor's degree, you'll need to enroll at a partner institution or another school that accepts the credits and follow its transfer credit process. Because transfer credit policies vary by institution, review your intended institution's transfer credit requirements to understand how your credits may apply toward your degree." Same move as Coursera's `Each university determines…`, phrased as an instruction to the learner rather than a disclaimer.

**Named credit partners** `[documented]`: `ASU`, `Charter Oak` — with articles for `Purchasing academic credit through ASU or Charter Oak`, `How do I transfer the credits that I earn through ASU?` and `How long after a course can I pay for the credit through ASU?`. **Credit is a separately-purchasable item with its own expiry**, which is a materially confusing commercial structure, and edX documents it per partner.

### The certificate-vs-certification distinction `[observed]`

> Q: `Do verified certificates expire?`
> A: `No, verified certificates do not expire. Unlike certifications, which license an individual to practice a profession in a given state or jurisdiction for a limited amount of time, certificates serve as verified and trusted evidence that you completed a course or program.`

A one-sentence gloss distinguishing **certificate** (evidence of completion, permanent) from **certification** (licence to practise, jurisdictional, time-limited). This is a real distinction that almost nobody in the sector draws, it is the source of persistent learner confusion, and edX draws it in a single subordinate clause while answering a different question. Excellent.

### Prerequisites and eligibility, stated as negatives `[observed]`
- `No application required` (MicroBachelors table)
- `Is there an application or prerequisites for MicroBachelors?` (article)
- `Can anyone enroll in a MicroMasters® program? Are there any eligibility requirements?` (article)

## T11 Help-centre architecture — **the best-documented help IA in this batch**

**Structure: 3 levels.** 8 categories → named sections → article lists, with `See all N articles` per section and a per-article `Articles in this section` sidebar. Localised into 6 languages with per-article language switching (and the switcher degrades gracefully — where a translation is missing it points at the locale home rather than 404ing).

**Category → section map** `[observed]`

| Category | Sections |
|---|---|
| `Courses` | `Enrolling in edX Courses or Programs` · `About Dates and Schedules` · `About Audit and Verified Courses` · `Accessing Course Content` · `Completing Assignments` · `Completing Essay Assignments` · `Taking Timed and Proctored Exams` · `Checking Your Course Progress` · `Participating in Course Discussions` |
| `Certificates` | `Certificates` · `Upgrade to the Verified Track` · `About Identity Verification` · `Receive and Share edX Certificates` · `Certificate Payments & Refunds` |
| `Payments & Refunds` | `Financial Assistance` · `Payments` · `Refunds` |
| `Programs` | `General` · `Learner records` · `About MicroBachelors` · `About MicroMasters` · `About XSeries Programs` · `About Professional Certificate Programs` |
| `Credit` | `Enrolling for Academic Credit` · `Earning Credit` · `Purchasing Academic Credit` |
| `Account Basics`, `Mobile`, `FAQ` | not expanded in this harvest |

**Three structural observations.**

**One. The `Programs` category is organised by credential type, one section each** — `About MicroBachelors`, `About MicroMasters`, `About XSeries Programs`, `About Professional Certificate Programs`. This is the **authoritative credential taxonomy**, and it contradicts the marketing footer (T1), which files three of those four under "Professional Certificates". When marketing IA and support IA disagree about what your products are, the support IA is usually right, because it was built by people answering the questions.

**Two. `Credit` is split by verb into three sections** — `Enrolling for Academic Credit` / `Earning Credit` / `Purchasing Academic Credit` — which correctly models that credit is a three-step commercial and academic process, not a property of a certificate. `Purchasing Academic Credit` as a section name is bracingly plain about the fact that credit costs extra money.

**Three. Assessment gets two sections, split by grading mechanism**: `Completing Assignments` (14 articles) and `Completing Essay Assignments` (14 articles), the latter entirely about `open response assessment (ORA)`. Splitting help IA by *how the work is marked* rather than by topic is unusual and correct — peer-and-self-graded essay work has a completely different failure surface from auto-graded problems, and ~14 articles of evidence says so.

**Article-title grammar — five consistent shapes** `[observed]`

| Shape | Example |
|---|---|
| `How do I …?` | `How do I enroll in a course?` · `How do I download my certificate?` |
| `What is / What's …?` | `What is an XSeries program?` · `What's the Course Homepage?` · `What's an open response assessment (ORA)?` |
| `Can I …?` | `Can I get a refund?` · `Can I re-take a course?` · `Can I get a paper copy of my certificate?` · `Can I complete ID verification without a webcam?` |
| `Why …?` / `Why can't / Why doesn't …?` | `Why is my payment failing?` · `Why doesn't the green progress checkmark appear?` |
| Imperative / gerund topic | `Viewing and sharing your program record` · `Submit a file with your ORA response` · `Guide: Get Ready for a Proctored Exam` · `Step by Step Guide: How to Redeem your MicroBachelors Certificate for credit` |

The `Can I …?` family is the distinctive one and it is heavily used — `Can I get a refund?`, `Can I earn a certificate after the end?`, `Can I upgrade to the verified track after the course ends?`, `Can I start for free and pay later?`, `Can I get a transcript?`, `Can I get an IBM Badge?`, `If I fail a course, can I retake it?`. **A `Can I …?` title is a permission question, and using it as the title commits the answer to being a clear yes or no.** For a platform whose whole problem is "what am I allowed to do with this credential", that is the right default title shape.

Two titles carry an explicit format prefix — `Guide: Get Ready for a Proctored Exam` and `Step by Step Guide: How to Redeem your MicroBachelors Certificate for credit` — signalling length and type before the click. Good practice, applied to exactly the two highest-anxiety procedures on the platform.

**Promoted articles (8), and what they reveal** `[observed]`:
`Is there a walk-through of a sample course?` · `What if I am having login problems or forgot my password?` · `When does my course start and end?` · `How do I get help with my course?` · `How do I apply for Financial Assistance?` · `How can I pay?` · `Where can I find my program certificate?` · `What is the deadline to upgrade to a verified certificate?`

Ordered roughly **orientation → access failure → timing → help → money in → money out → credential → deadline**. `Is there a walk-through of a sample course?` in position one is a pre-commitment question — the promoted set is written for someone who has not started, or has just paid and is lost.

**Defect: the same article renders under two different titles.** Article `215167857` appears as `Does edX offer financial assistance? How do I apply?` in the `Popular search topics` seeds and as `How do I apply for Financial Assistance?` in the category listing and promoted list. Article `215298797` appears as `How do I get help with assignments, grading, or course content?` in the seeds and `How do I get help with my course?` everywhere else. In both cases the *seeded* version is longer and more specific, and the *listed* version is shorter — so someone deliberately wrote SEO-length titles for the search seeds and short titles for the IA, and the two are now unsynchronised. Also `Where can I find my program certificate?` and `Where can I find my certificate?` are two adjacent articles distinguished by one word.

**Routing furniture** `[observed]`: search first (`Search for your answer below`) → seeded popular topics → 8 categories → promoted articles → per-article `Was this article helpful?` with a raw count → `Contact Us form` / in-product `Help` button. Human contact is last, and the escalation lede appears at the top of the search page rather than at the bottom (T5).

## T13 Credential & pathway terminology — **PRIORITY**

`[observed]` / `[documented]` as marked. Every term below appears verbatim on a cited page.

### The authoritative structure, from edX's own help centre

> `edX currently offers two types of certificates:`
> - `Certificates for single courses`
> - `Program certificates for programs (series of courses)`

**Two types.** Then: "edX offers program certificates for MicroMasters™, MicroBachelors™, Professional Certificate, and XSeries programs." And a separate article exists for `What's a program? How is a program different from a course?`

A learner who understands "program = series of courses" can place any edX credential.

### The naming problem, evidenced

edX's model is cleaner than Coursera's, but it leaks in four specific ways:

1. **Trademark symbol inconsistency on the core marks.** `MicroMasters™` and `MicroBachelors™` in the certificate-types help article; `MicroMasters®` and `MicroBachelors®` in the Programs help section, the hub page titles, the meta descriptions, and the marketing body copy. edX publishes a `Trademark Policy` as a footer legal page and does not apply its own marks consistently across its own surfaces.
2. **`Certificate` is used at three different scopes** with no modifier: a homepage *card type* (`Certificate` `1 course`), a single-course credential (`Course Certificate`), and a program credential (`Program certificate`). The homepage card label `Certificate` for a one-course item is the most confusing, because it sits adjacent to `MicroMasters` and `MicroBachelors` cards that are also certificates.
3. **`MicroBachelors` and `MicroMasters` are `Micro` + a degree name, and are not degrees.** edX handles this with disclaimer articles (T10) rather than with naming, which is the pragmatic choice given the marks are established — but it means the platform maintains a standing library of articles whose job is to undo its own product names. `Does enrolling in MicroBachelors mean I'm enrolled in a university?` exists because the name implies it does.
4. **The marketing footer files three program types under `Professional Certificates`** (T1), contradicting the help centre's own four-way split.

### The enrollment-vs-audit distinction — edX keeps the word "audit" `[observed]`

Unlike Coursera (which retired "audit" for `Full Course, No Certificate`), edX uses `audit` prominently and glosses it everywhere it appears:

`audit (free)` / `verified (paid)` · `free audit track` / `paid verified track` · `Audit Track` / `Verified Track` · `Auditors` · `How to Audit a Course` (footer resource) · `you can audit a course before purchasing`

The gloss is always parenthetical and always immediate — the canonical article is literally titled `What are the differences between audit (free) and verified (paid) courses?`, teaching both terms and their price in the title. And there are **four separate articles** on the distinction (`What is the audit track?`, `What is the verified track? What does verified mean?`, the comparison article, and `Can I start for free and pay later?`), so the concept is approached from four directions.

**`What does verified mean?`** appended to `What is the verified track?` is the sharp bit. `verified` is edX's most overloaded word — it means *identity-verified* (ID check), *payment-verified* (paid track), and *credential-verified* (trustworthy to an employer), and the platform has a whole `About Identity Verification` section keeping the first sense alive. Asking "what does verified mean?" as a help title is edX acknowledging that its own central term is ambiguous.

`upgrade` / `upgrade deadline` · `estimated course length` · `audit access duration` · `self-paced` · `session` / `Select Session` · `archived` · `expires` · `Certificate & Credit Pathways` · `credit partners` · `transfer credit process` · `program record` · `learner records` · `Program Enrollment page` · `course Enrollment page` · `Course Homepage` · `open response assessment (ORA)` · `proctored exam` / `timed exam` · `Honor Code` · `green progress checkmark` · `Course Completion` · `IBM Badge` · `Credly`-equivalent (not named; edX uses partner badges) · `Enterprise Subscriptions` · `Idea Hub` · `Open edX`

## T14 Voice, tone & accessibility — **the accessibility work here is the best in the batch**

**Person and tense.** Second person to the learner throughout. First-person plural for edX in institutional and policy copy ("we are committed to ensuring", "We prioritize accessibility bug fixes", "We welcome your feedback"), third person in marketing ("edX helps learners around the world"). The help centre is almost entirely second person with named human authors and update dates on every article (`Carol Tong`, `Ned W.`, `Deb Chatigny`, `Dave Dean`, with `September 17, 2026`, `September 21, 2026`, `April 30, 2024`). **Attributed, dated help articles with a visible author photo** is a trust device most help centres have abandoned; it also exposes staleness honestly (the MicroMasters article last touched April 2024).

**Register: plain, flat, low-affect, near-zero colloquialism.** No exclamation marks outside the promo banner and the homepage lede. No `Oops!`, no jokes, no mascot. The only informal word found in the whole harvest is `hassles` ("without extra fees or hassles"), and it is the one overclaiming phrase on that page — which is a neat illustration of why the flat register is load-bearing.

**Tone does not vary much by stakes**, because it starts sober. The exception is the marketing stat block (`have had their lives changed`) and the testimonials, which are the only emotionally-pitched copy on the site.

**Testimonials are quoted with full name and country**, and one is doing specific accessibility work:

> "I'm hard of hearing, which makes it difficult and exhausting to follow regular courses. Thanks to edX lectures have captions, I can follow the courses without any problems."
> — Sandy Van den Borne, Belgium

**A disabled learner's testimonial in the main homepage rotation**, naming the specific accommodation (captions) as the reason the product works. Not on a dedicated accessibility page — in the general social-proof carousel, third of six. That is inclusion as a marketing claim rather than as a compliance artefact, and it is rare. (The quote also contains a grammatical error — "Thanks to edX lectures have captions" — reproduced verbatim on the live page, presumably preserved as the learner wrote it. Defensible, but it reads as unedited.)

### Accessibility policy — substantial, and the strongest in this batch `[observed]`

`https://www.edx.org/accessibility`, titled `Website accessibility policy`, linked from the `Legal` footer block on every page **and from the footer of every help-centre page**. Seven commitments, paraphrased:

1. **Named standard with a named verification method.** Conformance target is `WCAG 2.1 AA`, and verification is specified as "a combination of manual code inspection, automated testing using industry-recognized tools, compatibility testing using the most widely used accessibility support tools and, from time to time, review by an independent website accessibility consultant." Four methods, one of them external.
2. **Bug-priority parity, stated as a principle.** "We prioritize accessibility bug fixes to ensure they are remedied with the same level of priority as any other equivalent loss of function for individuals without disabilities." **This is the single best sentence in the accessibility policies I have seen in this corpus.** It does not promise speed; it promises *equal* treatment, and it defines the comparator precisely ("any other equivalent loss of function"). It is checkable, it survives contact with a real backlog, and it reframes accessibility bugs as ordinary functional bugs rather than a separate class.
3. **Policy distribution obligation.** Links to itself from edx.org and distributes it "to content providers and relevant edX employees and contractors."
4. **A hard content requirement on third parties.** "We require that all course videos include timed captions" — not "encourage", and it extends the WCAG target to course *authoring tools* so partners can meet it. Backed by published `Accessibility Best Practices Guidance for Content Providers`.
5. **A named accountable role.** "Our team includes a website accessibility specialist who is knowledgeable on website accessibility and digital accessibility standards and responsible for coordinating edX's responsibilities with respect to accessibility."
6. **Annual training** for employees and relevant contractors on WCAG 2.1 AA conformance.
7. **A monitored public inbox** — `accessibility@edx.org` — described as "managed by a knowledgeable edX support team", and explicitly covering "requests for accommodation" as well as feedback.

Closing framing: "As with any rapidly advancing technology platform, enhancement of accessibility at edX is an ongoing process." A conformance *aim* rather than a conformance *claim* — honest, and it does not overpromise.

**One real defect: `Last updated: August 11, 2020`.** A six-year-old accessibility policy on a platform that has since changed corporate owner (2U), rebuilt its front end (Next.js), and published a new design language. WCAG 2.2 has been a W3C Recommendation since 2023. The policy's substance is excellent and its currency is not, and the staleness is disclosed by edX's own dateline.

**Also:** the policy is the only public accessibility artefact found. No VPAT, no conformance report, no known-issues list.

### Accessibility in practice, as observable
- **Disability support is routed, and named in an odd place** `[documented]`: `As a student with a disability, who do I contact for help?` — a help article that exists, and sits inside the **`Credit` → `Earning Credit`** section. Almost certainly a mis-filing; a learner with an accessibility need will not look under Credit.
- Alt text on the MicroBachelors hub is descriptive and scene-level: `Smiling businesswoman holding coffee and documents while walking outside a modern office building.` Testimonial headshots are alted with the person's name. Icon images (`post326-favorite.svg`, `post326-done.svg`, `post326-school.svg`) carry **no alt**, and one logo is alted with an empty string — correct for decoration, though the icons sit beside benefit headings and are arguably meaningful.
- Help-centre screenshots carry **filenames as alt text**: `learner_home_course_actionsv2.png`, `audit_access_expired_dashboard.png`, `credit_pathways.png`, `est_length_new.png`, `Screen_Shot_2021-05-13_at_10.57.09_AM.png`. **This is a genuine failure and it matters more here than usual**, because those screenshots carry the information (what the expired-audit dashboard looks like; what a program certificate looks like; where the `Certificate & Credit Pathways` control is). A screen-reader user reading the refund article hears `learner_home_course_actionsv2.png` where a sighted user sees the buttons they are being told to click. `Screen_Shot_2021-05-13_at_10.57.09_AM.png` is the worst case — it is the example certificate image in the certificate-types article.
- **Language switching is per-article and graceful.** Six locales, and where a translation is missing the switcher links to the locale home rather than a 404. Locale names are given in their own language (`العربية (مصر)`, `Português do Brasil`, `Русский`).
- `Close site banner.` is a complete sentence as a control label — verbose for a visual button, but unambiguous to a screen reader. The banner itself renders twice in the DOM.
- No `Skip to content` link found in the server HTML of any page inspected.
- The MicroBachelors and verified-certificate comparison tables **render twice**: once as a table and once as a flattened label-value sequence (`Course delivery format` / `MicroBachelors® Programs` / `Online` / `Traditional college courses` / `Often in-person` …). This looks like a responsive card fallback, and it means an assistive-technology user may encounter the entire comparison twice. Worth flagging as suspected rather than confirmed, but the duplication is unambiguous in the server HTML.

## Transferable patterns

1. **Answer the hope you cannot meet, in the title, and say no in the first sentence.** `Does earning a MicroMasters® credential guarantee admission to a university master's program?` → `Earning a MicroMasters® credential does not guarantee admission to a full master's program.` → then the positive conditional → then the route to the learner's specific answer. Sixty words, zero possible misreading. Directly applicable to eligibility, pre-approval, guaranteed-rate and dispute-outcome copy, where the honest answer is "no, but".

2. **Write the disclaimer article your product name makes necessary.** `Does enrolling in MicroBachelors mean I'm enrolled in a university?` — if your name implies a status the product does not confer, ship the article that says so, and promote it. Cheaper than renaming, more honest than silence. Condition: the article has to be findable from the surface that creates the impression, which edX only partly achieves.

3. **Give ineligibility its own column.** The refund table's `When is the refund NOT available?` heading makes the outer bound (`6 months after the Payment Date`) as visible as the inner one, and names both clocks (`Course Start Date`, `Payment Date`) as defined terms. Reusable for any windowed right — chargebacks, returns, cancellation, warranty.

4. **Price the credential in the unit the comparison will be made in.** `around $166 USD per credit` plus `at least three college transfer credits`, rather than a program price. The user computes their own total against their own alternative. Same idea as a rate-conditional duration. `at least` as a floor cannot be disappointed; a range can.

5. **Show the free tier winning rows.** The audit-vs-verified table lists four rows where both tracks are identical. A paywall comparison that concedes the majority of the feature list buys credibility for the rows it needs. And give the free tier a stated purpose (`Explore course content before committing.`) rather than defining it by absence.

6. **Promise accessibility-bug parity, not accessibility-bug speed.** "remedied with the same level of priority as any other equivalent loss of function for individuals without disabilities" — a commitment that is checkable, survives a real backlog, and reclassifies accessibility defects as ordinary functional defects. The single most reusable sentence in this file.

7. **Name a help article after the visual affordance, not the data state.** `Why doesn't the green progress checkmark appear?` beats "Troubleshooting completion tracking" for the same reason `I sent money to the wrong person` beats "Correcting a mistaken payment": it is what the user will type. Applies wherever a badge, colour, tick or dot carries meaning.

8. **State the consequence of refusing consent, and supply the fallback in the same sentence.** "To chat live with an edX Learner Support agent, the use of cookies is required. If you reject these cookies, you can still contact edX Learner Support by sending an email to info@edx.org." Two buttons, no dark pattern, and refusal lands somewhere that works.

9. **Distinguish `certificate` from `certification` in one subordinate clause** — the first is permanent evidence of completion, the second is a time-limited licence to practise. A persistent sector-wide confusion, resolved in about twenty words while answering an adjacent question.

10. **Attribute and date every help article.** Named author, photo, `Updated` date, and a raw helpfulness fraction (`1161 out of 3229`). It exposes staleness and unhelpfulness honestly, which is the cost, and it is the reason a reader trusts the ones that score 93%.

## Caveats & gaps

- **Course detail pages are body-blocked.** `/learn/<topic>/<course>` renders only header and footer to a non-JS fetch. Consequently the **per-course commitment badges — weeks, hours per week, `Self-paced`, start date, price, prerequisites — were not observed**, and this is the biggest gap in a file whose brief includes commitment cues. The `estimated course length` figure is confirmed to exist (it governs audit expiry) but was never seen. A browser-rendered pass on two or three XDPs would close this.
- **The `Learn` mega-menu and all catalogue/search surfaces are client-rendered.** T1 is footer- and help-derived only.
- **Three help categories were not expanded**: `Account Basics`, `Mobile`, `FAQ`. `FAQ` in particular is flagged as a likely IA smell but was not inspected.
- **Article bodies: only four were opened** (refunds, certificate types, audit vs verified, MicroMasters admission). Everything else in T6, T7 and T11 is **article *titles* only** — high-signal for IA and task phrasing, silent on answer structure and quality.
- **MicroMasters and XSeries hub pages were not fetched.** The MicroMasters credential is characterised here from its help section and one article; `Certificate & Credit Pathways` is named but its actual content was not seen. `Executive Education` and `Boot Camps` are known only from price/duration prose and a refund exclusion.
- **No degree program page was inspected** — tuition, admissions copy and application wording for the actual degrees are unharvested.
- **Terms of Service & Honor Code, Privacy Policy, Trademark Policy and Modern Slavery Statement were not fetched.** The binding refund policy (`edx-terms-service#refund-policy`) was read only via its help-centre summary.
- **Pricing is promo-active and approximate.** Every figure here is prefaced in the source by `as little as`, `start at around`, `about`, or `up to 30% this week only`. A `Save up to 30%` banner was live during the harvest. Treat all prices as indicative and re-verify.
- **The 100M vs 86M learner discrepancy is reported, not adjudicated.** Both figures are quoted with their sources; neither is dated.
- In-product errors, empty states, toasts, validation messages, dashboard copy and email copy are all behind auth or unreachable. Marked `[documented]` or `[absent]` throughout.
- Localised help content was not compared against en-US; the six locales are noted structurally only.
