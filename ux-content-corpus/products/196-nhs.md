## T1 Navigation & IA labels

**Global nav — five items, all noun phrases, none verb-first** `[observed]`

`Health A to Z` · `NHS services` · `Healthy living` · `Mental health` · `Care and support`

Two of the five are *audience states* rather than content types (`Healthy living`, `Care and support`). `Mental health` is broken out of `Health A to Z` as a peer, which is a deliberate destigmatising IA decision — it is not filed under "conditions".

**The homepage splits information from action into two named blocks** `[observed]`

- `Health A to Z` — "Find out about conditions, symptoms, medicines, tests and treatments, including when to get help"
- `NHS services` — "Find out about NHS services including GPs, pharmacies, prescriptions, hospitals and dentists"

Note the scope line on `Health A to Z` ends on `including when to get help` — the escalation promise is made in the IA description, not only inside articles.

**`Manage your health` — four verb-first tasks, two of them "Find out how to…"** `[observed]`

`Order a repeat prescription` · `View your test results` ·
`Find out how to book an appointment at your GP surgery` ·
`Find out how to register with a GP surgery`

The `Find out how to…` prefix is doing honest work: the NHS cannot book the appointment, so it does not use `Book an appointment`. Two tasks are transactional and two are informational, and the grammar tells you which is which before you click. This is a reusable honesty device.

**`NHS services` finder block — bare imperative `Find a …`** `[observed]`

`Find a pharmacy` · `Find a dentist` · `Find a GP` ·
`Find NHS talking therapies for anxiety and depression` · `Find other NHS services`

**Service hub — `Top tasks in NHS services` then 13 categories** `[observed]`

Categories: `GPs` · `Hospitals` · `Dentists` · `Prescriptions` · `Pharmacies` ·
`Urgent and emergency care services` · `Mental health services` · `Vaccination services` ·
`Sexual health services` · `Opticians and sight tests` ·
`Refer yourself for help for back and joint problems` · `Find a service A to Z`

Twelve are noun-phrase categories; one — `Refer yourself for help for back and joint problems` — is a full imperative sentence sitting in a list of nouns. It is a live self-referral service smuggled into a category list, and it reads as an inconsistency.

**Urgent care sub-nav is the escalation ladder made navigable** `[observed]`

`When to use NHS 111 online or call 111` · `When to call 999` · `When to go to A&E` ·
`When to go to an urgent treatment centre (UTC)` · `Find urgent and emergency care services`

Every label is `When to <action>`. The site models the user's real question — *which door?* — rather than describing each service.

**Footer groupings** `[observed]`: an unlabelled four-column set — the nav repeated; then `NHS App`, `Find my NHS number`, `View your test results`, `About the NHS`, `Healthcare abroad`; then `Manage NHS profiles`, `Other NHS websites`; then `About us`, `Report an issue with the NHS website`, `Accessibility statement`, `Our policies`, `Cookies`. Closing line: `© Crown copyright`.

**Service manual IA** `[observed]`: `NHS service standard` · `Design system` · `Content guide` · `Accessibility` · `Community and contribution`. The content guide is a peer of the design system, not a subsection of it — an org-chart decision visible in the IA.

## T2 Value proposition & headline patterns

**There is no value proposition.** `[observed]` The homepage H1 is
`NHS website for England Find information and services to help you manage your health` —
an identity statement plus a purpose statement, with no claim, no number, no differentiator,
no persuasion. For a corpus dominated by commercial products this is the single most
useful negative example: a service with no competitor writes no value prop.

**Article openers are one-sentence definitions that lead with severity** `[observed]`

- Sepsis: "Sepsis is a serious reaction to an infection that can be life-threatening. It develops very quickly and needs urgent treatment in hospital."
- Asthma: "Asthma is a common condition that affects your breathing. It cannot currently be cured, but if it's well treated you should not have problems with symptoms."

The pattern is: **what it is → how serious → what that means for you.** Asthma pairs an
unwelcome fact ("cannot currently be cured") with an immediate bound ("if it's well treated…")
in the same sentence. The service manual's `Voice and tone` page prescribes exactly this:
"Our voice is neutral and factual. It's authoritative, but also calm and reassuring."

**Chest pain uses a bold standfirst that reassures then escalates in two sentences** `[observed]`

> "Most chest pain is not a sign of anything serious but get medical advice just in case. Get immediate medical help if you think you're having a heart attack."

Reassurance first, escalation second, in the same breath. Compare the sepsis page, which
does the reverse. The ordering tracks base rates, not tone preference.

**Section headers are questions the user is holding** `[observed]`:
`When and where to get medical help` · `How asthma affects your life` ·
`Things you can do to help with asthma` · `Common causes of chest pain` ·
`Who's at risk of getting sepsis` · `Help and support for sepsis` ·
`Noticing sepsis symptoms`.

`How asthma affects your life` is notable — a section about lived experience rather than
pathology, sitting in a clinical article.

## T4 Onboarding & getting-started

`[absent]` as a product concept. There is no onboarding, no first-run, no account setup on
the public surface. The equivalent artefact is the **service manual's 8-step guidance for
designing a form**, which is the NHS's published onboarding-design method rather than an
onboarding flow `[observed]`:

1. `Understand the problem before you write your questions`
2. `Make sure you need each question`
3. `Consider the sensitivities around your questions`
4. `Use filter questions to route users`
5. `Get the questions into order`
6. `Think of the form as a conversation`
7. `Write the supporting content for your form`
8. `Test your questions`

Steps 2 and 3 are the notable ones: *prove the question is necessary* and *prove it is safe
to ask* both precede any wording work. In a health context the second question is a
safeguarding step, not a UX nicety.

## T5 Form & field labels — **PRIORITY**

The NHS does not expose a public transactional form, so this section is built from the
published specification, which is more precise than most live forms would be.

**Definition of a form** `[observed]`, from the service manual:
"A form is a page that asks users for information in order to give them a service. It could
be a web page or a paper page." Note it covers paper — the guidance is channel-agnostic by
design.

**The three-discipline split** `[observed]`: "interaction design – making the form easy to
use / content design – making it easy to understand and answer / service design – making it
easy for the user to get things done." Content design is explicitly scoped to
*understanding and answering*, not to the whole form.

**Live label, hint and legend strings from the design system** `[observed]`

| String | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `What is your date of birth?` | Legend, styled as H1 | Question-as-page-heading: one question per page |
| `For example, 15 3 1984` | Hint | No separators, no leading zeros, no "DD MM YYYY" mask language |
| `Day` / `Month` / `Year` | Sub-field labels | Three inputs, `inputmode="numeric"`, widths 2/2/4 |
| `Have you changed your name?` | Legend, styled as H1 | Closed yes/no question |
| `This includes changing your last name or spelling your name differently` | Hint | Hint *expands the scope of the question*, it does not explain the control |
| `Yes` / `No` | Radio labels | |
| `Full name` | Label | Single field, not First/Last — a deliberate simplification |
| `First name` / `Last name` | Labels | Used where the split is genuinely needed |
| `Your details` | Page H1 above a multi-field group | |
| `Continue` | Submit button | Never "Next", never "Submit" |

The hint pattern is the transferable one. `This includes changing your last name or spelling
your name differently` pre-empts the user's real uncertainty ("does a spelling correction
count?") rather than restating the field. Compare the date hint, which teaches the *format*
by example rather than by notation.

**Question-grammar rules from the A to Z that bear on labels** `[observed]`
`you and your` has its own entry; `people or patients` instructs writers to address the
reader as "you"; `should` is to be avoided "as it can sound patronising".

## T6 Status & state language

Clinical *urgency* is the NHS's status vocabulary, and it is a closed, specified set rather
than an emergent one. See T7/T11 for the ladder itself.

**Content-lifecycle states shown to the user on every article** `[observed]`

- `Page last reviewed: 08 August 2023` / `Next review due: 08 August 2026` (chest pain)
- `Page last reviewed: 14 May 2026` / `Next review due: 14 May 2029` (sepsis)
- `Media last reviewed: 7 June 2024` / `Media review due: 7 June 2027` (video, sepsis)

Video assets carry their own independent review clock. There is a dedicated design-system
pattern named `Know that a page is up to date`, i.e. the freshness stamp is treated as a
*user need*, not as an editorial artefact.

**Defect worth recording** `[observed]`: the chest pain page shows
`Next review due: 08 August 2026` — a review date that had passed at harvest (2026-09-22).
The freshness pattern is doing its job by exposing the lapse, but the lapse is on a
999-escalation page.

**Documented product states** `[documented]`, from the accessibility statement's own defect
list: `Profile Manager` "automatically logged out after 15 minutes"; the Azure B2C login
system's error states; "Service search (newer templates)". These are the only named
in-product states reachable without auth.

## T7 Error, failure & recovery — **PRIORITY**

### 7a. The urgency-escalation ladder — the highest-stakes content pattern in the corpus

The NHS renders clinical escalation as three specified card variants, each with a
**visually hidden urgency prefix** inside the heading so screen-reader and heading-navigation
users get the severity before the instruction. Quoted exactly from the published pattern
and from live articles.

| Level | Card variant | Heading as specified / observed (verbatim) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Non-urgent care card (blue) | `Non-urgent advice: ` + `See a GP if:` |
| 2 | Urgent care card (red) | `Urgent advice: ` + `Ask for an urgent GP appointment or get help from NHS 111 if you have:` |
| 3 | Emergency care card (red and dark grey) | `Immediate action required: ` + `Call 999 or go to A&E now if:` |

Live variants observed on articles:

- `Immediate action required: Call 999 if:` — chest pain, asthma
- `Immediate action required: Call 999 or go to A&E if:` — sepsis
- `Immediate action required: Call 999 or go to A&E now if:` — pattern example
- `Urgent advice: Call NHS 111 if:` — sepsis (post-sepsis repeat infection)
- `Urgent advice: Ask for an urgent GP appointment if:` — asthma
- `Non-urgent advice: See a GP if:` — chest pain, asthma
- `Important:  Trust your instincts` — sepsis (warning callout, not a care card)
- `Important:  Using your inhaler` — asthma (warning callout)
- `Information:` — the neutral inset, used for logistics ("Do not drive to A&E.")

**The grammatical rule is fixed and load-bearing.** Every card heading is
`<urgency prefix>: <single call to action> if:` followed by a bulleted list of thresholds.
The published rule: "The card's heading should be 1 clear call to action. Use the format:
'Call 111 if:' or 'Speak to a GP if:' followed by a bulleted list in the body of the card."

**Why the hidden prefix exists**, verbatim: "People with visual disabilities may not
recognise care cards by their colour. Use the card's heading to tell them what to do. This
will also help people who are stressed or need to act quickly." Colour carries severity
for sighted users; the hidden text carries it for everyone else; and the visible imperative
carries it for people in panic. Three redundant channels for one signal.

**Threshold-writing rules, verbatim** `[observed]`

- "Try to keep bullet points short and to limit your list to the most likely symptoms or scenarios, but follow clinical advice."
- "In an urgent care card, you can add a final catch-all bullet point such as 'you or your child have any other symptoms you're worried about'." — note: **catch-all bullets are sanctioned at urgent level, not emergency level.**
- "Avoid vague or subjective language. Instead of 'a young child', for example, say 'a child aged 5 years or younger'."
- "Even if your care card includes only 1 symptom, use a bullet point under the 'if:' in the heading."
- "Ideally you should signpost to just 1 service in the heading but you can signpost to 2 (the top 2 services)."
- Ordering: "order them according to the likelihood and seriousness of symptoms. For example: for headaches, start with a non-urgent care card; for head injury, start with an emergency care card."
- Volume: "Avoid having too many care cards on the page. We want to give essential information and reassure people, not to scare them."
- Known failure mode: "Be aware that users often overlook text between components" — i.e. copy sandwiched between two cards is read as belonging to neither.

**Live threshold structure, described not reproduced.** The sepsis emergency card splits
into two age-banded sub-headings — `A baby or child under 5 years:` and
`An adult or child aged 5 years or over:` — each with its own bullet list. Temperature
thresholds are given as exact figures with age bands attached, and the skin-colour bullet
carries an explicit inclusion clause about black and brown skin. The asthma emergency card
inverts the usual structure: it is *conditional on an action already taken*
("do not feel better after taking the maximum dose…"), so the threshold is defined by
treatment failure rather than by symptom. The asthma page then places an **urgent** card
*after* the emergency card, for the post-attack case
(`Urgent advice: Ask for an urgent GP appointment if:` → "you've had an asthma attack and
have recovered"). Escalating *down* after an emergency, to catch the follow-up, is an
unusual and good pattern.

**`Information:` inserts that immediately follow an emergency card** `[observed]`, for the
logistics the panicking reader will not think of:
"Do not drive to A&E. Ask someone to drive you or call 999 and ask for an ambulance." ·
"Bring any medicines you take with you." · "Do not drive yourself to A&E." ·
"The person you speak to at 999 will give you advice about what to do."

### 7b. Validation errors — the published specification

**Error summary title, fixed string** `[observed]`: `There is a problem`
No exclamation, no apology, no "Oops", no "Error". Rendered in a `role="alert"` container.

**Error messages observed in the specification** `[observed]`

| Message | Field |
|---|---|
| `Date of birth must be in the past` | Date input |
| `Date of birth must include a year` | Date input, partial |
| `Select yes if you have changed your name` | Radios |
| `Enter your full name` | Text input |
| `Enter your first name` / `Enter your last name` | Text inputs |

Grammar: `Enter <thing>` for missing text, `Select <option> if <condition>` for missing
choice, `<Field> must <constraint>` for invalid input. Each is an instruction, not a
diagnosis. Screen readers receive a hidden `Error: ` prefix — "These users will hear, for
example, 'Error: Date of birth must be in the past'."

**Rules, verbatim** `[observed]`

- "Focus on telling users how to fix the problem rather than describing what's gone wrong."
- "Only display an error when someone tries to move to the next part of the service." Not on focus, not while typing, not on blur.
- "Do not clear any form fields when adding error messages." — with three reasons given, all in the user's interest.
- The aspiration: "make them easy to use so that users never see an error message."

**The single most transferable rule in this file** `[observed]`:

> "Do not use error messages to tell users that they are not eligible or do not have
> permission to do something. Instead, take them to a screen that: explains why you cannot
> accept the entry or selection; tells them what to do next; includes a way to leave the
> transaction."

**Ineligibility is not an error.** It is a destination with three mandatory content
elements, the third of which — an exit — is the one almost everyone omits. This maps
directly onto regulated financial eligibility copy.

### 7c. Recovery content in the article body `[observed]`

Asthma's `How to treat an asthma attack` is a recovery procedure written *before* the
emergency card, so the reader acts first and escalates second. It opens with posture and
composure ("sit up straight, to help your breathing. Try to stay calm.") before any dosing
instruction, and closes with a deference clause: "If your personal asthma action plan has a
different maximum dose…, follow the instructions in your plan." The national content
explicitly yields to the individual's plan.

## T9 Notifications & system messages

`[observed]` — three named callout components carry all in-page messaging:

- `Warning callout` — used for `Important:  Trust your instincts` and `Important:  Using your inhaler`. Prefix is `Important:` with a doubled space in the rendered output (a defect).
- `Inset text` — the published route for pointing to another organisation, "like a charity".
- `Notification banners` — a named component; no live instance observed.
- `Information:` inset — the neutral logistics channel beside care cards.

The pattern boundary is specified negatively and precisely: do not use a care card
"to warn users about something – use a warning callout instead"; do not use it "to point
users to another organisation, like a charity – use inset text"; do not use it "to signpost
users to the start of a digital service – use an action link".

**`Important:  Trust your instincts`** `[observed]` deserves its own note. It says a person
"might not have every symptom listed" and tells the reader to get help anyway. It is a
content component whose entire job is to **undercut the completeness of the list above it**.
Very few products are willing to publish "our own checklist may be wrong for you."

There is a public campaign/seasonal slot on the homepage (`Featured` — heatwave advice, flu
vaccination, self-referral), which functions as the system's broadcast channel.

## T10 Disclosures, legal & compliance

**Content-governance standard, six numbered clauses, each split `Essential` / `Best practice`** `[observed]`

1. `Design a clear process for creating content and follow it`
2. `Use relevant, up to date and recognised clinical evidence`
3. `Comply with relevant laws and regulation`
4. `Focus on user needs`
5. `Make your content easy to use`
6. `Make your content inclusive and accessible`

Essential requirements include "Follow a process for checking content and getting approval,
including clinical approval", "Have a planned review cycle", "Make clear which organisation
has published the content", and "Be transparent about adverts and any potential conflicts of
interest, such as sponsorship." Clause 6 Essential: "Make your content accessible to everyone
who needs it (WCAG 2.2 AA for websites and mobile apps)" and "Consider people who may have
difficulty getting the healthcare they need, like Gypsies and Travellers or people who do not
have English as a first language."

The standard states it "replaces their former assessment and certification scheme known as
the Information Standard, which has now closed" — i.e. a certification regime was replaced by
a published, self-applied standard. That governance shift is itself a content-ops artefact.

**Accessibility statement — a statutory disclosure written as an itemised confession** `[observed]`

Structure: what you should be able to do → `How accessible this website is` (leading with
PDFs) → `Feedback and contact information` → `Alternative formats` → `Reporting accessibility
problems with this website` → `Enforcement procedure` → `Technical information` →
`Compliance status` → `Non-accessible content` → `What we're doing to improve accessibility`
→ `Preparation of this accessibility statement`.

- Compliance claim: "This website is **partially compliant** with the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) version 2.2 AA standard, due to the non-compliances listed below."
- Response SLAs are published: "We aim to respond in 5 working days" for queries; "get back to you in 10 working days" for alternative-format requests.
- Alternative formats named: "accessible PDF, large print, easy read, audio recording or Braille".
- Every defect is listed with its **WCAG success criterion and number**, e.g. "Help and error information is not announced automatically to screen readers (Info and relationships 1.3.1)"; "Error messages are not associated with form fields. This fails under 3.3.1 Error Identification (Level A); 3.3.3 Error Suggestion (Level AA); and 4.1.3 Status Messages (Level AA)."
- Third-party failures are named with the vendor and the remediation status: Qualtrics ("We have reported the issues and asked for updates… currently a specific date has not been provided") and Microsoft Azure B2C ("Tickets for these issues have been raised with Microsoft").
- The regulatory exemption is stated plainly: "The accessibility regulations do not require us to fix PDFs or other documents published before 23 September 2018 if they're not essential to providing our services."
- Audit provenance is named: Nomensa, September 2020, "over 40 pages", retested August 2021, further audits June–July 2022 and January–February 2024.

**This is the model accessibility statement in the corpus.** It names the auditor, the
date, the standard, the criterion number for each failure, the third party responsible, and
the fact that some fixes have no date. It does not claim conformance it does not have.

**Health-content disclosures inside articles** `[observed]`: charity helplines are given
with website, phone number and a named support route (UK Sepsis Trust, Asthma and Lung UK,
Allergy UK, Beat Asthma). Clinical deference is explicit: "Do not self-diagnose – see a GP
if you're worried."

## T11 Content architecture & page templates — **PRIORITY**

### The condition-article template, described from three live articles

Observed order on **Sepsis** (the fullest instance):

1. H1 = condition name, bare
2. One-to-two sentence definition, leading with severity
3. `Easy Read` — a link to an alternative-format version, placed *above* the clinical content
4. `Symptoms of <condition>` — sub-divided by population (`in babies and children`, `in adults`), each a bullet list
5. A video with its own review dates
6. `Noticing <condition> symptoms` — who is harder to assess (named: "people with dementia", "people with a learning disability", "people who have difficulty communicating")
7. `Important:  Trust your instincts` warning callout
8. `When and where to get medical help` — **the care-card block**, age-banded
9. `Treatment for <condition>`
10. `Recovering from <condition>` with a `Do` list
11. `Complications of <condition>` — including a second, lower-level care card for the residual risk
12. `Causes of <condition>`
13. `Who's at risk of getting <condition>`
14. `Help and support for <condition>` — named charities with helplines
15. Review-date stamp

**Asthma** follows the same spine with condition-specific inserts (`How to treat an asthma
attack` placed before the emergency card; `Asthma inhalers` with a nested
`Important:  Using your inhaler`; `Disposing of your inhalers`; `Do` and `Don't` lists;
`How asthma affects your life`; `Asthma and pregnancy`).

**Chest pain** uses the *symptom* template, which is materially different: care cards come
**second, immediately after the standfirst**, before any explanation, and the body is a
two-column `Chest pain symptoms | Possible cause` table. Symptom pages escalate first and
explain later; condition pages explain first and escalate in the middle. The URL
`/conditions/chest-pain` redirects to `/symptoms/chest-pain/`, so the two templates have
separate URL namespaces (`/conditions/`, `/symptoms/`, `/medicines/`, `/tests-and-treatments/`,
`/mental-health/conditions/`).

**Structural defect** `[observed]`: on the sepsis page, everything from `Recovering from
sepsis` onward renders as one undifferentiated run of text in the served markup — headings
such as `Complications of sepsis`, `Causes of sepsis`, `Who's at risk of getting sepsis` lose
their block structure. If that reflects the delivered HTML rather than the fetch, the second
half of a life-threatening-condition article has no heading structure for screen-reader
navigation. Flagged as **suspected, not confirmed** — needs a browser-rendered check.

### Published page patterns

`[observed]` The design system names its templates explicitly: `A to Z page` ·
`Confirmation page` · `Hub page` · `Interruption page` · `Mini-hub` · `Question pages` ·
`Start page`. Plus behaviour patterns: `Check answers` · `Complete multiple tasks` ·
`Decide when and where to get care (care cards)` · `Find British Sign Language content` ·
`Know that a page is up to date` · `Ask for NHS numbers`.

`Interruption page` is the sanctioned way "to stop users in a journey", and the care-card
guidance says do not use a card for that purpose "unless it's to tell them to get medical
help now". So there are two stop mechanisms, and the clinical one is allowed to override the
transactional one.

### Content-guide IA

`[observed]` 13 pages: `Standard for creating health content` · `How we write` ·
`Voice and tone` · `Health literacy` · `A to Z of NHS health writing` · `Inclusive content` ·
`Writing NHS messages` · `Formatting` · `Punctuation` ·
`Numbers, measurements, dates and time` · `How to write good questions for forms` ·
`PDFs and other non-HTML documents` · `British Sign Language (BSL) quality assurance standard`.

A published **BSL quality assurance standard** as a peer of the punctuation page is a strong
signal about how the NHS scopes "content".

## T12 FAQs

`[absent]` as a component. The NHS does not ship an FAQ block on the pages harvested. The
equivalent artefacts are:

- **`When to <service>` navigation** on the urgent care hub, which is an FAQ set rendered as IA rather than as an accordion.
- **Care-card headings**, which answer "should I get help, and from whom?" without ever asking it.
- **A to Z entries**, which answer editorial questions rather than user questions.

The deliberate absence is itself a finding: where a commercial product would ship
"When should I see a doctor?" as an FAQ, the NHS ships it as a styled, colour-coded,
screen-reader-prefixed component with a specification. The question is too important to be
in an accordion.

## T13 Terminology & glossary — **PRIORITY (the A to Z)**

The **A to Z of NHS health writing** is a ~330-entry style lexicon covering every letter
A–Z, framed by a single sentence: "Words and phrases we use to make our content about health
and the NHS easy to understand." It mixes clinical vocabulary, service names, and typography
in one alphabet — `haemorrhage` sits near `headings`, `poo` near `PDFs`.

### Clinical and emotional vocabulary

| Term | NHS rule (verbatim or tightly summarised) | Rejected alternative |
|---|---|---|
| `death` | "Be direct wherever possible." Say "death" not "fatality", "passing" | euphemism |
| `died` | Say "died" instead of "passed away", "deceased" | |
| `loss` / `bereavement` | Not used as euphemisms for death; retained for feelings of loss and grieving in mental health content | |
| `terminal` | "We use 'terminal' when a condition or illness is likely to lead to death." | |
| `incurable` | "We avoid 'incurable'. Instead we say 'cannot be cured'." | |
| `prognosis` | "We prefer 'outlook'." | |
| `reduce pain` | "We say 'help with pain' or 'ease the pain'." | |
| `risk and risk factors` | "We prefer 'chance' to 'risk' when we're writing for the public." Both retained for specialist audiences | |
| `safe and safer` | "It's hard to know what is really 'safe' so we talk about 'safer sex' or 'safer drinking'." | "safe sex" |
| `acute` | Prefer "sudden", "starts suddenly", "short-term" | |
| `chronic` | Prefer "for a long time" or "does not go away" | |
| `normally` | "We use 'usually'. 'Normally' in a health context can make people feel they are not 'normal'." | |
| `positive` (test) | Some users read it as good news — explain "the test found what it was looking for" | |
| `negative` (test) | Some users read it as bad news — explain "the test did not find what it was looking for" | |

**The `death` entry carries an explicit, honest incompleteness note**: "We do not recommend
this language in cases of miscarriage or abortion, however. We are working on guidance on
this." A published style guide admitting an unresolved area rather than over-generalising.

**The `risk` → `chance` substitution** is the sharpest single rule for financial-services
transfer. "Risk" is the regulator's word; "chance" is the reader's. The NHS keeps both and
splits them by audience rather than picking one.

### Person-first and non-stigmatising rules

`suffering from` — "We do not use 'suffering from'. We talk about people having or living
with a disability or condition." · `diabetic` — "We do not talk about people as 'diabetic'.
We say they have diabetes" (retained in "diabetic eye screening") · `epileptic` — same rule ·
`homeless` — "We do not say 'the homeless' or 'homeless people'. We prefer to talk about
'people who are homeless'" · `people or patients` — address as "you"; use "people"; reserve
"patients" for where "people" would confuse · `service user` — "We prefer 'people' or
'patients'" · `BAME` / `BME` — "Use 'ethnic minorities', not 'BAME' or 'BME'" ·
`race` — "Use 'ethnicity', not 'race'" · `condition` — prefer "condition", "problem" or
"illness"; "disease"/"disorder" "can sound negative".

### Service-name conventions

| Term | Rule (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| `A&E` | "We use 'A&E'. You do not need to spell out 'accident and emergency'." |
| `ampersand (&)` | "Avoid using an ampersand, except in 'A&E'. Use 'and' instead." |
| `NHS 111 online` | "say 'Go to NHS 111 online or call 111' if it's a call to action"; small "o" in online; shorten to "111 online" after first mention; always "NHS 111 online" on non-NHS services |
| `111 online` (age limit) | "111 online is not for children under 5. Do not direct users to 111 online if the call to action is only about young children." |
| `GP` | "a GP" for urgent/out-of-hours advice (then "the GP"); "your GP" for ongoing care |
| `GP surgery` | For the public, "GP surgery" or "surgery" rather than "practice"; "practice" is for staff |
| `pharmacy` | "We use 'pharmacy', not 'chemist'." |
| `triage` | "Do not use the word 'triage' in a GP setting." |
| `online consultation` | "Do not use the term 'online consultation' or any variation of it, like 'eConsultation'." |
| `clinician` | "Do not use the word 'clinician'." Use "a doctor, nurse or other healthcare professional" |
| `walk-in centre` | "Not walk in centre or Walk-In Centre (unless it's the name of a particular centre)" |
| `phone numbers` | "We prefer to use 'call' or 'phone', not 'ring' or 'dial'." |
| `NHS number` | Format 3‑3‑4 with single spaces — "999 123 4567"; never a real number |
| `NHS` | "We do not usually spell out 'National Health Service'." |

**`999` has no entry in the A to Z and is not mentioned anywhere in it.** The most
consequential string on the site is unspecified in the style guide — an omission worth
recording.

### General-English rules

`drugs` → "We use 'medicines'. We only use 'drugs' for illegal drugs." ·
`medication` → "We use 'medicine', not 'medication'." ·
`side effects` / `symptoms` → people "get" or "have" them, "not 'develop' or 'experience'
them" · `eg` → "eg can sometimes be read aloud as 'egg' by screen readers" ·
`etc` → "Avoid etc." · `oral` → "we say 'mouth cancer' rather than 'oral cancer'" ·
`money` → "£75" and "£75.50", never "£75.00".

## T14 Voice, tone & accessibility — **PRIORITY (the published standards)**

### The three-word writing standard

`[observed]` `How we write` is organised under exactly three headings: **`Accurate`**,
**`Clear`**, **`Concise`** — in that order. Accuracy precedes clarity precedes brevity, and
the ordering is the policy.

**Accurate**: "Our content is factual, neutral and unambiguous. **We do not use metaphors.
We say what we mean.**" Subjectivity is bounded rather than banned — "good" and "bad" are
allowed "where our users do to describe their symptoms, for example 'bad pain' or 'bad
breath'" and "when there's a good evidence base". The worked table is the artefact:

| We would say | We would not say |
|---|---|
| It takes X weeks to get an appointment | It can take a long time to get an appointment for X |
| 4 out of 5 people recover fully in a week | You have a good chance of recovery |
| A 250ml glass of red wine | A large glass of wine |
| (More precise symptoms like) stomach ache, cramps, feeling sick, diarrhoea | Upset stomach |

Every left-hand cell replaces a **subjective adjective with a measurable quantity**. This is
the corpus's cleanest statement of the "vague qualifier → number" rule, and it is stated as
a clinical-safety requirement, not a style preference.

**Clear**: "We aim for a reading age of 9 to 11 years old. But we recognise that, with some
medical information, it's not easy to achieve this. In this case, try to make sure that an
11 to 14-year old will understand." A stated target *and* a stated fallback, which is more
honest than most published targets. "We use a plain English term first, then the medical
term. Example: piles (haemorrhoids)." And, importantly: "We use the same style for
specialist audiences like health professionals" — **no register split for clinicians.**

**Concise**: "short sentences of up to 20 words", "short paragraphs of up to 3 sentences",
"We use short words. For example, we prefer 'have' or 'get' to 'experience'", "We use the
active voice, for example 'find a pharmacy' rather than 'a pharmacy can be found'."

### Voice and tone

`[observed]` "Our voice is neutral and factual. It's authoritative, but also calm and
reassuring. It's empowering, rather than patronising, and personal, rather than formal."

Four operational rules: address the user as "you"; reassure by naming the good case beside
the bad ("Sertraline can cause side effects, but many people have no side effects or only
minor ones"); empower by writing "talk to your doctor about…" rather than "your doctor will
tell you about…"; and **"avoid using 'should' as it can sound patronising."**

Tone is explicitly variable and the variation is specified by subject matter: "direct,
serious and reassuring" for a diagnosis; "encouraging and conversational" for exercise or
diet. The register floor is stakes-indexed, exactly as the Wise exemplar observed — but the
NHS *publishes the rule* rather than merely practising it.

### Health literacy as the rationale

`[observed]` "more than 4 in 10 adults struggle with health content for the public" and
"more than 6 in 10 adults struggle with health content that includes numbers and statistics."
Consequences named include "increased A&E attendances and hospital admissions" and "reduced
life expectancy", with the cost stated: "up to 5% of national health spending". It is framed
as inequality: "There is a close link between socio-economic deprivation and low health
literacy."

The four worked examples of comprehension failure are the most persuasive content-design
artefact in the corpus — a woman who "sprayed her inhaler on her neck because she had been
told to spray it on her 'throat'", and a man who "did not turn up for cancer tests because he
did not know Radiology and X-ray department were the same thing". Terminology failure is
documented as clinical harm, with cases.

**On readability tools**: "We do not recommend readability tools, except to help you
prioritise your content. Tools cannot tell you how usable your content is. It's best to test
it with your users." A published reading-age target *and* a published refusal to measure it
mechanically.

### Inclusive content

`[observed]` Five named dimensions: `Age` · `Disabilities and conditions` ·
`Ethnicity, religion and nationality` · `Sex, gender and sexuality` · `Skin symptoms`.
Governing instruction: "Check with your users how they want to be described and test your
content with them."

**`Skin symptoms` as a first-class inclusive-content category** is distinctive and shows up
live: both the sepsis adult and child bullet lists carry "on black or brown skin this may be
easier to see on the palms of the hands or soles of the feet". A diagnostic instruction, not
a representation gesture.

### Accessibility practice

`[observed]` `Skip to main content` first in DOM on every page. Visually hidden text carries
urgency (`Non-urgent advice: `, `Urgent advice: `, `Immediate action required: `) and error
severity (`Error: `). `role="text"` wraps the care-card heading so the hidden prefix and the
visible imperative are announced as one string. `aria-hidden="true"` on the decorative card
arrow and on inline SVG icons. `focusable="false"` on icons. Error summary in a
`role="alert"` container. Alt text on a social image is scene-level and clinically framed:
"Cropped image of a child getting urgent medical treatment on a hospital bed."

### Negative findings, recorded honestly

- **`call 111` on the homepage has a malformed `tel:` URI** (`<tel: 111>`, with a space). The escalation link most likely to be tapped in a hurry.
- **`Next review due: 08 August 2026`** on the chest-pain page had passed at harvest.
- **Doubled space after `Important:`** in warning-callout headings on both sepsis and asthma (`Important:  Trust your instincts`), and after the section prefix in service-manual H1s (`Content guide –  How we write`).
- **Sepsis article loses heading structure** in the served markup from `Recovering from sepsis` onwards (suspected, not confirmed).
- **`Find my NHS number` (first person) sits beside `View your test results` (imperative)** in the same footer column.
- **`Refer yourself for help for back and joint problems`** is an imperative sentence inside a list of noun categories, in both the homepage `Featured` block and the service-category list.
- **The A to Z contradicts itself in several places**: the `can't or cannot` entry mandates "cannot", but the page's own examples use "can't" (under `poo` and `organisations`) and "doesn't" (under `Deaf people`); the `normally` entry warns against "normal" while the `temperature` entry writes "it's normal for people's temperature to rise when they are ill"; `jab` says "We use 'vaccine', not 'jab'" while `flu jab or flu vaccine` permits "flu jab" on search grounds; the `microgram` entry grants and withdraws the same permission in consecutive sentences.
- **The A to Z has at least one dead anchor**: `convulsions` links to `#seizure-or-fit` while `fit` links to `#seizure-fit`; the actual heading is `seizure (fit)`.
- **Stale example organisations**: the A to Z retains `clinical commissioning groups (CCGs)` and `NHSX` as entries purely to say they no longer exist, yet still cites Public Health England (abolished 2021) as a current example under `immunisation` and `cervical screening`.
- **Typos in published guidance**: `unplanned` reads `Use "unplanned' rather than "unwanted"` (mismatched quote marks); the Inclusive content contents list reads `Sex, gender and sexualithy` in the numbered list while the nav above it reads `Sex, gender and sexuality`.
- **`999` is absent from the A to Z** despite being the highest-consequence string the NHS writes.

## Transferable patterns

1. **Specify escalation as a component, not as copy.** Three fixed variants, a fixed heading
   grammar (`<prefix>: <one call to action> if:` + bulleted thresholds), a hidden severity
   prefix for non-visual users, and an ordering rule based on likelihood-and-seriousness. Any
   product with graduated urgency (fraud alerts, account compromise, payment failure,
   regulatory deadlines) can lift this wholesale. **Condition: the prefix must be part of the
   heading, not a sibling element, or heading-navigation users lose the severity.**
2. **Ineligibility is a page, not an error.** "Do not use error messages to tell users that
   they are not eligible… take them to a screen that explains why, tells them what to do next,
   and includes a way to leave the transaction." The exit is the part everyone forgets.
   Directly applicable to credit decisioning, KYC failure and BNPL eligibility.
3. **Replace every subjective adjective with a measurable quantity, and state it as a safety
   rule.** "4 out of 5 people recover fully in a week" over "You have a good chance of
   recovery"; "A 250ml glass of red wine" over "A large glass of wine". Transfers to APR,
   timing and fee copy with no modification.
4. **`risk` → `chance` for the public, `risk` retained for specialists.** Keep the regulator's
   term where the regulator reads, use the reader's term where the reader reads, and publish
   the split so it is auditable.
5. **Publish a reading-age target with an honest fallback, and refuse to measure it with a
   tool.** "9 to 11 years old… In this case, try to make sure that an 11 to 14-year old will
   understand", plus "We do not recommend readability tools… It's best to test it with your
   users."
6. **Write the component whose job is to undercut your own list.** `Important:  Trust your
   instincts` tells the reader the checklist above may not fit them. Any eligibility,
   symptom or troubleshooting list needs one.
7. **Ship the logistics the panicking user will not think of.** "Do not drive to A&E. Ask
   someone to drive you or call 999 and ask for an ambulance." The obvious-in-hindsight
   instruction belongs immediately after the escalation, in a visually distinct neutral
   inset.
8. **Let the task grammar tell the truth about what you can do.** `Find out how to book an
   appointment at your GP surgery` rather than `Book an appointment`. Verb-first where you
   can transact, `Find out how to…` where you cannot.
9. **Write the accessibility statement to the criterion number.** Name the auditor, the date,
   the standard, each failure with its WCAG reference, the third party responsible, and the
   fixes that have no date. Claim partial compliance when that is what you have.

## Caveats & gaps

- **No authenticated surface harvested.** NHS App, NHS login, Profile Manager, appointment booking, prescription ordering and test results are all behind auth. Their states, errors and empty states are `[documented]` at best, mostly via the accessibility statement's defect list.
- **Service search not executed.** No query was submitted, so no results page, no zero-results copy and no validation behaviour was observed. `[absent]`
- **Only three condition/symptom articles read** of tens of thousands. The template description is evidence-based but not exhaustive; mental-health, medicines, pregnancy and tests-and-treatments templates were not examined.
- **A to Z entry bodies were read in a delegated sub-pass**, and only ~45 of ~330 entries were quoted in full. Headword coverage is exhaustive; rule coverage is selective and skewed toward the death/pain/risk/service-name cluster this brief prioritised.
- **The sepsis heading-structure defect is unconfirmed.** It may be an artefact of the text-extraction route rather than the delivered HTML. It needs a browser-rendered or source-HTML check before it is cited.
- **No Welsh, Scottish or Northern Irish equivalent examined.** nhs.uk is England-only; NHS Inform (Scotland), NHS 111 Wales and nidirect use different content standards and were out of scope.
- **British Sign Language and Easy Read versions** were linked but not opened; the BSL quality assurance standard page was not fetched.
- **Clinical content deliberately not reproduced.** Symptom lists, temperature thresholds, dosing instructions and treatment sequences are described structurally and by heading only. No clinical threshold has been paraphrased anywhere in this file; where a threshold is referenced, only its exact heading is quoted.
