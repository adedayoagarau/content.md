## T1 Navigation & IA labels

**Five-group nav, and the groups are a content strategy** `[observed]`

| Group | Members |
|---|---|
| `Product` | `Tracking cycle`, `Getting pregnant`, `Pregnancy`, `Help Center`, `Flo for Partners`, `Anonymous Mode`, `Real stories`, `Flo app reviews`, `Flo Premium` (New), `Secret Chats` (New), `Symptom Checker` (New) |
| `Health Library` | `Your cycle`, `Health 360°`, `Getting pregnant`, `Pregnancy`, `Being a mom`, `LGBTQ+`, `Quizzes` |
| `Calculators` | Ten named tools (see below) |
| `About` | `Medical Affairs`, `Science & Research`, `Pass It On Project` (New), `Privacy Portal`, `Press Center`, `Flo Accuracy`, `Careers`, `Contact Us` |
| `For Clinicians` | → med.flo.health |

Three observations. First, **`Anonymous Mode` is a nav item under Product** — a privacy control promoted to peer status with tracking and pregnancy. Second, **`Privacy Portal` and `Flo Accuracy` sit under About**, alongside `Medical Affairs` and `Science & Research`: Flo's nav treats privacy and accuracy as *institutional credentials* rather than legal boilerplate. Third, `LGBTQ+` is a **top-level Health Library category**, not a tag.

**The three life-stage product names are gerund/noun user states, not features** `[observed]`: `Tracking cycle` · `Getting pregnant` · `Pregnancy`. Expanded in the footer to the `Flo for …` construction: `Flo for Tracking Your Period` · `Flo for Getting Pregnant` · `Flo for Pregnancy`. **The product is named after the user's intention, and switching intention switches the product.** This is the core IA idea: Flo is one app with named modes, and the mode names are things the user is *doing*, one of which (`Getting pregnant`) is a hope rather than a state.

**Calculators as a nav group** `[observed]` — ten tools, each named for the number it produces: `Ovulation calculator` · `hCG calculator` · `Pregnancy test calculator` · `Menstrual cycle calculator` · `Period calculator` · `Implantation calculator` · `Pregnancy weeks to months calculator` · `Pregnancy due date calculator` · `IVF and FET due date calculator` · `Due date calculator by ultrasound`.

The granularity is the point: **four separate due-date calculators** (standard, weeks-to-months, IVF/FET, by-ultrasound). `IVF and FET due date calculator` names assisted-conception routes explicitly rather than folding them into a generic tool — a small but real inclusion decision for people whose conception did not follow the default model. `hCG calculator` uses the unglossed clinical abbreviation, which assumes a user already deep in fertility tracking.

**Health Library category names** `[observed]`: `Your cycle` (possessive) · `Health 360°` (coined, meaning whole-body health beyond reproduction) · `Getting pregnant` · `Pregnancy` · `Being a mom` · `LGBTQ+` · `Quizzes`.

`Being a mom` is worth flagging against Flo's own inclusive-language positioning — it is the one life-stage label that is unambiguously gendered and informal, where `Getting pregnant` and `Your cycle` are not. Sub-categories under it: `Recovering from birth` · `Adjusting to motherhood` · `Raising a baby`.

**Footer: four groups** `[observed]` — `Flo App` · `Company` · `Content` · `Legal`. The `Content` group is unusual and good: `Health Library`, `Editorial process and standards`, `Advertising principles`, `Tools`. **Publishing your editorial standards and your advertising principles as standing footer links** treats content governance as a user-facing commitment.

`Legal` contains eight entries including `Your Body, Your Data. Period.` — a **slogan used as a legal-section link label**, pointing to the Privacy Portal. Emotional framing on a compliance link.

## T2 Value proposition & headline patterns

**Hero** `[observed]`

> Eyebrow: `Over 7 million 5-star ratings¹`
> Headline: `We're Flo, the world's #1 women's health app²`
> Subhead: "Over 420 million people³ around the world use the Flo app to track their periods, ovulation, pregnancy, and perimenopause."

The headline/subhead pair contains Flo's entire language tension in two lines: the **headline says `women's health app`**, the **subhead says `people`**. Superscript footnotes on both. Three of the four content elements above the fold carry a numbered reference marker.

**The footnote apparatus is the most disciplined in this batch** `[observed]`. The home page carries a `References` block listing:

1. AppFollow, April 2024, iOS
2. Sensor Tower, August 2023, by downloads
3. Total app downloads
4. Survey of 500 US obstetricians and gynecologists, DRG, 2021
5. Monthly active users as of May '24

Note reference 3: `Total app downloads`. Flo's headline number (`420 million people`) is footnoted to *downloads*, and Flo says so. A claim that would read as user count is bounded by its own footnote to the weaker metric. Reference 1 bounds the ratings claim to `iOS` only. This is **claim-then-bound**, the Wise pattern, executed with superscripts rather than adjacent small text — and Flo applies it to social-proof numbers, which most products leave unbounded.

The `#1` claim is bounded twice over: to `by downloads` and to a named third party with a date.

**Section headers are questions or possessive statements** `[observed]`:
- `What can you do with the Flo app?`
- `Why choose Flo?`
- `What our users say about Flo`
- `Your cycle is so much more than your period` (tracking-cycle hero)
- `What could your symptoms mean?` (Symptom Checker hero)
- `Access for all, not just the privileged`
- `Questions about your body, answered by experts`

**`Your cycle is so much more than your period`** is the strongest single headline. It reframes the category: the competitor set is "period trackers", and Flo's opening move is to say the period is the smallest part of the thing. The subhead completes it — "It can affect everything — from your mood to your energy levels and sex drive."

**Three-pillar trust block, each a noun phrase + a link to proof** `[observed]`:

| Pillar | Proof link |
|---|---|
| `Predictions you can plan around` | `Why our users love the Flo App` |
| `Personal data that stays private to you` | `Your privacy FAQs answered` |
| `Powered by doctors, trusted by millions` | `Meet our medical team` |

Every pillar routes to evidence rather than elaborating. `Predictions you can plan around` is benefit-as-consequence (not "accurate predictions" but what accuracy lets you *do*). `stays private to you` — the preposition does the work: not "private" but private *to you*, i.e. not to Flo either.

**Tagline** `[observed]`, in the footer of every page: `Know your body. Own your health.` Two imperatives, three words each, escalating from knowledge to ownership. A second tagline runs the privacy surfaces: `Your body, your data. Period.` — the pun on `period` as both punctuation and menstruation is the most-reused piece of copy on the site, appearing as a legal link label, a page H1, and a section close.

**Symptom Checker headline** `[observed]`: `What could your symptoms mean?` — modal `could`, not "do". The hedge is in the headline. Supported by two cited statistics with inline links to PMC and JMIR: "One in 10 of us has a female health condition like polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), endometriosis or fibroids. But getting a diagnosis can take up to 12 years." Then the mission line: "Flo wants to change that. We'll give you the information you need to have a confident conversation with your doctor about what's going on inside your body."

**That sentence defines the product's boundary as its value proposition.** The output is not a diagnosis — it is *a better conversation with a doctor*. Flo sells the handoff.

## T4 Onboarding & getting-started

**Onboarding is mode selection** `[documented]`. The app's five states, as named on the home page:

1. `Track your cycle and symptoms`
2. `Understand your fertility better`
3. `Follow your pregnancy week by week`
4. `Share Flo with your partner`
5. `Feel supported through Perimenopause`

Each is an imperative or a felt-state, not a feature name. Note #5 — `Feel supported` is the only one that names an emotion as the outcome rather than an action.

**Mode-switching is explicitly reversible and named** `[observed]`, from tracking-cycle: "If you decide you want to get pregnant, you can switch to our Trying to Conceive mode for your personalized journey to parenthood."

`Trying to Conceive` (abbreviated `TTC` in the Health Library — "why is it important if you're TTC?") is the community's own term, adopted as a mode name. **Flo uses the user community's acronym in its editorial headlines**, which signals membership to people already inside the fertility world.

**The onboarding entry is a quiz** `[observed]` — most CTAs route to `quiz.flo.health`. A questionnaire-first onboarding, which is why the app can open in the right mode. No public step sequence is published. `[absent]`

**Symptom Checker: a three-step flow, each step a truncated sentence completed by its heading** `[observed]`:

| Step heading | Continuation |
|---|---|
| `Take five minutes` | "...to answer questions about your symptoms.*" |
| `Know instantly` | "...if your symptoms match those of PCOS, endometriosis and fibroids.**" |
| `Feel empowered` | "...to have a conversation with your doctor about your health." |

The construction is elegant: the bold heading is the *verb phrase the user experiences*, and the light continuation supplies the object. Read down, the headings alone form the emotional arc — **take / know / feel**. And the arc terminates not in an answer but in `a conversation with your doctor`.

Both asterisks resolve immediately below, not at page foot:
> `*Not available in the UK or EU/EEA.`
> `**Flo's Symptom Checker is for informational purposes only. It is not a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment of any kind.*`

**Note what is being disclosed at step 2.** `Know instantly` is the most over-promising phrase on the page, and it is the phrase that carries the disclaimer marker. The hedge is attached to the exact word that needs it.

## T5 Form & field labels

`[documented]` — no public form; all logging is in-app.

**The tracking vocabulary, as published** `[observed]`:

- **`70+ symptoms and events`** — the field count, stated twice ("Track 70+ symptoms and events", "tracking 70+ symptoms and events"). Note the pairing: `symptoms` **and** `events`. Two different data classes — things that happen to you and things you do — named separately rather than collapsed into "logs".
- Named loggable items across pages: `the first day of your period`, `your body temperature`, `your symptoms`, `cycle dates`, `marked symptoms`, `your aim for using Flo`, `birth control method`, `reminders`.
- Example symptoms given in user-language, not clinical: `Cravings before your period starts? Bloating around ovulation?` — posed as **questions the user recognises**, tied to cycle phase, rather than as a checklist.
- `your aim for using Flo` — the onboarding intent field, and the noun is `aim`, not "goal". Softer, less performance-loaded.

**Birth-control logging is comprehensive and named by method** `[observed]`, from the tracking-cycle FAQ: "Whether you take oral contraceptives (aka the pill), have an IUD or implant fitted, or use the injection, patch, or ring, you can easily add your birth control in the Flo app." Seven methods enumerated, with the colloquial gloss `(aka the pill)` attached to the clinical term. Clinical-term-first with lay gloss — the inverse of Ada's ordering.

**The Symptom Checker's question design** `[documented]` and deliberately shallow: "This works by asking you a series of questions about your symptoms to see if they match common signs of female health conditions like endometriosis and PCOS." Duration stated as **five minutes**; described elsewhere as "a five-minute self-assessment that you can then share with your doctor". Scope limited by name to three conditions: **PCOS, endometriosis, fibroids**.

**The scope limit is itself the safety design.** Unlike Ada's open-ended differential, Flo's checker matches against three named conditions and nothing else. A narrow checker can hedge more confidently because it never has to say "or it could be anything".

**No question strings, field labels, placeholders or validation text were observed.** `[absent]` Nothing is reconstructed here.

**Anonymous Mode's one observable field** `[documented]`: an **access code**, evidenced by the screenshot alt text `Screenshot of the Flo app, showing creation of access code within Anonymous Mode.` and `...showing entering access code in Anonymous mode.` Because the account has no email, the access code is the sole recovery credential — and the FAQ is explicit that there is no recovery path (T10).

## T6 Status & state language

`[documented]`, from published feature copy:

- **Cycle-phase vocabulary**: `period`, `ovulation`, `fertile window`, `PMS`, `perimenopause`, `the day of ovulation`. From the Clue comparison it is worth noting Flo says `fertile window` and `day of ovulation` as predictions, not certainties — see the prediction hedging below.
- **Prediction language is always modal** `[observed]`: "know when your period is **likely** to start", "Know when your period's **likely** to arrive", "know how you **might** feel tomorrow", "learn what they **might** mean". `likely` and `might` are the two hedges, applied consistently across every prediction claim on the site.
- **Accuracy stated as a survey result, not a system property** `[observed]`: "90% of users say Flo accurately predicts period start³" — footnoted to "2,000 people surveyed in 2021". The claim is about **user perception**, and the footnote makes that unmissable. A weaker claim, honestly bounded, rather than a strong claim unsupported.
- `71% of users say Flo helps improve how they manage menstrual symptoms⁴` — same construction.
- **Cycle report** `[observed]`: "You'll get a handy report at the end of every cycle so you can spot patterns and figure out what's normal for you." The terminal artefact of a cycle is a report; `monthly cycle report alerts` is the notification name.
- **Account states in Anonymous Mode** `[observed]`: original account → switch → "a new, separate account is created for you" → original "will be automatically deleted". Explicitly one-way: "you will not have the ability to reconnect your Anonymous account (or its associated data) to your original account."

**`what's normal for you`** is the recurring frame, appearing three times across pages: "Figure out what's normal for you", "Understand what's normal for you", "No two women's cycles are the same." Flo's status language consistently **relativises normality to the individual** rather than to a population range. For a category historically built on "the 28-day cycle", replacing the population norm with a personal baseline is the substantive content position.

## T7 Error, failure & recovery

`[documented]`, and concentrated entirely in Anonymous Mode — which is where Flo's recovery content is genuinely exceptional.

**Flo documents the costs of its own privacy feature, at length, before you use it.** The Anonymous Mode FAQ contains five questions whose answers are essentially "here is what you lose":

| Question (verbatim) | What it discloses |
|---|---|
| `Are any features of Flo unavailable in Anonymous Mode?` | Flo for Partners, Flo for Family, web subscriptions, wearable pairing, and all emails including cycle reports |
| `How does customer support work in Anonymous Mode?` | No data recovery, no in-app support, no export/delete/amend assistance, no access to prior Health Assistant chats, saved stories or Secret Chats |
| `Trouble switching to Anonymous Mode` | Four blocking conditions with a remedy for each |
| `Should I switch to Anonymous Mode?` | "It's up to you" — then the irreversibility warning |
| `What happens to the data I logged in my original account…?` | Deletion trigger, what transfers, what does not |

**The strongest sentence in the file** `[observed]`:

> "This limitation is an intentional one to ensure you remain anonymous."

A product naming a support degradation as **intentional** and attributing it to the user's own benefit. Most products present feature limitations as regrettable or technical. Flo says: this is broken *on purpose*, and here is the purpose.

**The default is defended rather than assumed** `[observed]`: "we will no longer be able to recover the data you've previously shared with us, including if your phone is lost, damaged, or stolen. **That's why we offer Anonymous Mode as an option rather than the default.**" A design-decision rationale published in a consumer FAQ. Flo explains why the more-private option is not on by default, and the reason given is data loss risk, not business interest.

**`Trouble switching to Anonymous Mode`** is a conditional troubleshooting block — four `If you're…` / `If you are…` / `If you have…` branches, each with an instruction and, in one case, a remedy offer: "Please reach out to support@flo.health, where we can assist you with a refund and advice on the next steps." A **refund offered inside a privacy-feature help answer**, because a web subscription blocks the privacy feature.

**Pattern worth naming: the pre-mortem FAQ.** Flo sequences the Anonymous Mode FAQ as how-to → why → how-it-works → is-it-safe → who-can → *should I* → what-breaks → support-loss → blockers → subscription impact → data fate. The persuasive questions come first, the cost questions immediately after, and the user is walked through every consequence **before** the irreversible switch. Compare products that put the irreversibility warning in a modal at the point of no return.

## T10 Disclosures, legal & compliance — medical disclaimer, data use, consent

**Priority section.**

### Medical disclaimer

**Flo's disclaimer is narrow, targeted and duplicated at the point of claim** `[observed]`. There is no site-wide medical disclaimer of the Ada type. Instead the disclaimer attaches specifically to the Symptom Checker, and appears twice on one page:

> "Flo's Symptom Checker is for informational purposes only. It is not a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment of any kind."

Once as a footnote to the `Know instantly` step, once inside the FAQ answer to `Does Flo track symptoms?` prefixed `Please note:`. Both in italics.

**What is disclaimed and what is not.** Flo disclaims the *checker*. It does not attach a disclaimer to the Health Library, the calculators, the Health Assistant, or the prediction engine. The trust argument for those is made structurally instead — through medical review attribution (below) rather than through a limitation statement. That is a coherent strategy but it leaves the ten calculators (including `hCG calculator` and `Implantation calculator`, both used by people in acute fertility distress) without any observed caveat.

**The boundary is framed as the product's purpose, not its limit** `[observed]`: "We'll give you the information you need to have a confident conversation with your doctor", "gives you the information you need to get answers from your doctor", "you can then take this information to your doctor for personalized medical advice, support, and a referral to a specialist for further tests if they think this is necessary."

Note the final clause: `if they think this is necessary`. Flo declines to promise the referral. Even in the handoff sentence, the clinician's judgement is preserved as the deciding authority.

**Geofencing as disclosure** `[observed]`: `Not available in the UK or EU/EEA` appears three times across two pages, as an asterisk and as a footnote to the Premium feature list. Flo tells users in the EU/UK that a feature does not exist for them rather than silently hiding it. A jurisdiction limit surfaced as product copy.

### Medical-review attribution — the reusable trust device

**Flo's pattern is institutional-plus-linked-individual** `[observed]`, from the editorial-standards page:

> "Professional experts from our medical board or a trusted organization review every piece of Flo content for the app and website before it goes live. Our reviewers check the content, provide notes, and confirm that it's thorough, accurate, and reflects the latest medical findings. **At the bottom of each article, you'll find a link to the profile of the person or organization who reviewed it.**"

Four components make up the device:

1. **A named reviewer profile link** at article foot — person *or* organisation.
2. **A last-updated date** — "You can find when the last update was made at the bottom of each article."
3. **References at article foot** — "The references for the medical information used are provided at the bottom of each article", with a published `list of trusted sources`.
4. **A re-certification commitment** — "When new information becomes available, content is re-worked and **re-certified** by our medical review board."

`re-certified` is the notable verb. It frames content maintenance as a compliance act with a named authority, not an editorial refresh.

**The source hierarchy is published and ranked** `[observed]`:
- "Peer-reviewed articles from credible databases (including unbiased and nonprofit domains such acog.org, cdc.gov, and others)"
- "Internationally recognized health advocacy organizations and academic institutions (such as nhs.uk, who.int, and others)"

Naming the specific domains (`acog.org`, `cdc.gov`, `nhs.uk`, `who.int`) rather than describing the category is what makes this checkable.

**A legal review stage is disclosed in the content pipeline** `[observed]`: "all of our content gets a comprehensive review by the Flo legal team to make sure we're providing information that's safe and relevant to our users **according to their age, location, and needs**." Age- and location-conditional content safety, stated as an editorial-process step. This is the mechanism behind the EU/UK geofence.

**Named collaborators** `[observed]`: `The United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA)`, `The European Board & College of Obstetrics and Gynaecology (EBCOG)`, `Society of Endometriosis and Uterine Disorders (SEUD)`.

**Expert-count claims are inconsistent across pages** `[observed]` — a content-ops defect on a trust claim: `100+ doctors, scientists and medical experts` (home), `100+ doctors, health experts, and scientists` (editorial standards), `120+ doctors and health experts` (tracking-cycle, twice), `over 140 external doctors, scientists, and health experts` (medical expertise), and a `panel of over 120 health care experts` (medical expertise, same page as the 140). **Four different numbers, two of them on the same page.** Recorded factually: the numbers may count different populations (internal vs external, reviewers vs total), but no page says so.

### Privacy — the FTC action and Anonymous Mode

**Flo publishes its own regulatory history in a timeline.** `[observed]` From the Privacy Portal, under the heading `Our Privacy Journey`, the entry `2019-2021: Learning & Improving`:

> "After receiving feedback about our use of the Facebook Analytics SDK in 2019, we immediately removed it. We then reached a settlement with the FTC to avoid the costs of litigation and conducted an independent privacy audit. We hired dedicated privacy leadership and overhauled our privacy framework."

**Recording this factually and without editorialising, as instructed.** Observations about the *copy*, not the conduct:

- The section heading is `Learning & Improving`, not "Enforcement" or "The FTC action". The frame is developmental.
- The trigger is described as `receiving feedback about our use of the Facebook Analytics SDK` — `feedback` is the softest available noun for what preceded an FTC proceeding.
- `to avoid the costs of litigation` states a reason for settling. The copy does not describe the FTC's allegations, and does not state what the settlement required.
- The remediation is listed in four verbs: removed, settled, audited, hired, overhauled.
- Subsequent timeline entries name the auditor (`Guidepost Solutions`, engaged August 2022) and the certifications by date.

**This is a genuinely unusual artefact: a consumer health company publishing a dated timeline that includes its own regulatory settlement, on its marketing site, under a nav item.** Whether the framing is adequate is a judgement this file does not make. What is recordable is that the disclosure exists, is findable in two clicks from the home page, and is placed inside a narrative of improvement rather than isolated in a legal notice.

**The "never sold" claim is stated in an absolute, repeated form** `[observed]`, appearing at least six times across pages in near-identical wording:
- "Flo has never — and will never — sell your personal data."
- "Flo has never, and will never, sell user information or share it with third parties for advertising purposes."
- "Flo never has sold, nor will sell user data."
- "Your data belongs to you, and at Flo, it's never for sale."
- "No. Flo does not, has never, and will never sell your data. Nor will we share your health data with third-party companies for advertising purposes."

Note the **two-part structure** in the fuller versions: no selling, *and* no sharing for advertising. The second clause is the one the 2019 issue turned on, and it is carried alongside the first in the longer formulations.

**Anonymous Mode — the privacy-control vocabulary**

The core promise `[observed]`:
> "Anonymous Mode gives you access to the Flo app without sharing any information that identifies you (such as your name or email address), so your health information is completely private to you."

And the consequence, stated as the feature's whole point:
> "if Flo ever receives an official request to identify an Anonymous Mode user by name or email, it won't be possible to satisfy that request."

This sentence appears **four times** across the Anonymous Mode page. It is the load-bearing claim, and it is phrased as a *capability limit on Flo*, not as a policy commitment. The distinction matters and Flo evidently knows it: a promise not to comply can be broken; an inability to comply cannot.

**The two-term glossary Flo teaches the user** `[observed]` — the clearest privacy explainer in this batch:

> "Your data is information you log in the app. This includes health data like the first day of your period, your body temperature, or your symptoms."
> "Personal identifiers are things like your name, email address, Apple ID, IP address, or Google account."

Two definitions, concrete examples for both, and then the mechanism in one sentence: "When you use Anonymous Mode, we don't ask for or log any of these personal identifiers, so you stay exactly that: anonymous."

Expanded elsewhere into a bulleted identifier list `[observed]`: `Email address` · `Google/Apple account ID` · `Payment identifiers` · `Technical identifiers, such as IP address, ID for advertisers (IDFA), or other IDs`.

**The architectural claim, explained in non-technical terms** `[observed]`:
> "Anonymous Mode ensures that no single party that is processing user data for an Anonymous Mode Account has complete information on both who you are, and what you are trying to access."

Then the named technology `[observed]`: "Anonymous Mode uses Oblivious HTTP (OHTTP) – a cutting-edge protocol that prevents anyone, including Flo, from linking your identity to your health data. We've also integrated post-quantum cryptography (PQC)…" With a **two-panel before/after comparison** captioned in plain language:
- Without OHTTP: "Flo can view: Your IP address, device info, and health data together"
- With OHTTP: "The relay acts as a privacy barrier. Cloudflare can view who you are, but not your health data. Flo can view your health data, but does not know who it belongs to."

**That two-line contrast is the best piece of technical-concept copy in the batch.** It names the third party (Cloudflare), splits the knowledge between two parties, and uses the same sentence shape twice so the reader can diff them. A cryptographic relay explained in 34 words with no analogy and no jargon.

**Open-sourcing disclosed, with a stated rationale** `[observed]`: "The code behind Anonymous Mode is publicly available… Our mission is for all women to feel safe and confident when tracking their health, **whether or not they choose Flo**. That's why we open-sourced the code, giving other health technology companies the opportunity to leverage the investments we've made."

A competitor-benefiting decision, explained by reference to users who are not customers.

**Encryption explained before it is named** `[observed]`, Privacy Portal: "Encryption is a way of turning your data into unreadable code, like locking it in a digital safe. Only someone with the correct key can unlock it and read what's inside." *Then* the standards: `AES-256` at rest, `TLS 1.3` in transit, "the same standards used by banks and government agencies." Concept → analogy → specification → benchmark. The privacy-FAQ version adds the vivid image: "they would only be able to see a scrambled, illegible mess."

**Rights stated as five plain-language actions** `[observed]`: `Access your data` · `Delete your data` · `Control data collection` · `Export your data` · `Use Anonymous Mode`. Each with a one-line explanation. The deletion line is unusually final: "Once deleted, it's gone. We can't recover it, and neither can anyone else." Two clauses of irreversibility where most policies would say "in accordance with our retention schedule".

**GDPR applied universally, and said plainly** `[observed]`: "So we apply GDPR rights to all our users, no matter where you live." Plus the jurisdictional explanation and the editorial flourish "The GDPR is widely regarded as the world's strictest privacy law."

**Research data use** `[observed]`: "We may share aggregated, anonymized, or de-identified health data with research institutions to help advance scientific research on female health or for statistical purposes. This data cannot be used to identify you and therefore is not considered personal data." Three-tier anonymisation vocabulary (`aggregated, anonymized, or de-identified`), and the legal consequence stated.

**Business model disclosed** `[observed]`: "Our business generates revenue from subscriptions only… The only money we make is from when you pay us for enhanced features and subscription products." The same trust device Ada uses.

**Payment identity** `[observed]`: `Can you identify me by my Flo Premium subscription payment details?` → "No… When you use Anonymous Mode, we don't store any data that could identify you directly (including payment information) on our servers." The question a sceptical user would actually ask, answered directly.

**Named accountable humans** `[observed]`: `Sue Khan` (VP of Privacy & Data Protection Officer, CIPP/E), `Laure Lydon` (VP of Security), `Roman Bugaev` (CTO), `Tsimafei Savitski` (Chief Legal & Compliance Officer), plus a `Privacy & Security Advisory Board` launched 2023 with external experts. Photographs, titles, credentials and awards. **Putting faces and names on the privacy function** is the trust move; the DPO is quoted on two separate pages.

## T14 Voice, tone & inclusive language

**Priority section.**

### How Flo refers to its users — the central finding

**Flo's default is `women`. Inclusive constructions appear, but as additions to `women`, not replacements for it, and their distribution is surface-dependent.** `[observed]`

| Surface | Phrase used |
|---|---|
| Home headline | `the world's #1 women's health app` |
| Home subhead | `Over 420 million people` |
| Home, social proof | `77 million women choose Flo every month` |
| Home, Pass It On | `1 billion women in underserved communities` |
| Tracking cycle | `420 million women and people who have periods trust Flo` |
| Tracking cycle | `No two women's cycles are the same` |
| Symptom Checker | `Chosen by over 420 million people worldwide` |
| Symptom Checker | `why women love Flo` |
| Anonymous Mode | `everyone deserves to understand their body better` |
| Anonymous Mode | `why women love Flo` |
| Medical expertise | `how women and people who menstruate engage with their health` |
| Medical expertise | `destigmatize issues affecting women and people who menstruate` |
| Medical expertise | `all women to feel safe and confident` (open-source rationale) |
| Privacy Portal | `women's health technology`, `femtech` |
| Nav / Health Library | `LGBTQ+` as a top-level category |

**The pattern.** Where Flo is making a **scale or market claim**, it says `women` or the neutral `people`. Where Flo is making a **mission or values claim** — the medical-expertise guiding principles, specifically — it says `women and people who menstruate`. The additive construction appears twice on one page and is largely confined to that page. On the tracking-cycle page the variant is `women and people who have periods`. Two different additive formulations, neither used consistently.

`everyone deserves to understand their body better` (Anonymous Mode, twice) is the one fully neutral values statement, and notably it appears on the **privacy** page — where the risk being addressed (being identified by health data) is one that bears especially heavily on people whose gender, pregnancy status or reproductive choices could expose them.

**The `LGBTQ+` Health Library category** is a structural inclusion commitment that outweighs any individual sentence: a top-level nav category, footer-linked, not a tag. Flo also names `gender-affirming care` nowhere observed, and the `Being a mom` category is unmodified.

**Assessment, stated factually.** Flo's inclusive language is **real but inconsistent and unevenly distributed**. It is strongest in the institutional/medical register and weakest in the marketing register. `women's health app` is the headline; `people who menstruate` is the footnote. This is the direct contrast with Clue (file 109), whose tagline `For everyone with a cycle` sits in the hero and whose inclusion is load-bearing rather than additive. Recording the difference rather than ranking it: Flo's positioning is explicitly `women's health` as a category claim, and the additive constructions are layered onto that base.

### Sensitive-topic handling

**Fertility.** Consistently hope-bounded rather than outcome-promising `[observed]`: "maximize your chances of a positive pregnancy test every cycle" — `maximize your chances`, not "get pregnant". "Get daily conception tips from our experts and learn how to read your body's fertility signals." The framing is **skill acquisition** (learning to read signals), not result delivery.

The one exception `[observed]`: a testimonial quoted on the home page ends "Now I'm pregnant!" — the strongest outcome claim on the site, placed in a user's voice with attribution (`Kristen DuVal, App Store review`). Same disowning-by-attribution device Ada uses, though without Ada's explicit disclaimer line.

**Pregnancy loss.** `[absent]` on the pages inspected. No observed copy about miscarriage, stillbirth, termination or how the app handles a pregnancy that ends. The pregnancy-mode description is uniformly forward-looking: "See how your body and baby are changing", "Know when your baby will hit important milestones", "track your baby's growth". **Recorded as an observed absence across eight pages, not as evidence that no such content exists** — `flo.health/pregnancy` and the help centre were not harvested, and Clue's equivalent handling (`How can I track a miscarriage or abortion?` as a help article) shows what the artefact would look like if present.

**Diagnosis delay** is named as an injustice, with citations `[observed]`: "getting a diagnosis can take up to 12 years", "Endometriosis and polycystic ovary syndrome affect one in 10 of us, but getting a diagnosis can take years." `one in 10 of us` — first-person-plural inclusion of the writer in the affected group.

**Sex.** Matter-of-fact and normalising `[observed]`: "It's totally normal to want sex more (or less!) at certain times in your cycle" — the parenthetical `(or less!)` heads off the assumption that more is the desirable direction. Section header `Supercharge your sex life` is the most tabloid phrase on the site and sits oddly beside the clinical register elsewhere.

**Perimenopause** `[observed]`: "Feel confident about your body's changes and find clarity on perimenopause symptoms with support to help you feel good, every step of the way." Three abstract nouns (`confident`, `clarity`, `support`) and no symptom named. Notably vaguer than the cycle-tracking copy, which names bloating and cravings specifically.

**Discharge** is named without euphemism `[observed]`, as a Health Assistant example topic.

**Crisis signposting: `[absent]`.** No mental-health, self-harm, domestic-abuse or reproductive-coercion signposting found on any of the eight pages inspected. Flo's Secret Chats is a peer community and its moderation model is not described publicly on these pages. For a product whose privacy copy explicitly contemplates users fearing identification ("without fearing for their safety"), the absence of any linked support resource on the privacy pages is a recordable gap — the copy names the fear and offers a technical control, but no human route.

### Register and craft

**Person.** Second person for the user, first-person plural for the company (`We'll teach them`, `Our clever tech`, `we don't ask for or log`). The company is a visible actor including in adverse copy ("we will no longer be able to recover…", "our support team won't be able to help you").

**Contractions** used freely throughout, including in privacy copy (`we don't ask`, `it won't be possible`, `That's why`). The only register that drops them is the certification descriptions.

**Em-dashes and parentheticals** carry the hedging: `(or less!)`, `(aka the pill)`, `— whether you switch to Anonymous Mode or not`.

**Bold used inside body copy for emphasis**, heavily, in the privacy explanations: "**access to the Flo app without sharing any information that identifies you**", "**it won't be possible to satisfy that request**". The key claim is bolded so a skimmer cannot miss it.

**`Remember:`** used twice as a paragraph opener in Anonymous Mode, both times to restate user control after describing a limitation. Same placement logic as Ada's `Remember,`.

**Exclamation marks** are rare but present — `(not even us!)`, `(or less!)`. Used only inside parentheses, never in a main clause. A defensible rationing.

**Numbers as trust devices**, all footnoted: `420 million`, `77 million`, `7 million 5-star ratings`, `4.8/5` / `4.7/5`, `90%`, `71%`, `70+`, `120+`, `200+ senior engineers`, `one in 10`, `12 years`.

**Handwritten-style CTA graphics** `[observed]` — several pages carry an SVG alt-titled `Handwritten CTA` near the download buttons. A deliberate informality device rendered as image, therefore **invisible to screen readers and untranslatable**. Recorded as an accessibility and localisation cost of a tone decision.

### Accessibility

**Flo publishes a full accessibility statement** `[observed]`, covering four products (`flo.health`, `app.flo.health`, iOS app, Android app).

- **Conformance stated honestly**: "Our digital products are **partially conformant** with WCAG 2.2 level AA. Partially conformant means that some parts of the content do not fully meet the requirements of the standard currently, but we are constantly working towards improving our digital accessibility stance." Targets **2.2**, a higher bar than Nike's 2.1.
- **Assessment method disclosed as a weakness**: "We assessed the accessibility of our digital products by **self-evaluation**." A one-line admission that no third party verified it — notable on a company that otherwise leads with third-party ISO certification. The contrast between "independently certified" for privacy and "self-evaluated" for accessibility is visible and unexplained.
- **Organisational measures named**, six bullets, including "Appointing a dedicated accessibility product manager, **reporting directly to the Chief Product Officer**" and "Creating a network of internal accessibility champions". Reporting line disclosed — an unusually specific governance detail.
- **Response SLA**: "We will try to respond to your feedback within five business days", with email and postal address.
- **An offer beyond remediation**: "we might also invite you to share your feedback verbally with our product team to help us build more accessible products." Inviting the complainant into research.
- **Dated and re-reviewed**: "prepared on September 16, 2024, and last reviewed on October 9, 2025." Both dates given.

**Alt-text quality on marketing pages is poor** `[observed]`, and the failure mode is systematic: most images carry title attributes ending in the literal string `title` — `"track cycle and symptoms title"`, `"Feature icons-3 title"`, `"Screen1 title"`, `"HandwrittenCTA2 title"`, `"Group 33322 title"`. This is a CMS field concatenation bug, shipped site-wide. Several images have `alt="Image"` or `alt="image"`.

The exceptions are genuinely good and cluster on **Anonymous Mode**: "Screenshot of the Flo app, showing how Anonymous Mode settings look like", "Screenshot of the Flo app, showing creation of access code within Anonymous Mode." Descriptive, stateful, useful. The page Flo cares most about has the best alt text.

**Negative findings, recorded honestly**
- Expert-count claim varies across four values, two on the same page.
- Alt text contains a shipped `title` suffix bug across most marketing images.
- `Handwritten CTA` graphics carry tone in image form, inaccessible and unlocalisable.
- Accessibility is self-assessed while privacy is third-party certified; the asymmetry is visible and unaddressed.
- No pricing or cancellation terms published despite a persistent `Manage subscription` nav item.
- `PQC` named and expanded but not explained, adjacent to an exemplary OHTTP explanation.
- Two different additive-inclusion formulations (`people who have periods` / `people who menstruate`), neither applied consistently.
- No crisis or support signposting observed on any inspected page, including the privacy pages that explicitly invoke user fear for personal safety.

## Transferable patterns

1. **Explain a privacy architecture with a two-panel diff, same sentence shape both sides.** "Flo can view: your IP address, device info, and health data together" / "Cloudflare can view who you are, but not your health data. Flo can view your health data, but does not know who it belongs to." 34 words, no analogy, one named third party. Reusable for any split-knowledge or tokenisation architecture — directly relevant to payments.
2. **Frame a privacy guarantee as a capability limit on yourself, not a policy commitment.** "it won't be possible to satisfy that request" outperforms "we will refuse such requests", because the user does not have to trust your future behaviour. Condition: only usable where it is architecturally true.
3. **Name a feature limitation as intentional and attribute it to the user's benefit.** "This limitation is an intentional one to ensure you remain anonymous." Converts a support degradation from a defect into evidence the promise is real.
4. **The pre-mortem FAQ.** Sequence an irreversible-action FAQ so that the persuasive questions come first, the decision question sits in the middle, and every cost — feature loss, support loss, data loss, billing impact — comes **after** it and **before** the user can act. Applies to account closure, data deletion, plan downgrade, opting out of a data-sharing programme.
5. **Footnote your social proof.** `420 million people³` where `3. Total app downloads`. Bounding your own headline number to the weaker metric that actually supports it costs nothing in impact and removes the strongest challenge to the claim.
6. **Publish the editorial pipeline, including the legal-review stage.** Flo discloses that content is checked "according to their age, location, and needs" — which is why the Symptom Checker is geofenced. Naming the governance step makes the resulting restriction legible rather than arbitrary.
7. **Put the cancellation route in the persistent nav.** `Manage subscription` beside `Try Flo today`, on every page, unauthenticated. Condition: only a genuine pattern if the flow behind it is equally direct — Flo's terms were not verifiable, so this is offered as an IA pattern, not an endorsed flow.
8. **Publish your own regulatory history as a dated timeline.** Recorded as an observed artefact, with the framing choices (`feedback`, `Learning & Improving`, `to avoid the costs of litigation`) noted alongside it. The transferable element is the *existence and findability* of the disclosure; the framing is a separate judgement each team must make for itself.

## Caveats & gaps

- **No health, cycle, pregnancy or body data was entered, and the Symptom Checker was not started.** All question design, symptom labels, and result copy are `[documented]` from published descriptions. No question string, symptom label or result string is reproduced in this file because none was observed.
- **Help centre not harvested.** `help.flo.health` (Zendesk) holds the task-level article corpus; T11 is consequently thin and rests on five deep links found in marketing FAQs.
- **Pregnancy-loss content unassessed.** `flo.health/pregnancy`, `flo.health/being-a-mom` and the help centre were not opened. The absence recorded in T14 is an absence across eight specific pages.
- **Pricing and cancellation terms not found.** `flo.health/flo-premium` exists in the nav and was not harvested; the subscription manager is behind auth.
- **Privacy Policy, Terms of Use, Cookie Policy and Security at Flo not opened.** T10 rests on the Privacy Portal, the privacy FAQs and the Anonymous Mode FAQ, which are Flo's *explanatory* layer. The binding documents may frame data use differently, and the Anonymous Mode whitepaper (a linked PDF) was not retrieved.
- **The FTC settlement is recorded solely as Flo describes it on its own site.** No independent source was consulted and none is characterised here. Readers of this corpus entry should treat the timeline as the company's account.
- **No Health Library article was read.** The medical-review attribution pattern in T10 is `[documented]` from the editorial-standards page, which describes the byline/reviewer/date/reference furniture, but the rendered article template was not observed. - **In-app UI entirely `[absent]`**: field labels, the 70+ symptom vocabulary, validation, empty states, toasts, the Anonymous Mode switch confirmation flow, and Health Assistant copy.
- **en-locale only.** Flo ships 21 language paths; inclusive-language decisions in gendered languages (de, es, fr, pt, it) are likely to differ materially and would be the more interesting study.
