## T1 Navigation & IA labels

**Global nav — five items, and the ordering is enterprise-sales-shaped** `[observed]`

`Products` · `Solutions` · `Developer` · `Resources` · `News` · `Pricing` ·
`Contact` · `Log in` · `Get started`

`Developer` as a top-level peer of `Products` and `Solutions` — ahead of
`Pricing` — is the tell. Box sells a platform to builders as a primary motion,
not as a footnote.

**`Products` — 12 entries, each with a scope line** `[observed]`. The scope lines
are the artefact:

| Product | Scope line (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| `Overview` | "Intelligent Content Management" |
| `Security & Compliance` | "End-to-end data protection" |
| `Collaboration` | "Securely work together on files" |
| `Workflow Automation` | "AI driven business processes" |
| `No-code Apps` | "Intelligent Apps for any workflow" |
| `Hubs` | "AI-powered content portals" |
| `Box AI` | "Unlock the value of your content" |
| `Box AI Agents` | "Intelligent agents to transform work" |
| `Box Extract` | "Extract structured data at scale" |
| `E-signature` | "Send, track, and manage e-signatures" |
| `Integrations` | "Thousands of connected apps" |
| `Content Platform` | "Build with content APIs" |

Every line is three to six words and most are **adjective-noun compounds rather
than verb phrases** — "AI-powered content portals", "Intelligent Apps for any
workflow", "End-to-end data protection". Compare Dropbox (014), whose equivalent
scope lines are verb triads ("Store, share, and access files across devices").
Box names *what a thing is*; Dropbox names *what you do with it*. That single
grammatical difference is the clearest register signal separating an enterprise
platform from a consumer-rooted product, and it recurs throughout both files.

Note the naming inconsistency inside one menu: four entries carry the `Box`
prefix (`Box AI`, `Box AI Agents`, `Box Extract`) and eight do not
(`Collaboration`, `Hubs`, `E-signature`, `No-code Apps`). `Hubs` and `Box Hubs`
are the same product, named both ways within the same site.

**`Solutions` — 16 industries, three services, two ecosystem entries**
`[observed]`. The industry list is the longest in this corpus and includes
`Federal Government` and `State & Local Government` as *separate* entries, plus
`Life Sciences`, `Law Firms`, `AEC`, and `CPG`. **Segmenting government into two
tiers** is a regulatory decision surfaced as navigation — FedRAMP and
state-procurement buyers have different content needs.

**Support-centre IA is product-shaped, not task-shaped** `[observed]`. The
support home lists **27 product tiles**, each with a paragraph:
`Announcements` · `Release Notes` · `Box Fundamentals` · `Box Admin Tools` ·
`Box AI` · `Box Apps` · `Box Archive` · `Box Automate` · `Box Canvas` ·
`Box Compliance` · `Box Doc Gen` · `Box Drive` · `Box Extract` · `Box Forms` ·
`Box Governance` · `Box Hubs` · `Box MCP Server` · `Box Mobile` · `Box Notes` ·
`Box Platform` · `Box Relay` · `Box Shield` · `Box Shuttle` · `Box Sign` ·
`Box Sync` · `Box Tools` · `Accessibility`.

**This is the inverse of every other product in this set.** Todoist, Craft, and
Dropbox organise help by *what the user is trying to do* (`Sync`, `Share`,
`Plan and Do`); Box organises by *which product they bought*. That is coherent
for an enterprise where entitlements differ per customer, and it is hostile to a
user who doesn't know which Box product owns their problem. The nav's secondary
grouping partly compensates — `Product Guides` · `Troubleshooting & FAQs` ·
`AI Library` · `Integrations` · `Learning` · `Community` · `Account & Billing` —
but those are collapsed menus, and only `Learning`, `Community`, and
`Account & Billing` rendered children to a non-JS fetch.

**`Accessibility` as a product tile in the support IA** `[observed]` is worth
flagging as a positive: accessibility gets a peer-level entry alongside
`Box Shield` and `Box AI`, rather than a footer link. It appears again in T14.

**Article breadcrumb depth is six levels** `[observed]`:
`Box Support > Product Guides > Box Fundamentals > For Users > Collaborating >
Collaborating by Inviting Others > <article>`. Note `For Users` as an explicit
audience fork at level four — the sibling is presumably `For Admins`. **Splitting
the entire guide tree by audience before splitting by topic** is an enterprise
pattern: the same feature has two documentation paths because the two readers can
do different things to it.

**Footer — five columns**: `Company` · `Use Cases` · `Service & Support` ·
`Resources` · `International sites`. `Use Cases` contains SEO-shaped destinations
(`Send Large Files Fast`, `Virtual Data Room`, `Cloud Backup`) and the
`Resources` column leads with `Trust Center` and `Box AI Trust`, then
`GDPR Compliance` and `GxP Validation`. **Four of seven `Resources` links are
compliance artefacts** — for this buyer, trust documentation *is* a resource.

A `Trending topics` block sits below the footer with nine blog links
(`What Is an Electronic Signature?`, `What Is AI?`, `What Is File Encryption?`,
`What Is Collaboration?`) — definitional SEO content in a site-wide slot.

## T2 Value proposition & headline patterns

**Hero — three words and a plus sign** `[observed]`

> Headline: `Content + AI`
> Subhead: `Intelligent content management, secure collaboration, and agentic workflow`

The shortest hero in this corpus, and the most abstract. `Content + AI` names no
task, no product, no outcome, and no user — it is a category equation. The subhead
carries the entire proposition as a **three-item noun-phrase list with no verb**:
intelligent content management / secure collaboration / agentic workflow. No
second person, no promise, no number.

Set this beside the other four heroes in the set:
`Clarity, finally.` (Todoist, emotional outcome) ·
"Things is the award-winning personal task manager…" (Things, definition) ·
`Your space for notes, tasks, and big ideas` (Craft, possessive) ·
`Find, organize, and share your work, all in one place` (Dropbox, task verbs) ·
`Content + AI` (Box, category). **The register gradient from consumer to
enterprise is visible in a single line each**, and Box sits at the far end: the
buyer is a committee, the headline is a positioning statement, and the emotional
work is delegated entirely to the analyst-leader block further down.

**`Intelligent Content Management` is the coined category and it is everywhere**
`[observed]` — nav scope line, hero subhead, product-plank body, support-tile
copy, and the `Overview` page name. Box is not describing a product; it is
naming a market it wants to own. The abbreviation `ICM` does not appear, which is
disciplined — a coined category loses its meaning the moment it becomes an
acronym.

**Feature-plank headlines are imperative verb phrases, and they are much better
than the hero** `[observed]`:

`Collaborate easily and securely` · `Get AI insights from content` ·
`Extract critical data at scale` · `Automate with AI agents` ·
`Protect all your content` · `Build with AI-native APIs` ·
`Power seamless e-signatures`

Seven planks, seven verbs, all imperative. And each body opens with **concrete
document nouns** rather than abstractions:
"Sales presentations, product specs, employee handbooks: Unstructured data is at
the center of how you work." / "lease agreements and finance documents" /
"from detailed contracts to bank statements and **handwritten bills of lading**"
/ "From loan processing to onboarding employees and vendors".

`handwritten bills of lading` is the standout. It is specific enough to be
unfakeable, it names a document type only a logistics buyer would recognise, and
it proves the OCR claim more effectively than any adjective. **Naming the hardest
real document your product handles** is a stronger proof than "any document."
Directly transferable.

`Protect all your content` is the plank that carries the benchmark strength, and
its body is a four-clause compression of the whole security portfolio,
summarised: AI-driven security detecting and mitigating threats in real time;
deep-learning malware detection; alerts about unusual activity; automated
classification and **granular access controls that protect your data at scale**;
and built-in compliance tools for GDPR, HIPAA, and FINRA. Three named regimes in
a marketing sentence — the compliance vocabulary is not quarantined to a trust
page.

**Analyst validation is a named section with three sub-blocks** `[observed]`:
`Named a Leader by independent analyst firms` → `Gartner MQ 2026`, `Forrester`,
`IDC`, each with a one-line reason. Notable that the reasons quote the analysts'
*own axis names* — "based on our 'Ability to Execute' and 'Completeness of
Vision'", "the highest scores possible in 13 criteria such as innovation, vision,
security, AI/ML". **Citing the evaluation criteria, not just the verdict**, is
the enterprise equivalent of Wise's claim-bounding: it tells a procurement reader
what the award actually measured.

**Rotating announcement bar — four messages** `[observed]`, and it is an
event/report/product mix:
`See the full BoxWorks 2026 agenda…` `Register now` ·
`The State of AI in the enterprise: See what sets AI leaders apart and delivers
significant ROI.` `Get report` ·
`Your business lives in content - unleash it with the new Box Agent.`
`Learn more` ·
`Deploy AI agents with confidence. Your content stays protected in Box.`
`Learn more`

`Your business lives in content - unleash it` is the only line on the site with
any lyricism (and it uses a hyphen where an em dash belongs). The fourth bar —
`Deploy AI agents with confidence. Your content stays protected in Box.` — is
the important one: **Box's AI positioning leads with a containment promise**, not
a capability promise. Same instinct as Dropbox's "keeping access and approvals in
place", stated more bluntly.

**Pricing headline** `[observed]`: three H1s render on one page —
`Choose the best plan for your business`,
`AI-powered content management for your business`, and `Platform Add-Ons` —
because three tab panels share the page. A genuine heading-structure defect (see
T14).

**Observation.** Box has the **best and the worst CTA practice in this corpus
simultaneously**. The seven feature planks each carry a bespoke verb-phrase CTA

high-urgency action.

## T4 Onboarding & getting-started

`[partial]`, and the structure is the finding: **Box treats onboarding as
certification, not as a walkthrough.**

**The `Learning` menu is a four-tier education programme** `[observed]`:
`User Courses` · `Admin & Co-Admin Courses` · `Developer Resources` ·
`Browse all Box University Courses` · `Get Certified`.

`Box University` is a named institution with its own domain (`training.box.com`)
and its own status-page component. `Get Certified` is a support-nav item.
**Onboarding is split by role before it is split by task** — the
`Admin & Co-Admin Courses` link even carries a `?render=admin` parameter on the
same section URL, so one section renders two audience views.

This is the far end of a gradient visible across all five products:
Things ships a 10-minute guide, Craft ships 11 videos, Todoist ships an 11-step
article, Dropbox relocates onboarding to `learn.dropbox.com` as "self-guided
courses and live instructor-led training", and Box ships a university with
certification. **The unit of onboarding scales with the unit of purchase.**

**No first-run sequence, wizard, or step language was observed.** `[absent]`

**Migration is treated as a product, not a task** `[observed]`. `Box Shuttle`
gets a support tile with a scope paragraph, summarised: it empowers admins and
co-admins to migrate content into Box at petabyte scale, with built-in tools for
analysis and planning, configuration and simulation, and migration and reporting,
across more than ten source systems. Compare Craft's eleven `Import from <app>`
articles. **At consumer scale migration is eleven articles; at enterprise scale
it is a named product with a three-phase methodology** (`analysis and planning`,
`configuration and simulation`, `migration and reporting`). The phrase
`configuration and simulation` is the enterprise tell — you rehearse the
migration before you run it.

**The deprecation notice is the best onboarding-adjacent copy harvested**
`[observed]`, on the `Box Sync` support tile:

> Summarised: Box will end-of-life Box Sync in December 2026. **There will be
> twelve months of notice before the EOL.** Users should migrate to Box Drive,
> which offers faster performance, enhanced security, and modern collaboration
> features. After the EOL date, Box Sync will no longer receive bug fixes or
> updates. Box has created a **transition toolkit** to help organizations move
> their users to Box Drive before the deadline.

Six elements in one paragraph: the date, **the notice period as a commitment**,
the named replacement, three reasons the replacement is better, the precise
consequence of inaction ("no longer receive bug fixes or updates"), and a named
migration artefact. `There will be twelve months of notice before the EOL` is the
sentence to steal — it is a promise about *communication*, not about the product,
and it is exactly what an enterprise admin planning a change window needs.
Compare Todoist's "`@` also works for now, but is planned to be retired by the end
of 2026," which is the same instinct at a tenth of the scale.

## T5 Form & field labels

`[documented]`, drawn from the sharing articles.

**Share dialog** `[documented]`:
`Share` (entry, reachable by hover, right-click, or `...` ellipsis) ·
`Link Settings` (button) · `Shared Link Settings` (window title) ·
`Require Password` (checkbox) · `Enter a password` (field) ·
`Link Expiration` (group) · `Disable Shared Link on` (checkbox) ·
`Choose Date` (box) · `Save` · `Sharing` (sidebar tab) ·
`End Collaboration` (the option offered when a co-owner tries to delete a root
folder).

**`Disable Shared Link on` is a better label than Dropbox's `Expiration`** — it
names the *action the system will take* and the preposition sets up the date
field grammatically, so the control reads as a sentence: "Disable Shared Link on
[date]". Compare `Expiration: On`.

**Admin settings paths are given in full** `[documented]`, and they are long:
`Admin Console > Enterprise Settings > Content & Sharing > Collaborating on Content` ·
`Admin Console > Enterprise Settings > Mobile`.
Named admin toggles quoted verbatim:
`"Preview only" collaborators can save files for offline use` ·
`Hide collaborators and their activity from non-owners` ·
`Restrict Content Creation` · `Restrict Invitations`.

`"Preview only" collaborators can save files for offline use` is a **scare-quoted
role reference inside a toggle label** — the admin is being told which of the
seven roles this affects, using an informal grouping (`"Preview only"`) that is
not itself one of the seven names. Helpful and slightly loose.

**Password requirements, stated as a rule list** `[documented]`:

> - Must be at least 8 characters long
> - Must contain at least one: Upper-case letter / Numeral / Special character
>   (any non-alpha-numeric character except for > and < symbols and [space])
> - **Cannot only be one type of character.** For example, a password cannot only
>   be upper-case letters

Three observations. The **exclusion set is enumerated precisely** (`> and <` and
space are excluded from "special character") rather than left to be discovered at
validation. The third rule is a **compositional constraint expressed in plain
language plus an example** — "cannot only be one type of character… a password
cannot only be upper-case letters" — which is a genuinely hard rule to state and
Box states it twice, abstractly then concretely. And the whole list is published
*before* the user reaches the field, in the help article, rather than only as
inline validation.

**Custom-URL constraints, with the rationale** `[documented]`: the custom ending
"must be between twelve and thirty characters, and can use letters, numbers, and
hyphens", is "intended to make content readily accessible to large groups of
users… using a customized URL that is easy to remember", and is **Business and
Enterprise only**. Then the `Important` warning (see T10).

**A named visual indicator** `[documented]`: a link with an expiry date displays
"a small red clock icon" throughout the interface. The icon has an asset name
(`icon_badge_shared_link_expiration_near.png`) implying a *near-expiry* state
distinct from *has-expiry* — a temporal state in the badge itself, though the
prose describes only one.

**Three things make this the best status IA in the corpus.**

**1. `Governance (Retention)` and `Governance (Legal Holds)` are separate
components.** So are the three `Box Shield` sub-functions
(`Threat Detection`, `Virus Detection`, `Auto Classification`). The
disambiguating parentheticals mean a compliance officer can tell whether the
*specific* control they depend on is degraded, rather than reading "Governance:
Degraded" and having to call support. **Decomposing a compliance product into its
individually-monitorable obligations** is the transferable move — it is the
status-page equivalent of a permission matrix.

**2. `Accessible Site (a.box.com)` is a monitored component.** Box operates a
separate accessible interface at its own subdomain and reports its health as a
first-class service alongside `Search` and `Preview`. An accessibility surface
that can be *seen to be down* is an accessibility surface someone is accountable
for. Most vendors do not have this to report. Alongside `Accessibility` as a
support-centre product tile (T1), this is a genuine, structural commitment rather
than a statement. (The architectural trade-off — a separate site rather than one
accessible site — is a real one and is noted in T14.)

**3. `Box Zones` is a status group whose components are countries.** Twelve
regions, including the compound entries `Germany/UK` and `Japan/Singapore`
(paired-region storage products). A customer with a data-residency obligation can
check the health of *their jurisdiction*. **Sovereignty as a monitorable
component** — the only instance in this corpus.

Note also `Case Submission Workflow` and `Blog Posts and Knowledge Articles` as
monitored components: Box reports the health of its own *support machinery*, so a
customer who can't file a ticket can find out why.

### The severity taxonomy is published, quantified, and versioned `[observed]`

Four levels, each defined by a **measured failure-rate threshold**:

| Level | Definition (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| `Outage` | "Component/Service is unavailable and no workaround is available." |
| `Critical` | "Component/Service is severely degraded with monitoring indicating **>50% failure rate** or impact to successful throughput." |
| `Medium` | "…**25-50% failure rate**…" |
| `Minor` | "…**<25% failure rate**…" |

**Publishing the numeric boundaries between severity levels** removes the
vendor's discretion from the user's view of an incident. A customer can look at
"Critical" and know it means more than half of attempts are failing. Almost
nothing else in this corpus commits a vendor to a number in this way.

And the taxonomy carries **its own change log, in a footnote**:

> "*Starting in 2026, Major level impact will now be referred to as Medium,
> reflected in the chart above and defined below.*"

**A severity level was renamed and the rename is disclosed in the document that
defines it**, so a reader with an older runbook can reconcile. Versioning your
own vocabulary in place is a rare and very high-value practice.

Component states are a separate, smaller set of three, each with its colour and
its examples:

> `Operational` (green dot) — online and functioning within expected norms.
> **"Sometimes we may still be in an active investigation or nearing the final
> stages and will indicate the service is operational."**
> `Degraded` (orange/yellow dot) — "not performing at the levels we expect.
> Examples include higher than expected latency loading the Box Web Application
> or extended intervals to receive new events/changes in our desktop clients."
> `Outage` (red dot) — "may be experiencing a full or partial outage, preventing
> customers from completing their tasks or accessing critical components."

The `Operational` caveat is the most honest sentence on the page: **green can
coexist with an open investigation**, and Box says so rather than letting users
infer that green means nothing is happening. `Degraded` is defined by *user-facing
symptoms* (latency, delayed events) rather than by internal metrics.

Note that the status page's own legend renders five states —
`Operational` · `Degraded Performance` · `Partial Outage` · `Major Outage` ·
`Maintenance` — which are **Atlassian Statuspage's defaults, not Box's three**.
So the documented vocabulary (`Operational` / `Degraded` / `Outage`) and the
rendered vocabulary (five Statuspage states) do not match. A real inconsistency
between policy and platform.

### The four-stage incident lifecycle `[observed]`

| Stage | Definition (summarised) |
|---|---|
| `Investigating` | Most events begin on initial notice of disruption; the status stays here while Box determines what led to the current state and identifies an action plan to restore availability |
| `Identified` | Entered **as soon as a proximal cause is understood**; Box moves to address the problem and ensure the action prevents recurrence |
| `Monitoring` | Entered when remediation is complete and analysis affirms services are back to expected levels; may be **extended** to observe impact across multiple timelines |
| `Resolved` | "A designation that the customer experience has returned to expected levels **based on the results observed during the monitoring period**" |

`proximal cause` rather than `root cause` at the `Identified` stage is a precise
and deliberate distinction — you can act on a proximal cause before you
understand the root one, and conflating them is how status pages end up claiming
premature understanding. Root cause is handled separately, later (see below).

`Resolved` is defined **by the evidence**, not by the fix: it is a designation
based on what was observed during monitoring. That prevents "Resolved" from
meaning "we pushed something."

**The retroactive-post exception is disclosed** `[observed]`:

> Summarised: sometimes Box publishes an update after an issue is already
> resolved — for example when service is restored before an initial notice can be
> issued. **For these retroactive posts no notifications are sent, because there
> is no ongoing incident to communicate in real time.**

Explaining *why a notification did not arrive* is exactly the kind of
meta-disclosure that prevents a subscriber concluding the alerting is broken.

### Commitments, stated as numbers `[observed]`

- "Our goal is to update the Status Site **within 60 minutes from the start of
  impact**, though some event notifications may stretch beyond this goal."
- "our objective is to provide regular updates -- **at least every 30-60 minutes
  or at the next significant change in status.**"
- Updates "include any known impact to the customer experience, including the
  affected service(s) and approximate times of impact. Where feasible, we share
  additional information about potential workarounds, remediation progress/
  actions, and **estimated time to recovery**."

Three numeric commitments, each **immediately bounded** — "though some event
notifications may stretch beyond this goal", "at the next significant change",
"Where feasible". This is the Wise claim-and-bound construction applied to
operational promises: the number is stated, then the exception is admitted in the
same sentence, so the commitment survives being missed.

### The notification matrix `[observed]` — the most unusual artefact here

A three-row × four-column table mapping **which components get a status post at
which severity**:

- **Tier 1** (`Login (Web/SSO)`, `Files page`, `Uploads`, `Downloads`,
  `Content API`, `Box AI`, `Box Notes`, `Box Sign`): posted at
  `Outage` / `Critical` / `Medium` / `Minor` — **all four**
- **Tier 2** (~40 components: `Admin Console: User Management`, `Box Drive`,
  `Preview`, `Search`, `Shared Links`, `Governance`, integrations, etc.): posted
  at `Outage` / `Critical` / `Medium`, **not `Minor`**
- **Tier 3** (`Admin Console: Settings`, `Box.com Marketing Website`,
  `Box Embed`, `Box Zones`, `Canvas`, `Comments & Tasks`, `Watermarking`,
  `Developer Console`, etc.): posted at `Outage` / `Critical` only

**Box publishes the rule that determines when it will and will not tell you
something is broken.** A customer can look up their critical component and learn
the exact severity at which silence becomes possible. The whole table is then
qualified:

> "*This chart represents a guideline. Our response times and decisions to
> provide notification on the Status Site may not always exactly follow this
> matrix.*"

Publishing a notification policy is rare; publishing its fallibility in the same
breath is rarer. Note the internal inconsistency: `Login (Web/SSO)` is Tier 1 and
posted at every severity, while `Login: Mobile`, `Login: mweb`,
`Login: EMM`, and `Login:(OAuth, JWT)` are Tier 2, and `Login: accessible site`
is **Tier 3** — the accessible site's login gets the *weakest* notification
commitment of the six login paths. Given the `Accessible Site (a.box.com)`
component's prominence elsewhere, that is a defensible capacity decision and an
uncomfortable one to have published.

### Monitoring methodology, explained to customers `[observed]`

Two named metrics — "the availability of our service" (site uptime, familiar to
`Premier` customers via SLA) and "a more holistic customer experience
measurement" — with the relationship stated plainly:
**"Simply said, our tracking extends beyond SLA commitments."**

Then `synthetic monitoring` is defined in a footnote, in plain language:

> Summarised: a form of website monitoring using scripted actions in an emulated
> browser to imitate **key customer journeys such as logins or shared link
> previews**, executed at one-minute intervals from various internal and external
> points, to identify whether Box responds appropriately from a variety of
> geographic locations.

**Defining your observability stack for a customer audience**, including the
distinction between synthetic checks and real-user transaction data, and stating
what each is good for. "These checks give us leading indicators of issues or
degradation that may be occurring but have yet to impact our customers" — a
vendor explaining that it tries to catch problems before they are incidents.

### Post-incident `[observed]`

> Summarised: immediately after remediation, Engineering commences a postmortem
> review examining the full event timeline, validation of root cause, and
> identified defect remediations **and owners**. The output is posted back to the
> related Status Site post as soon as it is available.

`and owners` is the detail that makes it credible — a postmortem that assigns
names. And the output goes **back onto the original incident post**, so the
incident record accumulates rather than the RCA living elsewhere.

**`Known Product Issues` is maintained as a separate public list** `[observed]`,
linked from the top of the status page: "We also maintain a list of Known Product
Issues separate from this site here." Distinguishing *incidents* (transient,
status page) from *known issues* (persistent, defect register) — and telling the
user both exist and where each lives — is a clean separation that Todoist also
makes and Craft and Things do not.

### Application state vocabulary `[absent]`

Unlike Dropbox, Box publishes **no sync-icon or file-state table** in the
harvested set. The `Box Drive` and `Box Sync` articles were not opened, and
`Box Sync` is in any case EOL. Box's state language is entirely
*service-and-incident* shaped, not *file* shaped — which is consistent with a
product whose primary interface is the web app rather than a synced folder.

## T7 Error, failure & recovery

`[partial]`. No error-string article equivalent to Dropbox's was harvested, and
the `Troubleshooting & FAQs` menu did not render children. What is observable is
**failure-prevention and support-process language**, which is where Box's
strength actually lies.

**The exposure-prevention article is the best "stop the mistake" artefact in this
corpus** `[observed]`. Its framing sentence sets up the entire problem:

> "However, when you add new content, or when you share, move, or copy content,
> **the permission to access those items might change, often to a less
> restrictive setting.**"

`often to a less restrictive setting` is the whole risk in six words — permission
drift is directional, and it drifts toward exposure. Box then states the copy's
own purpose, and the second half of the sentence is the notable part:

> Summarised: to help you avoid inadvertently disclosing sensitive information to
> a wider audience than you intend, and to let you quickly understand who you are
> actually collaborating with, Box displays a series of indicators and warnings —
> so you can identify in advance whether you've specified an appropriate
> destination or collaborator, **and also as an opportunity to change your mind
> before you complete a file or folder action.**

**`an opportunity to change your mind`** is the phrase to steal. It reframes a
warning from an obstacle into an affordance, and it names the user's actual
psychological state at that moment. Most confirmation copy is written as though
the user needs to be stopped; this is written as though the user needs to be
*informed in time*.

**Three named warning surfaces** `[documented]`:

1. **The `Sharing` tab in the right-hand sidebar** shows indicators when content
   "is available through an open shared link" or "is externally collaborated."
   Stated purpose: "Looking at the Sharing tab prevents you from unknowingly
   adding content – either by uploading or creation – to a folder that permits
   the general public to access to your files or documents." *(Note the typo:
   "permits the general public to access to your files" — a doubled preposition.)*
2. **Move/copy warnings** fire "If the destination folder, **or any of its parent
   folders**, has a public link enabled" or "allows external collaborators." The
   bolded `or any of its parent folders` is the crucial scope: **exposure is
   inherited from ancestors the user may never have seen.**
3. **The share dialog** carries four simultaneous signals for external
   recipients: a different coloured background for external names, the specific
   external domain, **"images of each person you invite, to help ensure you don't
   send an invitation to a different individual with the same name as your
   intended recipient"**, and a text advisory at the bottom.

The avatar rationale is the detail worth recording. In an enterprise directory
with thousands of people, **name collision is a real and silent data-leak vector**,
and Box states that the photo exists specifically to prevent it. Naming the
failure mode a UI element is designed to prevent, inside the help article, is
what turns decoration into a control.

**The recovery guidance for a warning the user doesn't understand** `[observed]`:

> Summarised: if you think you received one of these messages in error, you may
> have overlooked the permission level of a parent folder. If you don't own the
> parent folder, you may need to ask whoever does to examine the folder for
> public links or external collaborators.

**Telling the user the warning is probably right and where to look** — and
routing the social step ("ask whoever owns it") when the technical step is
unavailable. Same pattern as Dropbox's permission-conflict article.

**The inheritance exception, stated as a `Note`** `[observed]`:

> Summarised: if a subfolder has been shared externally, the parent folder does
> **not** automatically inherit the security indicator. The indicator applies at
> the folder level where the external collaborator was added, and is inherited by
> that folder's children.

So the warning system inherits **downward only**. A user standing in a parent
folder gets no signal that a child is exposed. Box discloses the limit of its own
safety mechanism — which is both honest and a genuine gap a content designer
should flag.

**Support-process language is unusually well specified** `[observed]`, with named
articles for each policy:
`Product Support Priority Definitions` · `Phone Support at Box` ·
`Billing Support at Box` · `Box Product Support` ·
`Our Approach to Feature/Component Version Numbering` ·
`Our approach to announcing releases` ·
`Information on our Terms of Service updates` ·
`Box Pulse – How to submit product feedback` · `Known Product Issues`.

**`Our Approach to …` is a named article genre** — three articles share the
construction (`Our Approach to Incident Response and Communications`,
`Our Approach to Feature/Component Version Numbering`,
`Our approach to announcing releases`). A **documented-policy genre with a
consistent title shape**, so an enterprise reader can find "how Box behaves about
X" as a class. That is a real IA invention and it is the reason the
incident-response content in T6 exists at all. (Note the casing inconsistency
within the genre: two `Approach`, one `approach`.)

**One documented recovery affordance** `[documented]`: when a co-owner attempts
to delete a root-level folder — which they cannot do — "they receive an option to
**End Collaboration**." A blocked destructive action that offers the *adjacent
legitimate action* instead of just refusing. The user's likely intent ("I want
this out of my view") is served without the impermissible operation.

## T9 Notifications & system messages

`[observed]` and `[documented]`.

**Status subscription — four channels, each with a distinct event set**
`[observed]`, and Box carries one channel Dropbox does not:

> Email: "whenever Box **creates**, **updates** or **resolves** an incident."
> SMS: "whenever Box **creates** or **resolves** an incident."
> **Webhook: "whenever Box creates an incident, updates an incident, resolves an

> Plus Atom/RSS and a link to the support site.

The webhook tier gets a **fourth event type — `changes a component status`** —
that no human-facing channel receives. **Machine subscribers get finer-grained

## T10 Disclosures, legal & compliance

**Priority section, and the reason Box is in this corpus.**

### Seven collaborator roles — the deepest permission vocabulary harvested

`[documented]`. Box's roles, in the order the matrix presents them:

`Co-owner` · `Editor` · `Viewer Uploader` · `Previewer Uploader` · `Viewer` ·
`Previewer` · `Uploader` — plus `owner` as an eighth, non-assignable level.

**The generative structure is the finding.** Box has two orthogonal read levels
(`Preview` = look at it in the viewer; `View` = preview **plus download**) and one
write level (`Upload`), and it **composes them**:

| Role | Read | Write |
|---|---|---|
| `Previewer` | preview only | — |
| `Viewer` | preview + download | — |
| `Uploader` | **none** (sees item names only) | upload |
| `Previewer Uploader` | preview only | upload |
| `Viewer Uploader` | preview + download | upload |

Five of the seven roles are **points on a 2×3 grid**, and the names are literally
the grid coordinates concatenated. `Editor` and `Co-owner` sit above as
full-access levels differing only in folder-settings authority.

That is a genuine content-design achievement: **a seven-level permission ladder
whose names are self-documenting because they are compositional.** A user who
learns two words (`Preview`, `View`) and one word (`Upload`) can decode five role
names without a table. Compare Dropbox's `Can edit` / `Can view` — simpler, and
incapable of expressing "can put files in but cannot read them", which is a real
enterprise requirement (evidence drop-boxes, submission folders, HR intake).

**`Uploader` is the most interesting single role** `[documented]`:

> "Limited write access. Uploaders can upload content and see item names in a
> folder, but cannot download or view content."

**Write-without-read.** The role exists specifically for the intake case, and the
definition names the exact residual visibility (`see item names`) so the user
knows what an Uploader *can* infer. Naming the leak in the definition of the
permission is the kind of precision that a security reviewer needs.

**Role descriptions are structured positive-then-negative, consistently**
`[documented]`:

> `Editor`: "Full read/write access to a folder or file. Editors can view,
> download, upload, edit, delete, copy, move, rename, generate and edit shared
> links, make comments, assign tasks, create tags, and invite or remove
> collaborators. **Editors cannot copy, delete, or move root-level folders.**"
> `Viewer`: "Read access to a folder or file. Viewers can preview, download, make
> comments, and generate shared links. **Viewers cannot add tags, invite new
> collaborators, edit shared links, upload, edit files, or delete items.**"
> `Previewer`: "Limited read access. Previewers can only preview items using the
> integrated content viewer. They can use the browser's search function to search
> text in content, but cannot use the file-specific search. **Previewers cannot
> select and copy text, share, upload, edit, or delete content.**"
> `Co-owner`: "All the read/write access of an Editor, plus the ability to change
> some advanced folder settings. **Co-owners cannot change the owner of a
> folder.**"

Every definition is: **one-line summary → enumerated permitted verbs →
enumerated forbidden verbs.** The forbidden list is never omitted, and it is
where the real information is. The `Previewer` entry goes further and describes
a *partial* capability — browser search works, in-file search does not — which is
the level of granularity a compliance reviewer actually needs.

### The 34-action × 7-role matrix `[documented]`

Grouped into six labelled sections:
`Viewing & Navigation` · `Content Editing` · `File Management` ·
`Sharing & Links` · `Collaboration` · `Workflows & Advanced`.

Rows worth naming for their precision:
`View Item List in Folder` (distinct from `Preview` — this is what makes
`Uploader` expressible) · `View Tags` · `View Metadata` ·
`View / Export Content Insights` · `View Recent User Activity in Preview Header` ·
`Edit Folder Settings` · `Create Annotations` / `Delete Annotations` ·
`Lock / Unlock File` · `Mark for Offline (Box Drive)` /
`Mark for Offline (Box Mobile)` — **the same capability split across two clients
because the answers differ** · `Send View-Only Links` ·
`Set Shared Link Password` · `File Request Link` · `Invite People` ·
`Set a Collaborator's Level of Access` · `Restrict Invitations` ·
`Build Relay Workflow` · `Create Box Sign Signature Requests` ·
`Create / Delete Folder Lock (API only)`.

**`Mark for Offline (Box Drive)` vs `Mark for Offline (Box Mobile)` is the detail
that proves the matrix is real**: `Previewer` can mark for offline on mobile (with
an admin toggle) but not on desktop. Rather than flattening to one row, Box
splits it and footnotes the condition.

### Twelve numbered conditions, grouped by kind `[documented]`

This is the disclosure mechanism, and it is more sophisticated than Dropbox's
four-symbol footnote system. The notes are grouped under **three headings that
classify the *type* of exception**:

**`Role-specific behavior`** — inherent product rules:
1. Comments can be deleted only by the folder owner, co-owner, or the comment's
   creator.
2. Previewers and Previewer Uploaders can create annotations but cannot highlight
   and comment.
3. Annotations can be deleted by owners and co-owners of the containing folder,
   and by the annotation creator.
4. **"Viewers can download a file and re-upload it elsewhere, or use `Ctrl+C` /
   `Ctrl+V` to copy a file to another folder they have access to."**
5. **"Viewer Uploaders can only copy using keyboard shortcuts. The *Copy* option
   does not appear in the context menu."**
6. File Request Link applies only to the enhanced version of File Request.

**`Scope and ownership`** — structural limits:
7. Co-owners and editors can delete sub-folders but **not root-level folders**;
   only owners can. A co-owner attempting it gets `End Collaboration`. Permanent
   deletion from trash depends on the owner's trash settings and on whether the
   content is **retained by a policy**.
8. Co-owners and editors can move content only *within* the collaborated folder —
   with a worked example: invited to Folder A containing Folder B and File C, they
   can move B and C but not A itself.
9. `Box Item` allows a custom scope to be defined.

**`Admin-configurable settings`** — things an administrator can change:
10. Previewers can save files offline on mobile only if
    `"Preview only" collaborators can save files for offline use` is enabled in
    `Admin Console > Enterprise Settings > Mobile`.
11. If Enterprise-level and folder-level permissions are restricted, **editors
    cannot invite collaborators.**
12. If `Hide collaborators and their activity from non-owners` is on, owners and
    co-owners still see file activity but **editors do not**. If
    `Restrict Content Creation` is enabled, only the primary admin and co-admin
    can export access stats reports.

**Notes 4 and 5 are the standout, and they are unusual to the point of being
uncomfortable.** Box documents that a `Viewer` can **circumvent the intent of
the permission** by downloading and re-uploading, and that `Ctrl+C`/`Ctrl+V`
works for roles where the `Copy` context-menu item is deliberately hidden.
The vendor is stating, in its official permission reference, that a control it
ships is **UI-deep rather than enforcement-deep.**

That is the same honesty class as Dropbox's "Disabling downloads… doesn't prevent
people from saving the content using other methods," but applied to a *role
definition* rather than a feature. It is the right thing to publish — a security
reviewer who assumes `Viewer` prevents copying will design a control that fails —
and it is a reminder that **every read permission is a copy permission in
practice.** The grouping under `Role-specific behavior` rather than under a
"limitations" heading slightly under-signals it.

**Note 7's clause "and the content is not retained by a policy"** quietly
subordinates all user permissions to retention governance: a `Box Governance`
policy outranks a co-owner. One clause, and it is the most consequential
permission statement on the page.

### The precedence rule, stated plainly `[documented]`

> "If a collaborator holds multiple permission sets, **the least restrictive set
> takes precedence.** For example, a Viewer who accesses a shared link set to
> allow Edit access gets Edit rights on that item."

**Permission resolution is most-permissive-wins, stated in one sentence with a
worked example that shows the *dangerous* case** — a Viewer becoming an Editor
via a link. Compare Dropbox, which states that link settings and direct
permissions do not interact; Box states that they do, and in which direction. For
anyone designing an enterprise permission model, this single sentence and its
example are the most reusable thing in the file.

**Shared-link behaviour for Viewers is enumerated as four constraints**
`[documented]`, each phrased as a limit:
a Viewer cannot change who can access a shared link; with no existing link, they
can create one only at the owner's default setting; with an existing link, they
can share it only at its current access level (worked example: a Viewer cannot
change "people with the link" to "people in the company"); and then the
precedence rule above. **A capability described entirely through what it cannot
do**, with a concrete example of the boundary.

### Entitlement is disclosed before the matrix `[documented]`

A `Feature availability by plan` section precedes everything:

- `All accounts:` Editor and Viewer roles.
- `Business and Enterprise accounts:` Co-owner, Previewer, Uploader, Viewer
  Uploader, and Previewer Uploader. **"An admin must enable these in
  `Admin Console > Enterprise Settings > Content & Sharing > Collaborating on
  Content`. These additional roles apply only to folders. Single-file
  collaborations support only Editor and Viewer."**
- `Paid plans only:` shared link password protection and auto-expiry are not
  available on free Individual accounts.

**Three separate gates disclosed in one block**: plan tier, admin enablement, and
object type. A reader learns before the table that five of the seven roles may be
unavailable to them for three independent reasons. This is the
`Available for:` badge pattern (Todoist) and the audience-scope sentence pattern
(Dropbox) executed at higher resolution.

And a cross-product interaction is footnoted in the role descriptions: "In Hubs,
if a folder Uploader is invited to a Hub as Viewer, the folder Uploader's
permissions change to Viewer Uploader." **A permission that mutates when an
object is added to another product** — disclosed rather than discovered.

### Shared-link disclosures `[documented]`

Link scope values: `People with the link` · (implied) `People in your company` ·
(implied) collaborators-only. Only `People with the link` is quoted verbatim, and
it is the one that carries the warnings.

> `Important`: "If the shared link is available with **People with the link**
> scope, then the linked folder or file is **publicly accessible**. Custom URL is
> appropriate for public-facing materials such as product documentation or
> marketing materials, and is **not intended for the secure sharing of sensitive
> content**."

A feature (memorable custom URLs) whose own documentation **tells you what not to
use it for**, with examples of appropriate use. `not intended for` is the right
register — it is guidance, not a prohibition Box can enforce.

Other disclosures on the same page, each a real edge case:
- Password protection: "Only owners, co-owners, and editors can set this
  password." And: "If you create a password-protected shared link to a folder,
  **Box Notes in that folder may be inaccessible**" — a named product interaction
  failure.
- Custom URLs are **lost when content moves between Enterprises**, while the
  scrambled-character links survive.
- Direct links cannot be created for `.dll`, `.exe`, `.jar`, and `.msi` —
  "It is important to be aware that these file types can be harmful if downloaded
  from an untrusted source." **A blocked capability explained by the threat it
  prevents**, so the restriction reads as protection rather than arbitrariness.
- Link redirection after an org merger "requires the user's permission" — consent
  for a URL change the user did not cause.
- "Depending on admin settings, you may be unable to create or edit shared link
  settings." A conditional-capability caveat repeated across the article.

### Compliance and plan disclosure `[observed]`

**Eight plan tiers plus four platform add-ons**, the most complex pricing surface
in this corpus:
`Individual` (Free) · `Personal Pro` ($10/mo) · `Business Starter` ($5/user/mo,
min 3) · `Business` ($15) · `Business Plus` ($25) · `Enterprise` ($35) ·
`Enterprise Plus` ($50) · `Enterprise Advanced` (quote), plus
`Core APIs`, `AI Units`, `Document Generation APIs`, `Sign APIs`.

**Tier names are consistent across the pricing page, the permission article
("Business and Enterprise accounts", "free Individual accounts"), and the
comparison matrix.** After Dropbox's nine names and Craft's six, Box's
consistency is notable — and it is the product with the most tiers. The plan
vocabulary survives because the *families* (`Business*`, `Enterprise*`) are
stable even as members are added.

**Compliance rows in the matrix are granular and conditional** `[observed]`:
`SOC 1/SOC 2/SOC 3 compliance` · `PCI DSS` · `HIPAA/HITECH-eligible` ·
`FedRAMP Moderate` · `FedRAMP High` (`Additional Cost`) ·
`GxP compliance with 21 CFR Part 11` (`Additional Cost`) ·
`Native security classifications` · `Classification-based access policies` ·
`Threat detection rules and alerts` (all three `$ Via Shield Add On` on lower
tiers) · `Compliant Archive` · `Device trust (advanced mobile requirements)` ·
`Password policy enforcement` · `Data loss protection`.

Three disclosure practices worth naming. (1) **`HIPAA/HITECH-eligible`** — not
"HIPAA compliant". The `-eligible` suffix is the legally accurate form (the
covered entity is compliant; the vendor enables it) and Box uses it in a
comparison-matrix cell where "HIPAA" would have fitted. (2) **`Additional Cost`
and `$ Via Shield Add On` as explicit cell values** rather than blanks or
checkmarks — a capability that exists but costs extra is marked as such, in the
cell. (3) **`Two-factor authentication` has three values** across tiers:
`User opt-in` / `User opt-in` / `Organization setting` — the same feature
differing not in presence but in *who controls it*. That is exactly the
distinction an enterprise buyer cares about and most matrices flatten.

**`AI Units` is a defined, disclosed consumption unit** `[observed]`:

> "AI Units are a new metric to track and manage AI API usage more transparently.
> **Each AI interaction — from querying content to extracting metadata — is
> quantified into discrete units**, enabling you to forecast AI needs and
> optimize budgets with clarity and precision. Annual contract required."

Plus a linked `Box AI Unit consumption table`, a `#` footnote on every row that
consumes them, and an FAQ answer confirming **"There are no rollovers and any
unused quantity in a Platform add-on will expire at the end of the term."**
Naming a consumption unit, publishing its conversion table, and stating the
expiry rule — versus Craft's `credits` and Todoist's undefined `Ramble`. The
transparency claim is backed by an artefact.

**Bandwidth limits are published with numbers and a reason** `[observed]`:

> Summarised: to help prevent excessive usage by any one user, Box may limit
> bandwidth above stated thresholds — shared-link download bandwidth of 10 GB per
> user per month on Individual plans and 2 TB on Business plans (applying to the
> file's owner), and 1 TB per user per month of combined upload/download
> ingress/egress on both (applying to the user who uploads or downloads).

Two different limits, two different **attribution rules** (owner vs actor), and a
link to a `Fair Use Policy`. Stating *whose quota a given transfer consumes* is
the part almost every product omits, and it is the part that determines whether a
shared link can be safely publicised.

**Trust surface** `[observed]`: `Trust Center` and `Box AI Trust` as separate
destinations, `GDPR Compliance` at `/privacyineurope`, `GxP Validation` at
`/gxp`. A dedicated **AI trust page distinct from the general trust centre** is a
2026 marker worth recording — AI governance has become a separable disclosure
obligation.

## T11 Help-centre architecture

**Two help systems in mid-migration, and the migration is disclosed on every
page** `[observed]`:

> "Product Guides are now only available at **docs.box.com**. Check out all the

A persistent site-wide banner linking to a change-explanation article. The legacy

migration in a banner, with a "what's changed" article, and keeping the old URLs

**Article-title grammar — five shapes** `[observed]`:

| Shape | Examples |
|---|---|
| `Understanding <concept>` | `Understanding Collaborator Permission Levels` · `Understanding Item Permissions` · `Understanding Information Barriers` |
| Gerund + object | `Configuring Individual Shared Link Settings` · `Creating Shared Links` · `Securing Shared Links` · `Managing Collaborators` · `Inviting Collaborators` · `Annotating Documents` · `Locking & Unlocking Files` · `Customizing Shared Link Content Ordering` · `Branding Your File Request` |

**`Understanding <concept>` is Box's signature title genre** and it is the

right. Same structural move, two audiences.

**No question-form titles at all**, and no first-person titles, in the harvested
set. All titles are gerunds, nouns, or `Understanding`/`Our Approach` genre
constructions — the most formal title register in this corpus.

**Four observations.**

**The `Box <Noun>` prefix is applied with near-total consistency** across ~20
products — `Box Shield`, `Box Governance`, `Box Zones`, `Box Relay`,
`Box Shuttle`. It makes every product name searchable, trademarkable, and
unambiguous in a procurement document. The cost is that the nav strips the prefix
inconsistently (`Hubs` vs `Box Hubs`, `E-signature` vs `Box Sign`), so the
marketing surface and the support surface name the same thing differently. Note
too that **the product names are almost all common nouns doing metaphorical work**
— a shield, a shuttle, a relay, a canvas, a zone, a hub. The portfolio reads as
infrastructure.

**`Preview` vs `View` is the single highest-value terminology decision in this
file.** Splitting "can look at it" from "can take a copy" is the distinction that
makes five of the seven roles expressible, and it is the distinction Dropbox's
`Can view` collapses. It also maps to a real legal difference: showing a document
and releasing a document are different acts.

**`Box Relay` and `Box Automate` overlap and both are current.** Relay is
"Automate content workflows and collaborative processes with a simple, no-code
builder"; Automate is "makes it easy to design and manage workflows with an
intuitive drag-and-drop builder and automate content-centric processes with AI
Agents." Two products, two support tiles, two status components, near-identical
descriptions. The portfolio has outgrown its own naming.

**`Box Sync` is being retired while `Box Drive` survives** — two products whose
names describe the *same operation* from different angles (the process vs the
place). The surviving name is the spatial one, which is consistent with the rest
of the portfolio (`Zones`, `Hubs`, `Canvas`, `Archive`, `Box` itself). Box's
metaphor is containers, not motion.

## Transferable patterns

1. **Compose permission names from orthogonal capability axes.**
   `Previewer` / `Viewer` / `Uploader` → `Previewer Uploader` /
   `Viewer Uploader`. Two read levels × one write level produces five
   self-documenting roles from three words. A user who learns the atoms can decode
   the compounds without a table. Condition: the axes must be genuinely orthogonal,
   and you must be willing to split `Preview` from `View` (look at it vs take a
   copy) — which is where the expressive power comes from.
2. **Model write-without-read.** `Uploader` — "can upload content and see item
   names in a folder, but cannot download or view content." Evidence drop-boxes,
   submission folders, HR intake, and regulated filings all need this, and a
   two-level view/edit model cannot express it. Name the residual visibility
   (`see item names`) in the definition.
3. **Define every role positive-then-negative, and never omit the negative.**
   Summary → permitted verbs → **forbidden verbs**. The forbidden list is where the
   information is, and its absence is what makes permission documentation
   untrustworthy.
4. **State the precedence rule in one sentence with the dangerous example.**
   "If a collaborator holds multiple permission sets, the least restrictive set
   takes precedence. For example, a Viewer who accesses a shared link set to allow
   Edit access gets Edit rights on that item." Any system with more than one grant
   path needs this sentence, and the example should show the escalation, not the
   benign case.
5. **Group your footnotes by *kind* of exception, with headings.**
   `Role-specific behavior` (inherent) / `Scope and ownership` (structural) /
   `Admin-configurable settings` (variable) — plus an instruction to skip the ones
   that don't apply. The reader learns what class of caveat they are entering
   before reading it. A step beyond Dropbox's symbol-coded footnotes.
6. **Document where your own control is UI-deep rather than enforcement-deep.**
   "Viewers can download a file and re-upload it elsewhere, or use Ctrl+C/Ctrl+V…"
   / "Viewer Uploaders can only copy using keyboard shortcuts. The Copy option does
   not appear in the context menu." Uncomfortable and correct: a reviewer who
   assumes otherwise will build a control that fails.
7. **Publish the numeric thresholds between your severity levels, and version the
   taxonomy in place.** `Critical` = >50% failure rate; `Medium` = 25–50%;
   `Minor` = <25% — plus a footnote recording that `Major` was renamed `Medium` in
   2026. Removes vendor discretion from the user's reading of an incident, and lets
   a reader with an old runbook reconcile.
8. **Publish the rule for when you will *not* tell them.** The three-tier
   notification matrix — which components get a status post at which severity —
   with the qualifier that it is a guideline. Almost nobody does this, and it
   converts silence from a betrayal into a documented policy.
9. **Distinguish `proximal cause` from `root cause` across incident stages.**
   `Identified` needs only a proximal cause; root cause belongs to the postmortem.
   Conflating them is how status pages claim premature understanding. And define
   `Resolved` **by the evidence observed during monitoring**, not by the fix.
10. **Decompose compliance products into individually-monitorable obligations.**
    `Governance (Retention)` and `Governance (Legal Holds)` as separate status
    components; `Box Shield` split into three. A compliance officer can check the
    specific control they depend on. Add data-residency regions as components and
    sovereignty becomes monitorable too.
11. **Grade notification event-sets by channel, including machine channels.**
    Email gets create/update/resolve; SMS gets create/resolve; **webhook also gets
    `changes a component status`**. Machines want every transition; humans want the
    incident.
12. **Frame an exposure warning as "an opportunity to change your mind."**
    Reframes a confirmation from obstacle to affordance and names the user's actual
    state. And scope the warning to ancestors — "If the destination folder, **or any
    of its parent folders**, has a public link enabled" — because inherited exposure
    is the exposure users never see.
13. **Name the failure mode a UI element prevents.** "images of each person you
    invite, to help ensure you don't send an invitation to a different individual
    with the same name as your intended recipient." Name collision is a silent
    data-leak vector in any large directory; saying so turns an avatar into a
    control.
14. **Commit to a notice period, not just a date, when deprecating.** "There will
    be twelve months of notice before the EOL," plus the replacement, three reasons
    for it, the exact consequence of inaction, and a named transition toolkit. The
    notice-period promise is about *communication*, which is what a change-window
    planner needs.
15. **Prove a hard capability with the hardest real artefact.** `handwritten bills
    of lading` beats "any document type." Name the specific document that a
    sceptical buyer would assume breaks your product.
16. **Define your consumption unit, publish its conversion table, and state the
    expiry rule.** `AI Units` — "Each AI interaction… is quantified into discrete
    units", a linked consumption table, and "There are no rollovers." Set against
    Todoist's undefined `Ramble` and Craft's unglossed `credits`, this is what
    transparency about a metered resource actually requires.
17. **State whose quota a transfer consumes.** Shared-link download bandwidth
    charges the *owner*; ingress/egress charges the *actor*. The attribution rule
    determines whether a link can be safely publicised, and almost every product
    omits it.
18. **Establish `Our Approach to <policy>` as a named article genre.** Three
    articles share the construction, so an enterprise reader can find "how this
    vendor behaves about X" as a class. This genre is why the incident-response
    content exists at all.
19. **Counter-example to steal against:** filename alt text on the screenshots
    that carry your warning copy. Box's exposure article describes a four-part
    visual warning system and then shows it as `External_alerts_annotated.png` — the
    actual strings are in images and nowhere in text. Pair with the two-register
    FAQ collision (`?!` beside `FedRAMP High`) as a case study in a content
    organisation whose reference writing is world-class and whose surrounding
    surfaces are unmanaged.

## Caveats & gaps

- **All in-product copy is `[documented]`, not `[observed]`, and crucially the
  in-product *warning strings* were not retrievable at all.** The exposure article
  describes four simultaneous external-sharing signals and shows them as
  screenshots with filename alt text, so T7 can characterise the warning *system*
  but quotes almost none of its *copy*. This is the single largest content gap in
  the file, and it sits on the product's flagged strength. An authenticated pass —
  or an OCR pass over those five images — would be required.
- **T8 is `[absent]`**: no in-product empty-state string was observed or quoted.
- **T7 is partial.** No Box equivalent of Dropbox's verbatim error-string article
  was found, and the `Troubleshooting & FAQs` menu rendered no children to a
  non-JS fetch. The one quoted UI string is `End Collaboration`. Box's error copy
  is genuinely unharvested, not absent.
- **`docs.box.com` — the new canonical Product Guides site — was not harvested.**
  Box migrated its guides there during 2026 and announces it in a site-wide
  banner. The legacy Zendesk articles used here remain live and are content-
  identical for the articles checked, but **the new site's IA, navigation, search,
  and any rewritten copy are unassessed**, and its semantic URLs suggest a
  restructured tree. This is the most important follow-up.
- **9 pages of a very large estate.** Unharvested and likely high-value:
  `Understanding Item Permissions` (the object-level model beneath the role
  model), `Managing Collaborators`, `Securing Shared Links`,
  `Folder and Account Sharing Settings`,
  `Important Admin Considerations for Editable Shared Links`,
  `Understanding Information Barriers`, `Security Control Types`,
  `Known Product Issues`, `Product Support Priority Definitions`,
  `Our Approach to Feature/Component Version Numbering`, and the entire
  `For Admins` branch of the guide tree — which, for a product whose strength is
  enterprise permission language, contains the other half of that language.
- **`www.box.com/security-compliance` and the `Trust Center`
  (`box.com/trust`, `box.com/ai/trust`) were not opened.** T10's compliance
  coverage rests on the pricing matrix, the home-page plank, and the permission
  articles. The authoritative security narrative and the AI-governance disclosures
  are unassessed.
- **Admin Console copy is entirely unobserved.** Named toggles are quoted
  second-hand from user-facing articles (`Hide collaborators and their activity
  from non-owners`, `Restrict Content Creation`,
  `"Preview only" collaborators can save files for offline use`). `Box Shield`,
  `Box Governance`, `Box Keysafe`, and `Box Archive` interfaces — where the
  classification, retention, and legal-hold vocabulary lives — are unharvested.
- **No incident sample.** The status page's 15-day window contained **zero
  incidents**, so while the incident *policy* is exceptionally well documented,
  **no actual Box incident copy was observed** — no `Investigating` body, no
  postmortem, no maintenance notice. The four-stage lifecycle's language in
  practice is unevidenced. Compare Dropbox, where one real incident was visible.
- **Sync/file state vocabulary is `[absent]`.** Box publishes no equivalent of
  Dropbox's sync-icon table in the harvested set, and `Box Drive` / `Box Sync`
  articles were not opened. T6 is service-state rich and file-state empty.
- **Plan facts** are taken from the comparison matrix where the cards render
  defectively (doubled prices, concatenated strings). Re-verify before citing.
- **Locale coverage unassessed.** 14 marketing locales and 3 support locales are
  declared; only `en-US` was harvested. The 11-locale gap between marketing and
  support is itself a finding worth a dedicated pass for compliance content.
- **Analyst awards and customer quotations** (Gartner, Forrester, IDC; Morgan
  Stanley, Broadcom) are third-party material reproduced by Box. They are
  referenced and summarised, not quoted at length, and are not treated as Box's
  own voice.
- **Public helpfulness ratios** (`123 out of 175`, etc.) are Box's own displayed
  metrics, recorded as observed. They are not independently verifiable and the
  feedback controls are behind a sign-in wall, so the sample is authenticated
  users only.
- **No published design system, content style guide, or voice documentation
  found.** The consistency of the permission-article construction and the
  `Our Approach to` genre implies internal standards for reference and policy
  content specifically; the pricing-page defects suggest none for marketing
  surfaces. T14 is reconstructed from observed copy.
