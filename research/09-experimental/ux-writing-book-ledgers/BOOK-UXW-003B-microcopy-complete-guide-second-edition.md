### `MC2-C02` — the book defines microcopy around an action sequence

- **Locator:** Introduction, printed pages 8–9; PDF pages 6–7.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Microcopy is defined as interface words or phrases directly related to an action: motivation before it, instructions during it, and feedback after it; the label is traced to Joshua Porter's checkout example.
- **Candidate implication:** Treat this as a useful action-relative view over purpose, state, instruction, control, feedback, and recovery rather than as a complete content ontology.
- **Boundary:** Navigation, status, permissions, legal disclosure, safety warnings, identity, search, help, and nonaction content can span or exceed the three moments. One historical anecdote does not validate the definition or its claimed conversion effect.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-action-relative-microcopy-view; nonexclusive-and-nonexhaustive`.

### `MC2-C03` — words are one necessary modality, not the whole interaction

- **Locator:** Introduction, printed pages 10–11; PDF pages 8–9.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The author rejects wordless-interface minimalism and argues that language enables communication, guidance, relationship, reassurance, and action in digital products.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve linguistic meaning as a first-class design layer bound to behavior, visuals, semantics, audio, tactile cues, and service operations.
- **Boundary:** Not every meaning requires visible prose, and words cannot repair inaccessible components, false behavior, missing recovery, or service failure. More copy is not automatically clearer or more humane.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-language-as-design-material; multimodal-and-behavior-binding-required`.

### `MC2-C04` — experience, usability, and brand differentiation are distinct outcome claims

- **Locator:** Introduction, printed pages 10–11; PDF pages 8–9.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Microcopy is said to create positive engagement, reduce friction and error, save time and frustration, and express a distinctive, coherent brand.
- **Candidate implication:** Separate affect/relationship, task performance, accessibility, error prevention, brand recognition, return behavior, and organization outcomes with independent evidence and guardrails.
- **Boundary:** These outcomes can conflict and require different methods. A distinctive or engaging expression can reduce usability, while a usable expression need not increase affection, loyalty, or revenue.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-outcome-separation; no-compensatory-quality-score`.

### `MC2-C05` — “human,” personal, mutual, and loving relationships are metaphors, not product facts

- **Locator:** Introduction, printed pages 9–13; PDF pages 7–11.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Microcopy is presented as reducing machine alienation, creating a human personal conversation and mutual relationship, moving users emotionally, and making them return or “fall in love.”
- **Candidate implication:** Translate metaphors into observable expression properties and outcomes such as respectful address, understandable guidance, appropriate warmth, recognition, task return, or self-reported response.
- **Boundary:** Software does not possess emotion, reciprocity, care, or friendship. Anthropomorphic language can create misplaced trust, obscure organizational agency, or manipulate attachment.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-anthropomorphism-boundary; observable-relational-properties-required`.

### `MC2-C06` — the book offers practitioner methodology, not immediate universal rules

- **Locator:** Introduction, printed pages 11–13; PDF pages 9–11.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The guide promises step-by-step, immediately implementable methods derived from years of commercial practice, supported by logic and hundreds of interface examples.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve each method as an attributed candidate with scope, evidence, applicability, conflicts, locale, risk, authority, and validation requirements.
- **Boundary:** Practitioner experience, many examples, and an explanation do not establish general validity, current implementation behavior, accessibility, legality, or transfer across products and cultures.
- **Promotion status:** `source-method-boundary; cross-book-and-runtime-validation-required`.

### `MC2-C08` — predetermined voice and messaging reduce improvisation but cannot eliminate uncertainty

- **Locator:** Part 1 introduction, printed pages 14–15; PDF pages 12–13.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The first three chapters aim to replace repeated speculation about motivation, value, humor, slang, formality, emotion, and phrasing with prior decisions about messages, voice, and tone.
- **Candidate implication:** Maintain governed voice, tone, terminology, audience evidence, message purpose, and context objects so writers do not rediscover stable constraints for every string.
- **Boundary:** Prior decisions can become stale, wrong, exclusionary, or inapplicable to a new state. A guide cannot predict every user, locale, product change, risk, or operational truth; uncertainty and exceptions must remain visible.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-governed-authoring-context; freshness-and-exception-path-required`.

### `MC2-C09` — voice audits must inspect complete flows and adverse states

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 16–18; PDF pages 14–16.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The author audits registration, self-service, password recovery, contact, 404, and deliberately erroneous form states to reveal outdated, clichéd, heavy, robotic, inconsistent language.
- **Candidate implication:** Inventory voice and expression across entry, success, failure, recovery, help, rare states, roles, channels, locales, and repeated exposure; bind screenshots to actual behavior and version.
- **Boundary:** One expert walkthrough is a partial heuristic observation, not complete coverage or user evidence. Deliberately triggering errors requires safe test environments and cannot reveal inaccessible or permission-dependent states automatically.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-flow-wide-voice-audit; coverage-and-runtime-evidence-required`.

### `MC2-C10` — historical social-response research is a hypothesis source, not a current universal law

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 18–20; PDF pages 16–18.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Citing Clifford Nass and Corina Yen, the chapter says people apply social norms to computers and respond to praise, warmth, similarity, emotion, consistency, and humanlike language with productivity, trust, persuasion, or offense.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve the cited claims with study, population, task, date, construct, effect, limitations, and replication status; test product-specific interpretation rather than encode a universal social-response rule.
- **Boundary:** The chapter does not provide the underlying 100 study protocols or effect sizes. Technology, population, culture, disability, language, context, and awareness of automation can change responses.
- **Promotion status:** `historical-research-context; primary-study-and-transferability-review-required`.

### `MC2-C11` — trust and persuasion must not be maximized through personality coherence

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 18–20; PDF pages 16–18.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Consistent verbal and nonverbal personality is said to improve comprehension, authenticity, trust, emotional response, persuasion, and willingness to act, while inconsistency creates suspicion.
- **Candidate implication:** Treat consistency as a comprehension and expectation hypothesis; require truth, source authority, disclosure, autonomy, proportional influence, and calibrated reliance independently.
- **Boundary:** A coherent false or manipulative persona can be more dangerous than an awkward truthful interface. Trust, persuasion, and action are not inherently beneficial and must never compensate for deception or coercion.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-calibrated-reliance-boundary; no-trust-or-persuasion-maximization`.

### `MC2-C12` — voice/personality and messaging are separate but interacting layers

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed page 20; PDF page 18.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Voice-and-tone design is divided into personality—formality, humor, slang, pace, warmth, friendliness—and messaging—what to highlight, how to motivate, how users should feel, and what relationship benefits both parties.
- **Candidate implication:** Separate semantic proposition and purpose from expression constraints, while binding both to actor, product state, audience evidence, consequence, channel, and governance.
- **Boundary:** “How users should feel” is an organization intention, not a user fact or automatic ethical goal. Voice cannot change controlling meaning, and messaging cannot invent value or behavior.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-semantics-expression-separation; state-and-authority-binding-required`.

### `MC2-C13` — brand-specific expression is a variant over shared meaning

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 21–22; PDF pages 19–20.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Two commerce sites express the same legally required date-of-birth rationale differently to sustain serious-corporate versus warm-intimate identities and relationships.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve a verified semantic core, legal/domain constraints, and component behavior while allowing governed expression variants by brand, product, locale, and context.
- **Boundary:** The examples do not prove legal sufficiency, collection necessity, accessibility, comprehension, or user preference. “Same message” can conceal differences in qualification or implication.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-controlled-expression-variant; semantic-equivalence-and-domain-review-required`.

### `MC2-C14` — voice work has event-driven lifecycle triggers

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed page 22; PDF page 20.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Voice-and-tone design is recommended at brand creation, product release or upgrade, differentiation work, campaigns, communication-infrastructure changes, market entry, or when current language fails.
- **Candidate implication:** Define creation, review, and migration triggers tied to brand/product changes, audience/locale changes, channels, evidence, incidents, policy, component systems, and observed defects.
- **Boundary:** These seven triggers are useful but incomplete; legal, accessibility, safety, organizational, terminology, service, acquisition, and deprecation changes may also require review. A trigger does not itself authorize a rewrite.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-event-driven-voice-lifecycle; broader-dependency-triggers-required`.

### `MC2-C15` — the four-stage process mixes source review, user evidence, stakeholder assertions, and synthesis

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 23–24; PDF pages 21–22.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The proposed process reviews brand documents, extracts user language, interviews key organizational personnel, then consolidates findings into a working voice-and-tone document, with a stated one-to-three-week duration.
- **Candidate implication:** Type every input as organization claim, historical artifact, user evidence, market evidence, researcher interpretation, decision, or approved rule; preserve conflicts, dates, owners, and synthesis lineage.
- **Boundary:** Documents can be aspirational or stale, stakeholders do not speak for users, and a fixed duration cannot establish adequate research or governance. Synthesis is not validation or ratification.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-voice-discovery-workflow; evidence-types-and-approval-separated`.

### `MC2-C16` — group discussion exposes conflict but does not resolve it by itself

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed page 24; PDF page 22.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Group interviews are preferred so marketing, digital, service, sales, and advertising participants can resolve conflicting views immediately and produce a rich portrayal.
- **Candidate implication:** Record participants, roles, missing authorities, claims, evidence, disagreement, power dynamics, decision rule, owner, unresolved issues, and follow-up research.
- **Boundary:** A meeting can suppress dissent, privilege senior voices, and manufacture consensus. Organizational agreement cannot decide user truth, legal requirements, accessibility, safety, or product behavior.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-stakeholder-conflict-record; consensus-not-evidence-or-authority`.

### `MC2-C17` — vision and mission are organization-selected commitments

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 25–26 and questionnaire page 50; PDF pages 23–24 and 48.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Writers should uncover or revise the desired future change and present means of achieving it, then let all language implicitly advance those commitments; repeated “why” questions and foundational documents help surface them.
- **Candidate implication:** Record vision and mission as attributed, ratified organization goals with source, scope, conflicts, affected parties, evidence, owner, effective date, review trigger, and constraints.
- **Boundary:** Repetition of “why” does not uncover objective truth, and an inspiring mission can conflict with actual incentives or rights. Mission alignment cannot override user goals or hard requirements.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-organization-goal-artifact; ratification-and-conflict-controls-required`.

### `MC2-C18` — values require operational definitions and noncompensatory limits

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 27–29 and questionnaire page 50; PDF pages 25–27 and 48.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Brands select five indispensable values, describe each, ask how it sounds, and derive language qualities and word toolboxes; the chapter supplies examples and a list of 116 values.
- **Candidate implication:** Define each value through observable decisions, applicable contexts, prohibited interpretations, conflicts, evidence, examples/counterexamples, owner, and review—not only expression words.
- **Boundary:** A fixed count and adjective list are ideation aids, not an ontology. Declared accessibility, empathy, transparency, safety, or justice does not prove implemented behavior or outcomes and cannot compensate for violations.
- **Promotion status:** `qualified-value-authoring-method; operational-definition-and-hard-controls-required`.

### `MC2-C19` — word toolboxes are source-scoped lexical candidates

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed page 27; PDF page 25.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Writers collect words, expressions, and phrases associated with a value from thesauri and user language for reuse when expressing that value.
- **Candidate implication:** Store lexical candidates with concept, source, context, speaker, consent, locale, register, audience, examples, prohibited senses, expiry, and validation status.
- **Boundary:** Synonyms are not interchangeable, and repeated user language may be stigmatizing, private, sarcastic, domain-specific, or conceptually inconsistent. A word's association with a value is not evidence of interpretation.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-lexical-toolbox; provenance-locale-and-sense-required`.

### `MC2-C20` — brand personification is an authoring device, not an organizational identity fact

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 30–31 and questionnaire page 50; PDF pages 28–29 and 48.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Teams imagine a brand as a detailed person with age, clothing, family status, devices, hobbies, humor, traits, and famous analogues to derive a way of speaking, while distinguishing brand from audience personality.
- **Candidate implication:** Use only if it helps teams specify observable communication behavior; store traits as hypotheses and translate them into testable rules, examples, exclusions, and state-specific overrides.
- **Boundary:** Personification can import stereotypes, founder preference, gender/class coding, celebrity imitation, and false intimacy. Brands are organizations and systems, not people, and personal detail is usually unnecessary.
- **Promotion status:** `qualified-brand-personification; observable-rule-projection-only`.

### `MC2-C21` — six personality scales are workshop prompts, not canonical dimensions

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed page 30; PDF page 28.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Key personnel rate the brand between friendly/corporate, spontaneous/planned, modern/classic, cutting-edge/established, fun/serious, and accessible/upscale.
- **Candidate implication:** Admit scales as one elicitation view with source, raters, disagreement, examples, context, and operational interpretation; permit additional or nonbinary dimensions.
- **Boundary:** The poles are not true opposites, can encode class and accessibility as branding aesthetics, and have no supplied validity or reliability. Ratings are stakeholder judgments, not audience evidence.
- **Promotion status:** `workshop-elicitation-only; no-canonical-trait-ontology`.

### `MC2-C22` — humor is a contextual candidate, not shorthand for humanity

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 31–33; PDF pages 29–31.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Humor should follow brand values, audience culture and age, broader personality, flow-level dosage, clarity, situation, and exposure frequency rather than merely making microcopy “human.”
- **Candidate implication:** Evaluate humor by purpose, product state, severity, responsibility, culture, locale, power, repetition, accessibility, semantic clarity, and user evidence; default to literal respectful language under uncertainty.
- **Boundary:** Brand fit and audience targeting do not make a joke safe. Health, loss, denial, money, identity, safety, repeated failure, and unequal power can prohibit humor regardless of stated personality.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-contextual-humor-boundary; literal-safe-default-and-prohibited-states-required`.

### `MC2-C23` — clarity and situation constrain stylistic expression across the whole flow

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed page 33; PDF page 31.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Wit must not be the only characteristic, jokes should be distributed across a coherent flow, clarity must never be sacrificed, adverse situations require sensitivity, and repeated jokes become tiresome.
- **Candidate implication:** Review semantic invariants, flow sequence, cumulative tone, failure severity, repeat exposure, recovery, accessibility, and locale before approving expressive variants.
- **Boundary:** “Read twice” is not a sufficient clarity test, and a writer cannot infer sensitivity from intuition alone. Usability, safety, truth, and recovery require implementation and research evidence.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-flow-level-expression-review; hard-constraints-before-style`.

### `MC2-C24` — demographics are unsafe proxies for language, skill, humor, or instruction needs

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 34–35 and questionnaire page 51; PDF pages 32–33 and 49.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Age, gender, location, education, marital status, interests, and technological inclination are proposed for predicting slang, explanations, cultural references, sophistication, patience, and conservative versus distinctive voice.
- **Candidate implication:** Base content decisions on task knowledge, language, terminology familiarity, disability/access needs, context, observed behavior, and user-selected support; minimize demographic data and prohibit unsupported inference.
- **Boundary:** Older people do not inherently need more instruction, young people do not share slang or technical skill, and broad audiences do not require bland language. These proxies can stereotype and discriminate.
- **Promotion status:** `critical-demographic-proxy-rejection; task-and-access-evidence-required`.

### `MC2-C25` — distinct audiences may need distinct messages without fixed person categories

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 35–36; PDF pages 33–34.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Sellers/buyers, youths/families, and other groups may have different needs, hopes, objections, and messages while sharing stable brand characteristics with minor tuning.
- **Candidate implication:** Model actor, role, task, state, goal, authority, context, locale, and evidence as composable attributes; allow scoped message variants with semantic and policy constraints.
- **Boundary:** Named segments can be internally diverse and fluid; brand characteristics may also need major adaptation or recession. Segmentation cannot justify unequal rights, hidden persuasion, or sensitive profiling.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-contextual-message-variants; composable-attributes-over-fixed-segments`.

### `MC2-C26` — needs and problems require attributed research, not brand empathy claims

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 36–39 and questionnaire page 51; PDF pages 34–37 and 49.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Teams identify practical challenges and emotional worries in users' words, then use them in messages, calls to action, instructions, reassurance, and feedback; a job-board example links research and usability findings to content choices.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve participant source, consent, method, sample, context, analysis, disagreement, construct, severity, scope, product behavior, and decision lineage for each claimed need or problem.
- **Boundary:** Listening does not grant access to a person's inner state, and selected quotes do not establish prevalence or causality. Product copy must not exploit distress, loneliness, fear, livelihood, or other vulnerability.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-needs-evidence-contract; anti-projection-and-vulnerability-controls-required`.

### `MC2-C27` — participant language is evidence, not free reusable copy

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed page 39; PDF page 37.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Chat transcripts, surveys, communities, social feedback, usability recordings, call-center calls, focus groups, and interviews are described as sources of authentic, precise phrases to embed in copy.
- **Candidate implication:** Record collection authority, participant expectations, consent, privacy, sensitivity, source context, quotation versus synthesis, representativeness, compensation, locale, transformation, and publication permission.
- **Boundary:** Operational access is not permission to mine or publish language. Verbatim reuse can expose identity, appropriate speech, strip context, preserve slurs or misconceptions, and convert support interactions into undisclosed marketing research.
- **Promotion status:** `critical-participant-language-governance; evidence-not-free-copy`.

### `MC2-C28` — fixed lightweight interview counts and convenience participants do not guarantee insight

- **Candidate implication:** Choose sampling and stopping strategy from research question, population diversity, risk, saturation/coverage, recruitment bias, accessibility, consent, compensation, and analysis plan.
- **Boundary:** Friends and family are convenience samples subject to relationship and desirability bias; five to ten is not a universal sufficiency threshold. Short duration does not waive research governance.
- **Promotion status:** `research-heuristic-only; sampling-and-ethics-design-required`.

### `MC2-C29` — emotional hardship claims must preserve source and uncertainty

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 37–41; PDF pages 35–39.

- **Promotion status:** `strong-emotion-provenance; no-role-based-inner-state-inference`.

### `MC2-C30` — hopes and aspirations create an influence surface requiring autonomy review

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 40–41 and questionnaire page 51; PDF pages 38–39 and 49.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Writers identify hoped-for outcomes and remind users how actions such as signup, purchase, categorization, or continued use move them toward dreams, control, security, or self-improvement.
- **Candidate implication:** Bind motivational content to verified capability, proportional claim, voluntary choice, consequence, audience evidence, uncertainty, and a nonpersuasive path to complete necessary tasks.
- **Boundary:** Aspirations can be exploited, especially in finance, health, work, education, or relationships. A product's availability does not prove it advances a dream or makes an action “best” for the person.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-motivational-influence-boundary; autonomy-and-claim-evidence-required`.

### `MC2-C31` — concerns should be resolved through product truth and remedy, not merely “overcome”

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 42–43 and questionnaire page 51; PDF pages 40–41 and 49.

- **Candidate implication:** Type each concern with source, actual condition, product/policy owner, evidence, disclosure, prevention, choice, remedy, eligibility, exceptions, support, and monitoring.
- **Boundary:** Reluctance to spend or provide personal data may be rational and should not automatically be dispelled. Copy cannot guarantee security, authenticity, refund rights, or service remedies without verified operational and legal support.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-concern-to-remedy-contract; no-copy-only-objection-handling`.

### `MC2-C32` — competitive advantage messages require current comparative evidence

- **Candidate implication:** Record comparator set, market/date, product/version, method, source, claim wording, qualification, locale, legal approval, substantiation, owner, expiry, and user relevance.
- **Boundary:** “Better,” “leading,” “more than anywhere,” salary, quality, and opportunity claims can be stale, incomparable, or misleading. Internal enthusiasm and competitor difference do not establish a genuine user benefit.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-comparative-claim-contract; substantiation-and-expiry-required`.

### `MC2-C33` — relationship labels are intent metaphors with power consequences

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 46–47 and questionnaire page 51; PDF pages 44–45 and 49.

- **Candidate implication:** Replace labels with observable interaction qualities, actor identities, authority, duties, boundaries, disclosure, user control, and prohibited implications.
- **Boundary:** Products cannot be friends, parents, partners, or couples; these metaphors can hide commercial interests, hierarchy, dependence, surveillance, or emotional manipulation. Users may reject the intended relationship.
- **Promotion status:** `critical-relationship-metaphor-boundary; explicit-power-and-role-contract-required`.

### `MC2-C34` — voice guidance is a governed living artifact, not a decorative deliverable

### `MC2-C36` — a digital product represents an organization but is not a human service agent

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed page 53; PDF page 51.
- **Documented practitioner position:** A released product is compared with an official customer-service representative that welcomes, explains, serves, jokes, and bids farewell; warm conversational writing is proposed to recreate trust and closeness.
- **Candidate implication:** Identify the actual organizational actor and service capability; express responsiveness, respect, guidance, and support availability without implying a live person, personal attention, or reciprocal relationship.
- **Boundary:** An interface cannot see, smile, care, react, or provide human judgment unless a disclosed human service actually does so. Human-service metaphors can disguise automation, scale, surveillance, or absent escalation.

### `MC2-C37` — warmth and conversationality are expression qualities, not trust evidence
