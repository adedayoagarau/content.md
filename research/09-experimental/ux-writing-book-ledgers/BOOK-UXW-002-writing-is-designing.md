## Author-position records

These records describe the authors' framework. They are not universal findings or approved `content.md` behavior.

### `WID-C01` — interface writing is an iterative design activity

- **Locator:** FAQ and Introduction, PDF pages 6–17; Chapter 1, PDF pages 19–22.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Words should be created as the experience develops, iterated, researched, and collaboratively validated rather than inserted after visual or interaction design. Writing arranges language, while designing uses that language and other materials to solve problems for people.
- **Candidate implication:** Treat UX-writing work as participation in problem definition, interaction and information design, candidate generation, prototyping, evaluation, and revision. An output packet must be able to recommend a non-text or behavior change when copy cannot solve the evidenced problem.
- **Boundary:** Calling writing “design” does not grant unilateral authority over product behavior, research, visual design, accessibility conformance, engineering, legal/domain facts, or release. The thesis is the authors' professional framework rather than experimental proof that every writing process must follow one organizational form.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-practice-model-candidate; role-authority-separate`.

### `WID-C02` — the writer is a role rather than a protected title

- **Locator:** “How to Use This Book,” FAQ, and Introduction, PDF pages 5–17; Chapter 1, PDF pages 36–38.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Anyone who writes words people encounter in a product is performing the writer role, whether their title is writer, designer, developer, researcher, product manager, or something else. Specialists may also coach teammates and build guidance for distributed writing.
- **Candidate implication:** Assign authorship, expertise, review, and decision authority per artifact instead of inferring them from job title. Self-service and coaching need governed context, patterns, escalation, and review proportional to risk.
- **Boundary:** Role inclusivity does not make all contributors equally qualified for every task or allow distributed authorship to bypass terminology, accessibility, localization, domain, privacy, safety, or approval controls.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-role-model-candidate; competency-and-authority-required`.

### `WID-C03` — usable, useful, and responsible are distinct design questions

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, PDF pages 21–27.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The authors propose evaluating words and the surrounding experience as usable, useful, and responsible: people can understand and act; the experience serves a real intent and provides control or value; and it is true, kind, inclusive, resistant to misuse, and does not exploit trusted language for business advantage.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve separate evaluation dimensions for task usability, user value/agency, and responsibility/harm. Define observable criteria, applicable hard failures, evidence, affected populations, and accountable reviewers for each rather than producing a compensatory total score.
- **Boundary:** This triad is a practitioner framing, not an exhaustive assurance taxonomy or validated scale. “Kind,” “value,” and “responsible” are context-dependent and require affected-user, domain, accessibility, safety, privacy, and legal evidence where applicable.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-evaluation-frame-candidate; noncompensatory-controls-required`.

### `WID-C04` — accessibility is part of usable implementation, not an editorial add-on

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, PDF pages 22–24.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Descriptive links and plain language can improve use for screen-reader users, people with cognitive disabilities, newer language learners, and people under stress. If content cannot be accessed, the design prevents use.
- **Candidate implication:** Bind visible link/control text to accessible name, role, purpose, context, focus/keyboard behavior, destination or effect, state, and implemented accessibility-tree evidence. Plain-language review should complement rather than replace user and assistive-technology evaluation.
- **Boundary:** “Accessible to everyone” is an aspiration, not a provable result from wording alone. Descriptive text does not establish semantic markup, focus order, input operation, visual access, reflow, announcements, or conformance; disability and language groups are not homogeneous.
- **Promotion status:** `corroborates-accessibility-as-design; implementation-evidence-required`.

### `WID-C05` — useful writing preserves intent, control, and separable choices

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, PDF pages 24–26.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Useful experiences honor what people came to do and balance that intent with organizational goals. The chapter criticizes a hotel checkout that bundles purchase terms, loyalty enrollment, marketing, and privacy into one required choice and reverses opt-out logic, while presenting plain-language legal summaries as a more useful approach.
- **Candidate implication:** Model each decision, purpose, consequence, recipient, data use, and withdrawal path separately. Material terms, marketing, account enrollment, and privacy choices need distinct semantic identities and authority; presentation must not exploit defaults, polarity, hierarchy, or workflow obstruction.
- **Boundary:** A plain-language summary cannot replace controlling terms or prove informed consent. Exact consent requirements are jurisdiction- and purpose-specific; legal/privacy authority, accessible implementation, equal choice, and observed comprehension remain necessary.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-user-control-and-choice-candidate; consent-authority-required`.

### `WID-C06` — responsible design anticipates foreseeable misuse and context failure

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, PDF pages 26–29.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Harm may be deliberate, as in confirm-shaming, or emerge when suggested replies and other predetermined language appear in an inappropriate sensitive context. Writers share responsibility for considering how language may be used by algorithms, internal teams, malicious actors, or accidental activation—not only its intended scenario.
- **Candidate implication:** Threat-model content across intended, accidental, adversarial, automated, stale, cross-context, and out-of-order use. Define prohibited rhetoric, sensitive states, candidate suppression/abstention, context and confidence requirements, human correction, reporting, and incident feedback.
- **Boundary:** Content review cannot predict all misuse or control a system without behavior, model, security, and operational safeguards. Responsibility must be allocated across product, data/model, engineering, policy, safety, and release owners rather than placed solely on the writer.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-content-misuse-candidate; system-level-controls-required`.

### `WID-C07` — language decisions participate in systems and outcomes

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, PDF pages 29–34.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Many contributors shape a person's environment, and language is one of the design materials affecting understanding, feeling, and outcome. Product words span multiple actors, surfaces, channels, and lifecycle moments rather than only button labels or one consumer app.
- **Candidate implication:** Map semantic messages across actors, tasks, surfaces, channels, dependencies, and lifecycle states. Keep shared meaning and terminology linked while allowing channel-, role-, locale-, and modality-specific expressions and behavior contracts.
- **Boundary:** Saying every word shapes an outcome does not establish the direction or magnitude of its effect. Emotional and behavioral impact require contextual evidence, and cross-channel consistency must not force identical wording where roles or constraints differ.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-ecosystem-message-candidate; effect-evidence-required`.

### `WID-C08` — content-first exploration and visual/interaction design are reciprocal

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, PDF pages 34–36.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Drafting language before visual design can help a team understand and respond to the experience, especially in voice interfaces, but writers and visual designers serve the same people and goals without one discipline universally preceding or replacing the other.
- **Candidate implication:** Support text-first, dialogue-first, structure-first, visual-first, and code-first entry points that converge on one semantic/behavior contract. Preserve reciprocal design changes and provenance rather than treating a text draft or visual frame as the final source of truth.
- **Boundary:** Text-first work does not prove the interaction is feasible, accessible, complete, or appropriate. Voice and conversational interfaces still have nonverbal timing, audio, error, privacy, discoverability, and device constraints requiring specialized evidence.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-multientry-design-workflow-candidate; convergence-contract-required`.

### `WID-C09` — professional confidence supports participation but not decision status

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, PDF pages 35–39.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Writers often enter too late, lack context, or apologize for participating in design despite contributing research, information architecture, prototyping, testing, strategy, rationale, facilitation, and alignment. Describing writing as design helps teams recognize the actual scope of work rather than serving as a title or power claim.
- **Candidate implication:** Capability onboarding should make contribution points, required context, artifacts, review routes, decision boundaries, and escalation explicit. Measure early involvement and resolved product problems rather than title adoption or perceived status.
- **Boundary:** Confidence, inclusion in workshops, academic credentials, or a design label cannot substitute for evidence, competency, accountable ownership, or affected-user participation. Professional advocacy must remain nondeceptive and outcome-bound.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-capability-positioning; outcomes-not-status-proxies`.

### `WID-C10` — names and labels shape mental models and can hide persuasion

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, PDF pages 38–40.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Product language establishes categories and expectations; repurposing socially important concepts for commercial systems can quietly influence how people think and act. People who define a digital environment through language should examine persuasion that may occur without conscious recognition.
- **Candidate implication:** Naming decisions need concept definition, audience interpretation, existing social/domain meaning, affected groups, business incentive, likely inference, ambiguity, manipulation risk, alternatives, research, decision authority, and post-release evidence. Flag appropriation of trusted institutional, safety, social, or legal terms.
- **Boundary:** The claim that language changes thought is broad and does not establish a deterministic effect for a particular term. Ethical review must consider interface hierarchy, defaults, data use, algorithms, and business model alongside words.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-naming-and-persuasion-candidate; contextual-harm-review-required`.

### `WID-C11` — the framework is intentionally adaptable rather than prescriptive

- **Locator:** Introduction, PDF pages 13–17; Chapter 1, PDF page 40.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The authors offer ways to think about the work rather than one tactical recipe and ask readers to adapt the ideas to their users, teams, products, and constraints.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve source-specific practices as configurable projections with applicability, exceptions, evidence scope, and abstention. Cross-book synthesis should identify convergent invariants without flattening meaningful disagreement or context.
- **Boundary:** Adaptability cannot waive external requirements or authorize unsupported local rules. Repository evidence, standards, laws, product behavior, and accountable decisions remain authoritative within their scopes.
- **Promotion status:** `source-method-boundary; cross-book-synthesis-required`.

### `WID-C12` — generic best practices are hypotheses until fitted to context

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, PDF pages 42–43.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The chapter rejects choosing words merely because large companies or an industry use them. An appropriate decision depends on the people, task, product strategy, and team goals in the particular situation.
- **Candidate implication:** Store external patterns and competitor examples with source, task, audience, locale, date/version, observed behavior, applicability, and transfer risks. Use them to generate candidates or research questions, never as automatic approval or proof.
- **Boundary:** Contextual fit does not mean every local preference is equally valid. Applicable standards, laws, platform contracts, domain terminology, accessibility requirements, and observed product behavior remain authoritative within their scope.
- **Promotion status:** `corroborates-patterns-as-advisory-evidence; local-validation-required`.

### `WID-C13` — strategy states a bounded direction and explicit exclusions

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, PDF pages 43–45.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Strategy aligns a team around where to focus for a defined period and, by implication, what not to pursue. It provides shared rationale across interface content, product, engineering, design, marketing, legal, and leadership decisions.
- **Candidate implication:** A strategy record should identify scope, time horizon, user and organizational outcomes, evidence, constraints, priorities, exclusions, dependencies, accountable owner, decision authority, measures, review triggers, and supersession. Candidate content should trace which strategic element it serves.
- **Boundary:** Alignment, executive support, and organizational rationale do not establish user benefit, legal sufficiency, feasibility, or causal effectiveness. Strategy cannot waive noncompensatory constraints or conceal conflicts among stakeholders and affected people.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-strategy-object-candidate; evidence-and-authority-required`.

### `WID-C14` — collaborative strategy statements expose assumptions and tradeoffs

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, PDF pages 45–49.
- **Documented practitioner position:** A facilitated statement can combine user type, user need, value provided, and business benefit, forcing groups to choose priorities and compare interpretations. The resulting statement should remain visible and guide product-, feature-, or initiative-level decisions.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve each proposed statement, participant role, disagreement, unresolved assumption, supporting evidence, selected formulation, and decision. Keep `audience`, `need`, `user_value`, and `organization_benefit` as separately sourced fields rather than one persuasive sentence.
- **Boundary:** Workshop agreement is stakeholder evidence, not user research or proof that a need, audience, value, or benefit exists. Facilitation by a writer does not grant strategy approval; conflicts and affected groups omitted from the room must remain visible.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-strategy-authoring-view; underlying-fields-remain-canonical`.

### `WID-C15` — strategy quality requires actionability, relevance, user evidence, and verifiability

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, PDF pages 48–50.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The authors recommend checking whether a strategy directs action, fits wider organizational work, benefits users and is research-based, and has a completion condition reasonable people can recognize. Vague statements and unshared personal agendas do not guide work.
- **Candidate implication:** Validate strategies with explicit allowed/blocked decisions, upstream alignment, evidence coverage, measurable exit or review conditions, and stakeholder/affected-user conflicts. Record what would falsify or require revision of the strategy.
- **Boundary:** “Reasonable people agree” is not an operational test, and verification may require multiple quantitative, qualitative, accessibility, harm, and domain measures. A strategy can be specific and measurable while still unethical or exclusionary.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-strategy-quality-candidate; construct-and-guardrail-review-required`.

### `WID-C16` — research begins with an explicit question and fitted method

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, PDF pages 50–53 and 60.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Before contacting participants, teams should state what they want to learn, then choose interviews, contextual inquiry, usability testing, or other qualitative or quantitative approaches suited to the question. Research may reveal that the original wording or even product problem was framed incorrectly.
- **Candidate implication:** A research plan needs question, decision it informs, current evidence and assumptions, population/task/context, method rationale, recruitment and sample logic, protocol, consent/privacy, accessibility accommodations, analysis, limitations, owner, and evidence-to-decision path.
- **Boundary:** A question does not justify collecting any available data or contacting people without authority. Method choice and inference require qualified review; research should be proportionate to risk and must not be manufactured by an LLM as if it were observed evidence.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-research-plan-candidate; ethics-and-method-authority-required`.

### `WID-C17` — stakeholder opinion and user evidence are different source classes

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, PDF pages 50–53 and 59–60.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Teams often substitute their own experience or a colleague's anecdote for knowledge about users. The authors urge practitioners to move beyond “if I were the user” claims and learn about actual people, environments, use, perception, and behavior.
- **Candidate implication:** Label every claim as stakeholder assertion, repository/product fact, analytics observation, participant report, observed behavior, expert judgment, external research, or hypothesis, with source and scope. Prevent assertions from being promoted to audience facts without corroboration and an accountable decision.
- **Boundary:** User testimony is also situated and fallible; “the user” is not one homogeneous primary stakeholder. Purchasers, operators, bystanders, support staff, administrators, and people exposed to downstream consequences may have different legitimate interests.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-evidence-type-separation; no-proxy-user-claims`.

### `WID-C18` — interviews elicit language and experience without seeding answers

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, PDF pages 53–55.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Open prompts that omit internal product terminology let participants describe work in their own words. Interviewers should avoid leading questions, interruption, and disclosure that would make participants self-monitor target vocabulary; effective interviewing is a specialized practice.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve interview guide/version, moderator, prompt intent, prohibited leading terms, participant context, consent, response/observation, coding, competing labels, frequency only within the sample, and interpretation. Candidate terms retain source and audience context until terminology governance decides.
- **Boundary:** Natural participant vocabulary is not automatically accurate, accessible, inclusive, legally appropriate, stable across locales, or representative. Concealing a study's exact hypothesis may reduce demand effects but cannot compromise informed consent or involve deception without qualified ethical review.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-interview-and-language-evidence-candidate; terminology-decision-separate`.

### `WID-C19` — contextual inquiry reveals environmental and organizational constraints

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, PDF pages 55–57.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Observing people perform work in their environment exposes physical conditions, interruptions, devices, protective equipment, time pressure, workflows, and actor relationships that interviews or internal assumptions may miss. Such findings can change the target actor or product strategy, not only wording.
- **Candidate implication:** Context evidence should record site/task, participant and actor roles, environment, device/connectivity, accessibility and safety constraints, observation conditions, deviations caused by the study, artifacts, analysis, and scope. Route product-strategy changes to accountable owners and affected groups.
- **Boundary:** Field presence is not neutral and a small set of sites cannot establish universal work conditions. Safety, site authorization, privacy, confidentiality, compensation, labor power, and recording restrictions require explicit handling; shifting features from workers to managers can create surveillance or power harms.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-context-evidence-candidate; workplace-power-and-safety-review-required`.

### `WID-C20` — usability testing evaluates the resolved experience and adverse states

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, PDF pages 57–59.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Usability tests give people realistic tasks and observe their interaction with the combined words, visuals, behavior, and performance. Writers should ensure scenarios exercise language-dependent moments such as confirmations, errors, missing information, and expected next actions.
- **Candidate implication:** A usability-test record should bind product/prototype build, locale, device, assistive technology, task/scenario, starting state, success and safety criteria, reachable error/confirmation/recovery states, moderator, observations, participant language, severity, limitations, and exact candidate/version.
- **Boundary:** Think-aloud commentary can alter behavior, prototype success may not transfer to production, and a few qualitative sessions do not estimate population rates. Accessibility-specific journeys require representative disabled participants, accommodations, and implemented technical evidence where applicable.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-resolved-experience-test-candidate; inference-scope-required`.

### `WID-C21` — isolated content tests diagnose interpretation but remove system context

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, PDF pages 58–59.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Highlighting or rating words outside the interface can reveal confidence, perceived helpfulness, motivation, expectation, and tone; follow-up questions often explain why participants responded as they did.
- **Candidate implication:** Label isolated tests as content-perception or comprehension evidence and preserve scenario framing, material shown, question wording, scales, response rationale, order effects, and candidate version. Pair them with contextual or implemented tests before making behavior or usability claims.
- **Boundary:** Removing content from hierarchy, interaction, timing, state, visuals, accessible semantics, and consequences changes the construct. Color-highlighting methods need non-color and accessible alternatives, and ordinal ratings do not become interval measures merely because they use numbers.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-diagnostic-method; contextual-validation-required`.

### `WID-C22` — research necessity does not waive feasibility, ethics, or evidentiary humility

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, PDF pages 59–60.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The authors reject lack of time, money, access, or perceived need as sufficient reasons to rely solely on intuition; research reduces expensive misdirection and anchors decisions in the people who use the experience. They acknowledge that the chapter covers only a small portion of available methods.
- **Candidate implication:** When primary research is unavailable, record the constraint, risk, existing evidence, safe proxy methods, uncertainty, decision limit, and plan to obtain stronger evidence. Fail closed for claims or releases whose risk requires participant, accessibility, domain, or runtime validation.
- **Boundary:** Research has real cost and potential harm; access restrictions may protect vulnerable people, confidential settings, or legal obligations. “User insight over anyone's opinion” does not override safety, law, technical reality, or the rights of nonuser affected parties, and no single study is final truth.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-evidence-necessity-boundary; proportional-and-ethical-research-required`.

### `WID-C23` — clarity is contextual rather than compliance with a mechanical style rule

- **Locator:** Chapter 3, PDF pages 62–64 and 82.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Grammar and style conventions, including punctuation choices, serve different purposes in different formats and domains. The authors frame clarity as a moving target shaped by functionality, audience, use context, and the consequences of ambiguity—not as universal obedience to one rulebook.
- **Candidate implication:** Represent style guidance with purpose, audience, channel, locale, domain, risk, exceptions, and precedence. Evaluate the resolved message against the task and consequence instead of treating a lint pass as proof of clarity.
- **Boundary:** Context does not excuse errors that change meaning or violate an applicable legal, regulatory, platform, accessibility, localization, or domain requirement. Exceptions need an accountable rationale rather than personal preference.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-contextual-style-candidate; applicable-rules-remain-binding`.

### `WID-C24` — define the content's job and product facts before polishing expression

- **Locator:** Chapter 3, PDF pages 64–66.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Writers need to know what the content must accomplish and ask concrete questions about billing, onboarding, notifications, timing, eligibility, and system behavior. An unconstrained text draft can help a team align on meaning before fitting words into a surface.
- **Candidate implication:** Require a message contract containing actor, intent, trigger, state, facts, action, consequence, timing, destination, exclusions, and source authority before style optimization. Allow low-fidelity semantic drafts to expose unresolved product questions and route them to owners.
- **Boundary:** A text document is a collaborative design artifact, not canonical runtime truth or evidence that behavior is feasible, complete, accessible, localized, or approved. Unknown facts must remain explicit rather than being filled by plausible copy.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-message-contract-candidate; unresolved-facts-block-assertion`.

### `WID-C25` — foreseeable sensitive states require early implication and harm review

- **Locator:** Chapter 3, PDF pages 66–68.
- **Documented practitioner position:** A playful weight-loss message can become harmful for a child or a person with an eating disorder. Because late organizational and technical constraints may prevent change, writers should investigate implications and participate while product behavior is still negotiable.
- **Candidate implication:** Review candidate behavior and language across sensitive identities, goals, health or crisis states, minors, coercion, failure, and unintended audiences before implementation hardens. Record harm scenario, exposed group, trigger, severity, evidence, mitigation, abstention, escalation owner, and residual risk.
- **Boundary:** A writer cannot diagnose health conditions or represent every affected population. Safety, clinical, child-protection, accessibility, policy, legal, and lived-experience expertise may be required, and early participation does not transfer their authority to content practitioners.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-sensitive-state-review-candidate; qualified-authority-required`.

### `WID-C26` — recognition depends on stable concepts, visible options, and truthful actions

- **Locator:** Chapter 3, PDF pages 68–69.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Interfaces reduce reliance on memory when people can recognize the relevant object, action, and options. Inconsistent labels such as “Delete” and “Erase” create avoidable uncertainty about whether two controls perform the same operation.
- **Candidate implication:** Give each governed concept and action a stable identity, map all surface terms to it, expose necessary options at the decision point, and bind labels to actual state transitions and consequences. Detect unexplained synonym drift across surfaces and channels.
- **Boundary:** Identical wording is not always appropriate across roles, locales, modalities, or levels of expertise, and visible choices can themselves overload an interface. Consistency must preserve semantic equivalence, not merely repeat strings.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-recognition-and-concept-identity-candidate; behavior-binding-required`.

### `WID-C27` — cognitive load is a property of the resolved interaction, not word count

- **Locator:** Chapter 3, PDF pages 69–74.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Clear content reduces unnecessary mental work, but extreme concision can remove distinctions people need. When a complex relationship-search module could not be described accurately in a short label, the team simplified and separated its functionality rather than shipping misleading copy.
- **Candidate implication:** Evaluate load across information, hierarchy, choices, prior context, memory demand, timing, behavior, and consequence. If required meaning cannot fit the surface, change disclosure, layout, sequence, or product behavior; do not optimize character count by deleting semantic obligations.
- **Boundary:** A fixed reading-time threshold, sentence length, or word count cannot establish comprehension or cognitive accessibility. Any formula is a heuristic requiring audience-, task-, disability-, device-, locale-, and risk-specific evidence.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-system-level-clarity-candidate; brevity-is-not-validity`.

### `WID-C28` — metaphors create audience-, locale-, time-, and capability-dependent contracts

- **Locator:** Chapter 3, PDF pages 70–72.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Familiar metaphors can support recognition, but they can also age, travel poorly across cultures, become internally inconsistent, or invite behavior the system cannot support. A conversational presentation, for example, may lead people to greet or question a system as if it understands open conversation.
- **Candidate implication:** Record a metaphor's intended mapping, target audience and locales, supported actions, misleading inferences, exclusions, consistency rules, accessibility implications, test evidence, and retirement trigger. Do not use humanlike language or affordances beyond demonstrated system capability.
- **Boundary:** A familiar metaphor is not universally understood, and literal language is not automatically clearer. Comprehension, localization, cultural, accessibility, deception, and runtime-capability review remain necessary.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-metaphor-governance-candidate; capability-truth-required`.

### `WID-C29` — audience vocabulary is evidence gathered from several situated sources

- **Locator:** Chapter 3, PDF pages 74–76.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Teams can learn the words people use through online communities and reviews, internal search queries, support interactions, customer-facing coworkers, and direct research. These sources may reveal a substantial gap between internal terminology and user language.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve vocabulary observations with source class, audience, task, channel, locale, date, frequency within sample, quotation/paraphrase status, analysis method, privacy treatment, and representativeness limits. Triangulate sources before governing a term.
- **Boundary:** Public availability or internal access does not eliminate privacy, consent, confidentiality, retention, platform-policy, or re-identification risk. Search and support data reflect particular populations and failures; coworker reports are mediated evidence, not direct user truth.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-vocabulary-evidence-candidate; privacy-and-sampling-controls-required`.

### `WID-C30` — plain language is audience-appropriate specificity, not universal vocabulary removal

- **Locator:** Chapter 3, PDF pages 76–80.
- **Documented practitioner position:** For broad audiences, familiar words, direct structure, and concrete instructions usually improve speed and reach. Specialized audiences may be better served by established domain terms; explaining basic professional concepts can slow them down and make an interface less useful.
- **Candidate implication:** Evaluate terminology against audience expertise, task, domain meaning, locale, search behavior, accessibility needs, and observed comprehension. Prefer specific instructions that name the actual product object or control, while preserving governed professional vocabulary where evidence supports it.
- **Boundary:** “Plain” is not a fixed reading level or list of short words, and industry usage alone does not prove comprehension, inclusion, correctness, or translation fitness. Legal and technical precision may require defined terms, layered explanation, or role-specific variants.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-audience-fit-language-candidate; no-universal-simplification-rule`.

### `WID-C31` — consequence-first confirmations depend on the preceding flow

- **Locator:** Chapter 3, PDF pages 80–82.
- **Documented practitioner position:** In a permission-setting example, the authors decompose a dense confirmation, inspect what the administrator saw immediately beforehand, prioritize the retroactive loss of existing public links, put the decision in the title, align answer controls to that question, and remove repeated explanation and jargon.
- **Candidate implication:** Model confirmations with initiating action, prior state/context, changed state, immediate and retroactive consequences, affected objects and people, reversibility, question, and control outcomes. Order material consequences before supporting detail and avoid repeating information that remains perceptible and unambiguous in the flow.
- **Boundary:** “Yes/No” is safe only when the question remains visible, unambiguous, accessible, stable across translation, and correctly bound to behavior. Destructive, high-risk, delayed, or cross-channel actions may require explicit action labels, authentication, review, undo, or additional disclosure.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-confirmation-contract-candidate; risk-calibrated-controls-required`.

### `WID-C32` — clarity claims require testing the message within its interface and audience

- **Locator:** Chapter 3, PDF pages 79 and 82.
- **Documented practitioner position:** When jargon, shortened copy, or contextual clues might or might not be understood, the authors recommend putting the copy and prototype in front of real users. What is clear varies with application, audience, context, and function.
- **Candidate implication:** Bind a clarity claim to candidate version, implemented or prototype context, audience and locale, task, starting knowledge, method, comprehension and action criteria, observed errors, limitations, and reviewer. Treat untested clarity as a hypothesis.
- **Boundary:** A usability session cannot prove universal clarity or production accessibility, and participant agreement does not validate factual, legal, safety, privacy, or behavioral accuracy. High-risk claims need qualified and runtime evidence beyond comprehension testing.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-contextual-clarity-evidence-candidate; inference-scope-required`.

### `WID-C33` — error states interrupt a goal and often carry emotional load

- **Locator:** Chapter 4, PDF pages 84–86.
- **Documented practitioner position:** People generally approach a product to accomplish something outside the interface; an error interrupts that goal and may produce confusion, frustration, apology, or abandonment. The authors ask teams to reframe error work around helping people continue rather than judging correct product use.
- **Candidate implication:** An error-state record should begin with the person's goal, interrupted task, current state, likely context and urgency, preserved work, consequence, emotional or safety exposure, and viable recovery—not with an error string. Review language for blame, shame, and implied incompetence.
- **Boundary:** Emotional reactions and abandonment rates vary by audience, task, severity, history, and context. The chapter's practitioner observations do not quantify prevalence or establish that wording alone caused an outcome.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-goal-centered-error-frame; effect-evidence-required`.

### `WID-C34` — causal uncertainty must remain visible instead of being assigned to the user

- **Locator:** Chapter 4, PDF pages 85–87.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Failures may result from input, technology, policy, design, or an unusual scenario. Teams should investigate plausible causes and real-life context and avoid language that makes people assume blame, especially when the product cannot establish causality.
- **Candidate implication:** Separate detected condition, verified cause, suspected causes, responsible subsystem, user-controllable factors, and safe disclosure. Generate causal language only from sufficiently specific runtime evidence; otherwise state the observable condition and a nonaccusatory next step.
- **Boundary:** Avoiding blame does not mean concealing an evidenced input mismatch, prohibited action, security event, or accountable responsibility. Accuracy, fraud resistance, incident response, and legal duties can require precise attribution with respectful language.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-cause-and-blame-candidate; runtime-confidence-required`.

### `WID-C35` — error discovery precedes composition and may require finer state taxonomy

- **Locator:** Chapter 4, PDF pages 87–89.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Before writing, teams should ask why the state exists, what happened before it, and what caused it. A financial sign-in project showed that one generic message across many codes produced dead ends or indiscriminate support referrals, while more specific states enabled self-service or direct specialist routing.
- **Candidate implication:** Map raw signals and policies into governed user-relevant states with trigger, prior step, known cause, security-disclosure limit, affected task, recoverability, destination, owner, observability, and fallback. Do not collapse states whose safe next actions materially differ.
- **Boundary:** More specific messages can expose account existence, security controls, fraud detection, private status, or exploitable distinctions. Security, privacy, legal, support, and domain owners must decide which distinctions may be disclosed and to whom.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-error-taxonomy-candidate; safe-disclosure-review-required`.

### `WID-C36` — business evidence can justify investment but cannot prove content causality

- **Locator:** Chapter 4, PDF pages 88–89.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The financial-product case connected generic sign-in failures with call volume and handling cost to obtain support for targeted error handling; the broader onboarding changes were associated with improved sign-in and adoption. The chapter presents organizational metrics as a way to make preventative design work legible to decision-makers.
- **Candidate implication:** Link error-state proposals to user outcome, operational burden, support demand, abandonment, adoption, safety, and cost hypotheses, with baseline, instrumentation, confounders, and evaluation plan. Preserve user benefit and noncompensatory protections even when no immediate revenue metric exists.
- **Boundary:** The reported case combines several onboarding changes and does not isolate the causal effect of particular messages. Monetizing risk can underweight dignity, exclusion, rare severe harm, accessibility, and people who are not paying customers.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-error-investment-evidence; causal-and-equity-limits-required`.

### `WID-C37` — avoid, explain, and resolve are distinct layers of error handling

- **Locator:** Chapter 4, PDF pages 89–95.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The authors organize error design into prevention or assistance that avoids unnecessary failure, an explanation of the condition, and a path to resolve it. They prioritize resolution because an accurate explanation without an actionable next step can still leave the person stranded.
- **Candidate implication:** Evaluate each state for prevention, detection, explanation, recovery, fallback, escalation, preserved data, reversibility, and confirmation of success. Permit `no-message-needed` only when the design prevents the invalid transition without obscuring requirements or blocking exploration.
- **Boundary:** Not every error is preventable, explainable in detail, or recoverable by the current actor. Prevention controls, disabled states, input formatting, and automation can introduce discoverability, accessibility, speed, coercion, or data-quality failures and require their own tests.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-avoid-explain-resolve-model; resolved-interaction-evidence-required`.

### `WID-C38` — prevention must expose prerequisites and preserve accessible ways to proceed

- **Locator:** Chapter 4, PDF pages 89–92.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Visual hierarchy, interaction sequencing, formatting assistance, date controls, and example utterances can help people meet prerequisites before an error occurs. The examples also acknowledge speed and capability tradeoffs.
- **Candidate implication:** Bind every gated action to a perceivable prerequisite, current validity, completion path, input flexibility, keyboard and assistive-technology behavior, and fallback. Test prevention patterns for discoverability, efficiency, locale formats, voice variation, and recovery from automation errors.
- **Boundary:** The chapter's disabled-control example is not a universal recommendation. A visually muted unavailable control may hide what action exists or why it is blocked; external implementation evidence specifically warns that disabled submit controls can confuse visual and screen-reader users.
- **Promotion status:** `qualified-prevention-pattern-candidate; accessible-prerequisite-contract-required`.

### `WID-C39` — explanation must preserve material limits and connect to needed detail

- **Locator:** Chapter 4, PDF pages 92–94.
- **Documented practitioner position:** A concise mobile-deposit-limit message states the threshold but may leave people unsure why it exists or where they can complete the task. The authors propose reconsidering field constraints, layering explanation, and testing the required level of detail rather than treating the assigned slot as fixed.
- **Candidate implication:** Explain the detected condition, applicable limit or requirement, scope, consequence, and what remains possible. When all material detail cannot fit, provide an accessible disclosure path that preserves context and does not make essential recovery information optional or unreachable.
- **Boundary:** More explanation is not automatically more useful, and links or tooltips can fragment attention or fail in constrained modalities. Policy rationale may be unavailable or unsafe to disclose; required information and support alternatives depend on risk and domain.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-layered-error-explanation-candidate; material-detail-and-modality-review-required`.

### `WID-C40` — recovery options must correspond to current state, intent, and actual capability

- **Locator:** Chapter 4, PDF pages 94–97.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Recovery means offering a viable next step toward the original goal, such as retrying automatic capture, taking a photo manually, or reaching an appropriately equipped support specialist. In a chatbot case, repeated nonanswers triggered different guidance and eventual human transfer rather than endless paraphrases of “I don't know.”
- **Candidate implication:** Model retry eligibility, attempt count, changed strategy, alternate channel, escalation destination, handoff context, wait/cost, data preservation, terminal condition, and success feedback. Labels and promises must be generated from current capability and destination availability.
- **Boundary:** Retry can repeat harm, duplicate transactions, lock accounts, or conceal an outage; escalation may be unavailable, inaccessible, costly, or inappropriate. Claims that questions help a system learn require factual data-use and model-update evidence and must not manufacture reassurance.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-recovery-and-escalation-candidate; capability-and-side-effect-controls-required`.

### `WID-C41` — stress-state testing must examine interpretation, action, and life context

- **Locator:** Chapter 4, PDF pages 97–99.
- **Documented practitioner position:** In a medical-product example, noninteractive screen testing exposed alarm, uncertainty, and mistaken beliefs about data loss; revised wording clarified that information was retained. The authors argue that testing should consider effects beyond consistency with the rest of the app.
- **Candidate implication:** Test error candidates with representative stress scenarios, preserved/lost data, time pressure, consequence, likely emotional interpretation, expected next action, and recovery. Separate observed comprehension and affect from inferred long-term impact, and retest in an interactive accessible implementation before release where risk warrants.
- **Boundary:** Static screens do not establish discoverability, focus, announcements, timing, successful recovery, production behavior, or lasting emotional effects. Sensitive medical or crisis research needs qualified protocols, consent, safeguarding, accommodations, and minimization of participant burden.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-stress-state-test-candidate; interactive-and-ethical-validation-required`.

### `WID-C42` — a so-called edge case may reveal exclusionary policy rather than a copy problem

- **Locator:** Chapter 4, PDF pages 98–100.
- **Documented practitioner position:** A registration rule rejecting birth dates more than one hundred years ago could not be repaired honestly with “Please enter a valid birth date.” Reframing rare scenarios as stress cases highlights the real people and business consequences hidden by majority-focused prioritization.
- **Candidate implication:** When a valid person or action is rejected, create a policy/product defect with affected population, rule source, rationale, prevalence uncertainty, severity, legal/accessibility/equity implications, workaround, owner, and release disposition. Prohibit copy that labels valid data invalid merely to mask a system constraint.
- **Boundary:** “Stress case” is a useful design frame, not proof that every rare scenario has equal priority or one solution. Prevalence estimates age, and risk prioritization must consider severity, rights, feasibility, security, safety, law, and affected-party evidence without erasing small populations.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-exclusionary-constraint-escalation-candidate; policy-owner-disposition-required`.

### `WID-C43` — accessibility and inclusive design overlap but are not interchangeable

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF pages 102–104.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The chapter describes accessibility as closing usability gaps experienced by disabled people and inclusive design as a broader orientation that also considers race, age, gender, lived experience, and other dimensions of exclusion. It argues that inclusion should guide work from the outset rather than arrive as a late addition.
- **Candidate implication:** Keep `accessibility`, `inclusive_design`, `equity`, `usability`, and `conformance` as related but distinct scopes with applicable populations, requirements, evidence, owners, and gates. Require early affected-population and exclusion analysis without allowing a broad inclusion claim to satisfy a technical accessibility requirement.
- **Boundary:** The chapter's destination/navigation metaphor is one expert's framing, not a universal taxonomy. Accessibility itself includes social, organizational, environmental, and technical dimensions, and disabled people are not merely gaps relative to a nondisabled baseline.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-scope-separation-candidate; disability-model-review-required`.

### `WID-C44` — scale and revenue arguments can support investment but cannot establish inclusion

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF pages 104–106.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The authors use global disability and LGBTQ+ population estimates and potential lost revenue to counter claims that excluded groups are too small to matter. They present inclusion as both social responsibility and business value.
- **Candidate implication:** A business case may record reachable population, exclusion severity, task value, support burden, market opportunity, and uncertainty, but rights, dignity, access, safety, and applicable obligations remain noncompensatory grounds for action even when population or revenue is small.
- **Boundary:** The cited counts were current around publication and must not be reused as present-day facts without verification. Market size does not prove a design is inclusive, and revenue framing can commodify people, erase intersectionality, or deprioritize those with limited purchasing power.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-inclusion-investment-evidence; rights-not-market-dependent`.

### `WID-C45` — inclusion requires decentering the design team's own baseline

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF pages 105–107 and 114–115.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Teams commonly use their own abilities, identities, and experiences as an implicit model of the user. The chapter asks practitioners to recognize positional limits, question assumptions, learn from people unlike themselves, respond nondefensively to mistakes, and share responsibility rather than making underrepresented colleagues carry all education work.
- **Candidate implication:** Record team positional gaps, populations affected but absent, assumptions, participation method, compensation, decision power, dissent, and learning or correction history. Route material identity and accessibility decisions to affected contributors and qualified specialists with authority, not merely consultation.
- **Boundary:** Positionality disclosure does not cure bias, and no individual represents an entire identity group. Participation can become extractive or tokenizing without consent, compensation, safeguarding, accessible methods, and influence over disposition.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-participatory-inclusion-candidate; anti-tokenism-controls-required`.

### `WID-C46` — permanent, temporary, and situational constraints broaden scenario discovery

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF pages 105–107.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Designs created with disabled people in mind may also help people facing temporary or situational constraints, such as occupied hands or a noisy environment. The chapter uses this spectrum to expand the contexts a team considers.
- **Candidate implication:** Model enduring, episodic, temporary, and situational access constraints across senses, cognition, speech, mobility, environment, device, bandwidth, language, and stress. Use adjacent benefit as secondary evidence while prioritizing the population whose access need drove the requirement.
- **Boundary:** Situational inconvenience is not equivalent to disability or evidence that nondisabled proxy testing represents disabled experience. “Benefits everyone” can erase conflicting needs and should remain a hypothesis scoped to a feature and population.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-scenario-expansion-candidate; no-disability-proxy-substitution`.

### `WID-C47` — personal-data questions require purpose, necessity, inference, and misuse review

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF pages 108–112.
- **Documented practitioner position:** A binary sex field can be ambiguous and exclusionary when people do not know whether it drives biometrics, health features, demographics, advertising, or another purpose. The chapter recommends questioning collection, explaining use, considering downstream assumptions and misuse, and making information optional or more expressive where possible.
- **Candidate implication:** Before requesting identity, sex, gender, disability, health, race, age, or related data, require purpose, necessity, lawful/organizational authority, data recipient, inference and decision use, retention, sharing, misuse threats, response model, `prefer_not_to_answer`, correction/deletion, access controls, and consequence of refusal. Separate fields whose meanings and uses differ.
- **Boundary:** Transparency cannot legitimize unnecessary or harmful collection, and free text is not universally safer: it can increase sensitivity, moderation burden, re-identification, data leakage, invalid inference, or incompatibility with clinical and legal systems. Qualified privacy, security, legal, domain, and affected-community review is required.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-sensitive-data-purpose-candidate; minimization-and-authority-required`.

### `WID-C48` — identity language is preference- and context-dependent and changes over time

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF pages 112–115.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The authors explicitly describe their language guidance as incomplete and time-sensitive. They caution against assuming that one people-first or identity-first construction applies to everyone and urge continued learning as communities and usage evolve.
- **Candidate implication:** Govern identity terms with community/source, self-description preference, audience, locale, domain, date reviewed, contested alternatives, prohibited uses and rationale, individual override, reviewer, and next review trigger. Preserve a correction channel and revision history.
- **Boundary:** A style guide, majority preference, or recent source does not authorize labeling an individual against their stated identity. Preferences vary within groups and across languages, regions, generations, and contexts; safety or privacy may also limit display of self-identification.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-evolving-identity-language-candidate; self-identification-precedence`.

### `WID-C49` — avoid assigning moral value, tragedy, or heroism to embodied traits

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF pages 112–114.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Terms such as confinement, suffering, bravery, and overcoming can portray disability as tragedy or turn ordinary activity into inspiration. The chapter recommends neutral descriptions of access tools, conditions, events, and achievements that do not impose an outsider's emotional judgment.
- **Candidate implication:** Detect unsupported value assignment, pity, infantilization, superlative resilience, cure narratives, and metaphors that treat disability or identity as burden. Prefer the person's own language and describe relevant action, access need, achievement, or system barrier precisely.
- **Boundary:** Neutrality must not erase real pain, discrimination, impairment effects, or a person's chosen account of suffering or pride. Automated substitution cannot determine respectful identity language without speaker, referent, purpose, context, and preference.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-nonjudgmental-identity-language-candidate; individual-context-required`.

### `WID-C50` — describe capability and work rather than prescribing who belongs

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF page 114.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Defining a creative tool as “for” a fixed list of professions can exclude learners, adjacent roles, hobbyists, and people who do not share the organization's category labels. Describing what the product helps people create can keep entry open.
- **Candidate implication:** Review audience labels for hidden eligibility, expertise, employment, purchasing, and identity assumptions. When membership is not truly required, express supported goals and capabilities while retaining role-specific variants where permissions, safety, workflow, or domain meaning materially differ.
- **Boundary:** Capability language is not always clearer, and some products legitimately require licensed roles, age, jurisdiction, authorization, or expertise. Removing a role label must not conceal eligibility or safety boundaries.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-open-capability-framing; legitimate-role-constraints-preserved`.

### `WID-C51` — pronouns are not derivable from gender and should remain correctable

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF pages 114–115.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The authors endorse singular “they,” advise asking pronouns separately from gender only when needed, reject treating pronouns as merely preferred, and recommend allowing people to update them. They also distinguish legal or clinical sex questions from pronoun use.
- **Candidate implication:** Do not infer pronouns from name, honorific, sex, gender, image, voice, or model prediction. If pronouns are needed, store self-supplied values, purpose and visibility, grammatical data required by each locale, change history appropriate to privacy, safe fallback, and correction controls separately from gender and legal/clinical fields.
- **Boundary:** English singular-they guidance does not define grammar or social practice across languages. Free-form and structured pronoun models each have accessibility, localization, privacy, safety, moderation, and rendering tradeoffs; some contexts should avoid collecting or displaying pronouns entirely.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-pronoun-separation-candidate; locale-and-privacy-design-required`.

### `WID-C52` — accessibility standards constrain the complete experience, not writing alone

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF pages 115–118.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The chapter summarizes WCAG's perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust principles and positions clear writing, error handling, alternatives, navigation, predictability, and compatibility as shared design responsibilities. It recommends formal training rather than treating the chapter as sufficient expertise.
- **Candidate implication:** Map each applicable accessibility requirement to content, visual, interaction, semantic/code, platform, assistive-technology, and test evidence with owner and conformance status. Prevent prose quality or an automated scan from producing an overall accessibility approval.
- **Boundary:** The book summarizes WCAG 2.1 as of publication; standards, laws, interpretations, and platform support change and must be verified for product, version, jurisdiction, and required conformance level. Legal claims require qualified current authority.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-cross-layer-accessibility-candidate; current-standard-verification-required`.

### `WID-C53` — screen-reader access depends on meaningful structure and semantic order

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF pages 118–121.
- **Documented practitioner position:** People using screen readers may navigate and skim through headings and interface elements at high speech rates. The authors recommend structured content, necessary context, sequence-based rather than visually dependent instructions, and placing prerequisites before the decision or action they govern.
- **Candidate implication:** Bind headings, labels, descriptions, instructions, prerequisites, controls, focus order, DOM/accessibility-tree order, and announcements into one reading-and-action sequence. Test navigation and comprehension with actual screen readers, browsers, devices, verbosity settings, and representative users.
- **Boundary:** Screen-reader users, strategies, rates, and technologies vary widely; the chapter's average-speed claim and left-to-right/top-to-bottom simplification must not become a persona or parsing rule. CSS visual order, bidirectional and vertical scripts, landmarks, modes, focus, and component semantics can change experienced order.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-semantic-sequence-candidate; assistive-technology-evidence-required`.

### `WID-C54` — color and iconography cannot be the sole carriers of meaning

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF pages 121–123.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Color and icons can be unseen, culturally variable, or interpreted differently across platforms and experience levels. The chapter recommends explicit language or other redundant context so warnings, success, required action, and control purpose remain understandable without the visual cue.
- **Candidate implication:** For every status and action, record visible text, accessible name/description, semantic state, noncolor visual cue, icon meaning, locale/culture review, contrast/forced-colors behavior, and assistive-technology output. Treat color and icons as reinforcement rather than canonical semantics.
- **Boundary:** Adding words alone does not establish contrast, perceivability, programmatic state, focus, announcement, or comprehension. Icons may be more efficient for expert users, and redundant cues must avoid clutter or conflicting accessible names.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-redundant-meaning-candidate; implemented-semantics-required`.

### `WID-C55` — prefer intent-level action language unless physical interaction is material

- **Locator:** Chapter 5, PDF pages 122–123.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Words such as “click,” “tap,” “press,” and “see” assume a device, input, or sensory mode that may not apply. The authors prefer action-oriented terms such as “select” or “view,” while retaining gesture language when the physical behavior itself must be taught.
- **Candidate implication:** Separate semantic action from input-specific instruction. Generate modality-neutral labels by default and add device-, platform-, or assistive-technology-specific operation guidance only when necessary, detected reliably, and kept equivalent across supported paths.
- **Boundary:** “Select,” “choose,” and “view” are not universally interchangeable and can have specific accessibility or component meanings. Voice, switch, keyboard, touch, pointer, game controller, and spatial interfaces may require distinct instructions, localization, and testing.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-intent-versus-input-candidate; modality-specific-variants-supported`.

### `WID-C56` — product voice is a decision system rather than decorative personality

- **Locator:** Chapter 6, PDF pages 126–130.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Voice shapes expectations and gives distributed writers a shared basis for choices about style, structure, grammar, and word choice. The chapter treats finding and maintaining voice as ongoing strategic work rather than adding lively language after the product is designed.
- **Candidate implication:** Represent voice as governed principles linked to product strategy, audience evidence, allowed and prohibited expression, examples, exceptions, applicable surfaces, decision owner, version, and evaluation—not as a free-form persona prompt.
- **Boundary:** A coherent voice does not prove connection, motivation, trust, comprehension, or product quality. Personality language must not impersonate people, obscure automated agency, or override truth, accessibility, safety, consent, legal, localization, and task requirements.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-voice-governance-candidate; outcome-and-agency-evidence-required`.

### `WID-C57` — brand voice and product voice may share lineage while serving different goals

- **Locator:** Chapter 6, PDF pages 130–133.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Brand voice can span marketing, service, corporate communication, and employee behavior, while product voice operates under tighter task, space, concept, and cognitive constraints. They may coincide in a small single-product company or diverge across a large portfolio and distinct audiences.
- **Candidate implication:** Model brand, portfolio, product, channel, feature, and component voice scopes with inheritance, local constraints, conflicts, permitted overrides, and accountable owners. Product clarity and task completion take precedence when a brand expression cannot fit safely.
- **Boundary:** Organizational scale alone does not determine whether voices should differ, and a local team should not silently invent a public identity that conflicts with approved brand, regulatory, support, or cross-product commitments.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-layered-voice-scope-candidate; governed-inheritance-required`.

### `WID-C58` — abstract voice attributes need behavioral bounds and counterexamples

- **Locator:** Chapter 6, PDF pages 133–138 and 146–148.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Adjectives such as friendly, reliable, thoughtful, or spirited become more actionable when paired with an excessive or harmful extreme—“this, but not that”—or expanded into statements with rationale and examples. The negative boundary helps writers see where a desirable trait stops serving the audience.
- **Candidate implication:** For each voice attribute, record intended outcome, positive behaviors, prohibited extreme, lexical and structural cues, valid and invalid examples, surface/state applicability, cultural risk, evidence, and exception. Test whether reviewers can classify unseen examples consistently.
- **Boundary:** Attribute pairs remain subjective and may encode class, age, race, gender, region, neurotype, or corporate norms. A counterword is not a hard safety boundary unless it maps to observable criteria and accountable review.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-operationalized-voice-attribute-candidate; bias-and-reliability-testing-required`.

### `WID-C59` — declarative principles should connect expression to audience and strategy

- **Locator:** Chapter 6, PDF pages 136–139.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Longer declarative principles can explain why a voice behaves a certain way, relate it to customers' context, and reconcile creative identity with business goals. The Mailchimp example subordinates humor to clarity, education, authenticity, and inclusion.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve the underlying `attribute`, `audience_need`, `organization_goal`, `rationale`, `expression`, `limit`, and `evidence` fields even when publishing a concise prose principle. Require humor and delight to pass comprehension, inclusion, risk, and task-value checks.
- **Boundary:** A polished rationale can rationalize brand preference after the fact and does not establish audience need or outcome. Historical company examples are time-bound reference evidence, not transferable rules or proof of effectiveness.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-voice-principle-projection; underlying-evidence-remains-canonical`.

### `WID-C60` — practical voice rules should evolve from reviewed product evidence

- **Locator:** Chapter 6, PDF pages 138–140 and 145–146.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Teams can translate principles into choices such as contractions, sentence rhythm, or avoiding idioms, then review successful and off-voice examples, discuss why they fit, and update shared documentation. Guidance is valuable insofar as it influences real decisions.
- **Candidate implication:** Capture rule provenance, example version and state, review participants, audience/locale, observed result, disagreement, decision, adoption, exception, and supersession. Measure guideline use, consistency, defects, comprehension, and outcomes rather than document polish or page views alone.
- **Boundary:** Reading patterns from a few “successful” messages risks survivorship and confirmation bias; perceived voice consistency is not causal evidence. Contractions, cadence, idioms, and naturalness vary across languages, literacy contexts, speech synthesis, and communication styles.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-evolving-voice-evidence-candidate; causal-humility-required`.

### `WID-C61` — clear, concise, and human form a priority order, not a compensatory score

- **Locator:** Chapter 6, PDF pages 140–143.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The authors place clarity first, concision second when it does not remove needed meaning, and a natural or empathetic quality third. They advise distilling unnecessary concepts and information rather than merely deleting words, and reject slang or idioms that narrow access.
- **Candidate implication:** Use `clear`, `semantically_complete`, `economical`, and `relational_fit` as separate review dimensions with precedence and hard constraints. Never allow warmth, brevity, brand fit, or a high aggregate score to compensate for false, incomplete, inaccessible, manipulative, unsafe, or unrecoverable content.
- **Boundary:** “Human” is underspecified and can encourage anthropomorphism, informality, ableist judgments about communication, or one-culture conversational norms. Clarity and concision require audience/task testing rather than author confidence.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-priority-frame-candidate; human-language-requires-definition`.

### `WID-C62` — named personalization and friendly flourishes are optional hypotheses

- **Locator:** Chapter 6, PDF pages 141–143.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Product examples use an avatar, a person's name, contractions, and a brief social flourish to create friendliness after the task and recovery information is clear. The authors present these as manifestations of voice rather than requirements.
- **Candidate implication:** Treat name insertion, avatar use, greetings, praise, celebration, and closing flourishes as independently testable options with purpose, data source, consent/expectation, pronunciation and rendering, privacy exposure, locale, frequency, risk state, and fallback.
- **Boundary:** Personalization can expose identity on shared devices, use stale or unsafe names, misgender, feel surveillant, increase cognitive load, or imply a relationship the organization has not earned. A decorative phrase still consumes attention and translation space.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-relational-expression; privacy-and-context-review-required`.

### `WID-C63` — voice must change as audience, culture, product, and context change

- **Locator:** Chapter 6, PDF pages 143–145.
- **Documented practitioner position:** A culturally specific rhetorical joke that worked for an early audience confused a fluent English speaker without the same television reference. As products reach broader populations, teams should test and revise language whose idiom or cultural frame no longer travels.
- **Candidate implication:** Version voice guidance with audience and market coverage, locale, cultural references, product maturity, evidence date, known failures, deprecation triggers, and migration impact. Test meaning and emotional effect through localization rather than assuming English fluency implies shared culture.
- **Boundary:** The anecdote demonstrates one interpretation, not population-wide failure, and “translatable” cannot be inferred from an author's intuition. Localization may require transcreation, removal, or a different voice manifestation while preserving intent.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-voice-lifecycle-and-localization-candidate; locale-evidence-required`.

### `WID-C64` — scaling voice requires bounded flexibility and plural review

- **Locator:** Chapter 6, PDF pages 145–149.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Too few rules leave voice abstract, while too many can paralyze contributors. The authors recommend documented examples, caveated rules, feedback from communicators with varied experiences and roles, reading aloud, rationale in design work, and collaborative editing.
- **Candidate implication:** Provide layered guidance: invariant constraints, default principles, component/state patterns, examples, exceptions, escalation, and contribution workflow. Track reviewer coverage, inter-reviewer disagreement, exception frequency, unresolved conflicts, and who can approve changes.
- **Boundary:** Cross-functional review is not a substitute for users, affected communities, translators, accessibility specialists, domain experts, or runtime testing. “Diverse” participation must be specified rather than inferred from job-title variety.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-scalable-voice-governance-candidate; decision-rights-and-coverage-required`.

### `WID-C65` — reading aloud is a diagnostic technique, not validation

- **Locator:** Chapter 6, PDF pages 145–146.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Hearing a draft can expose awkward cadence, pacing, or mismatch with intended voice. The chapter presents this as a lightweight review practice alongside broader feedback and documentation.
- **Candidate implication:** Support read-aloud review with locale and voice used, detected issue, revision, and reviewer, while separately testing screen-reader output, synthesized speech, voice interfaces, comprehension, and task behavior where applicable.
- **Boundary:** An author's own oral performance can smooth over ambiguous punctuation, pronunciation, hierarchy, or inaccessible implementation. Naturalness to one speaker does not establish readability, voice fit, localization quality, or accessibility.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-auditory-diagnostic; no-validation-authority`.

### `WID-C66` — brand expression should recede when urgency, impairment, or risk dominates

- **Locator:** Chapter 6, PDF pages 146–150.
- **Documented practitioner position:** In accident reporting and roadside assistance, a playful insurance brand becomes restrained, supportive, and goal-directed. The authors ask teams to balance engagement with speed, likely state of mind, and the value of a brand moment, including the possibility that the best voice is plain and nearly invisible.
- **Candidate implication:** Define state-specific maximum voice intensity using urgency, consequence, user control, impairment likelihood, emotional exposure, familiarity, task complexity, and recovery need. Suppress humor, exclamation, self-reference, metaphor, promotion, and unnecessary personalization in high-risk or distress states unless affected-user evidence supports them.
- **Boundary:** Teams cannot reliably infer an individual's emotion from state alone, and restraint is not synonymous with coldness. Crisis, medical, financial, legal, safety, grief, abuse, and accessibility contexts require specialized protocols and testing beyond a generic tone dial.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-risk-calibrated-voice-candidate; specialized-state-governance-required`.

### `WID-C67` — tone is a contextual expression of voice, not a second personality

- **Locator:** Chapter 7, PDF pages 152–154.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Voice distinguishes the product or brand, while tone changes how that voice is expressed in a particular workflow or interaction. Tone can alter word choice, message structure, explanation, directness, and benefit framing while retaining a recognizable underlying identity.
- **Candidate implication:** Store stable voice principles separately from a tone decision bound to product state, task, actor, journey position, severity, and message purpose. Trace each surface expression to both layers and record which constraint wins when they conflict.
- **Boundary:** The voice/tone distinction is a practitioner model, not a linguistic universal. Consistency of identity must not force stylistic continuity where safety, accessibility, legal, cultural, or role requirements require a different expression.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-voice-tone-separation-candidate; constraint-precedence-required`.

### `WID-C68` — digital tone is multidimensional rather than a single intensity dial

- **Locator:** Chapter 7, PDF pages 154–159.
- **Documented practitioner position:** A marketing “volume” scale measures how strongly brand expression appears, but interactive products must also respond to journey stage, experience, intent, receptiveness, stress, UI type, next step, and the product's own goal. The chapter proposes switching among contextual channels rather than merely turning personality up or down.
- **Candidate implication:** Represent tone as a profile of independently governed dimensions—directness, warmth, formality, urgency, explanation, celebration, reassurance, agency, and brand salience—plus applicability and prohibited combinations. Do not reduce tone to one numeric sentiment or expressiveness score.
- **Boundary:** Several listed user dimensions are hypotheses that a system may not know. Experience, intent, receptiveness, mood, and stress require evidence or conservative defaults and must not be inferred from sensitive data or behavioral proxies without purpose, authority, and harm review.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-multidimensional-tone-candidate; no-unsupported-emotion-inference`.

### `WID-C69` — tone can affect usability, but source evidence must retain construct and scope

- **Locator:** Chapter 7, PDF pages 154–157.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The chapter cites a factor-analysis study in which customer-service behavior—including friendliness, politeness, speaking pace, and familiar terms—was among important interface-usability dimensions. It uses this research to argue that tone merits evidence-based product attention rather than preference debate.
- **Candidate implication:** Store external tone findings with publication, population, modality, items, construct, method, effect or factor result, date, replication status, and transfer limits. Convert findings into local research questions and candidate profiles rather than universal tone rules.
- **Boundary:** A factor's relative importance does not show that a particular phrase causes task success, nor that friendliness is always desirable. The chapter does not provide enough methodological detail to validate broad claims across current visual, text, conversational, cultural, or high-risk interfaces.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-tone-research-evidence; construct-and-transfer-review-required`.

### `WID-C70` — conversational inspiration does not create a human relationship or emotional knowledge

- **Locator:** Chapter 7, PDF pages 157–161.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Interface exchanges can draw on the rhythm, contextual responsiveness, and trust-supporting behavior of interpersonal conversation, while the authors explicitly state that software does not have the same relationship as a friend and should not presume how someone feels.
- **Candidate implication:** Permit conversation-derived techniques only with explicit system identity, capability limits, interaction history, known state, and user control. Separate `observed_state`, `reported_emotion`, `possible_interpretation`, and `desired_delivery`; default to nonpresumptive wording.
- **Boundary:** Mirroring, relational cues, empathy language, and first-person claims can manipulate trust or suggest consciousness, care, understanding, confidentiality, or memory the system does not possess. Sensitive uses require deception, dependency, privacy, safety, and escalation review.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-conversational-boundary-candidate; agency-and-emotion-truth-required`.

### `WID-C71` — neutral, task-focused writing is an intentional tone default

- **Locator:** Chapter 7, PDF pages 160–162.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Friendly names, praise, greetings, and exclamation marks do not inherently improve engagement. For much routine transactional writing, a short, neutral style that helps people complete the workflow is an appropriate strategy rather than an absence of tone.
- **Candidate implication:** Define a low-expression informational default for states lacking evidence that stronger relational language helps. Any deviation should identify its intended user benefit, risk, locale behavior, frequency, test evidence, and rollback condition.
- **Boundary:** Neutrality is culturally situated and can read as cold, bureaucratic, threatening, or evasive; terse text may also omit necessary explanation. The default still requires audience, accessibility, domain, and consequence review.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-neutral-default-candidate; neutral-is-not-context-free`.

### `WID-C72` — tone profiles begin with interface, actor, and scenario evidence

- **Locator:** Chapter 7, PDF pages 161–164 and 171–172.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The chapter's profile method examines what the interface seeks to accomplish and convey, what a person might experience or misinterpret, and the UI type, journey position, preceding situation, and next step. Profiles are intended to turn subjective craft into a repeatable strategic starting point for testing.
- **Candidate implication:** A tone-profile record should include actor/role, task, product state, trigger and prior event, message purpose, known facts, plausible stress cases, next state/action, prohibited assumptions, tone dimensions, candidate examples, evidence, owner, and test plan.
- **Boundary:** An archetype, journey map, or team workshop is not observation of a person's state of mind. Profiles must distinguish actor facts from hypotheses and cannot collapse conflicting audiences, bystanders, support staff, or people affected downstream.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-tone-profile-candidate; evidence-types-separated`.

### `WID-C73` — an audit is the review process and an inventory is its versioned evidence artifact

- **Locator:** Chapter 7, PDF pages 163–169.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Teams should select representative workflows with an explicit learning goal, inspect all messaging from small labels through complex dialogs, capture each example and the actions leading to it, and preserve the resulting inventory. The chapter distinguishes the act of evaluating from the artifact produced.
- **Candidate implication:** Store audit goal, scope and exclusions, actors, workflow/build/locale/channel, sampling rationale, reviewer, criteria, findings and dispositions separately from an immutable inventory of message instances, locators, screenshots or snapshots, state, provenance, and version.
- **Boundary:** A representative sample can miss rare, personalized, permission-dependent, asynchronous, inaccessible, stale, or failure states. An inventory documents existence, not correctness, reachability, frequency, or user impact.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-audit-inventory-separation-candidate; coverage-evidence-required`.

### `WID-C74` — tone auditing must include all actors and state transitions in the service

- **Locator:** Chapter 7, PDF pages 163–169.
- **Documented practitioner position:** A food-delivery experience includes customers, couriers, and restaurants, each with different interfaces and relationships. Stage directions showing how a message is reached help reveal tone as part of a workflow rather than an isolated string.
- **Candidate implication:** Map tone across actor handoffs, permissions, channels, triggers, ordering, delays, notifications, failures, appeals, and consequences. Preserve shared event identity while allowing role-appropriate expressions and checking whether one actor's reassurance conceals another actor's burden.
- **Boundary:** A service map does not prove all affected parties are users or that their goals align. Workers, recipients, bystanders, administrators, regulators, and support personnel may require separate research and power analysis.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-multi-actor-tone-map-candidate; cross-actor-harm-review-required`.

### `WID-C75` — cleverness is unsafe when it depends on shame, cultural context, or assumed receptiveness

- **Locator:** Chapter 7, PDF pages 169–171.
- **Documented practitioner position:** A culturally timed food message can be funny to some readers yet shame assumed eating behavior or become opaque without the reference. The chapter asks reviewers to flag messages that are out of context, unempathetic, exclusionary, idiomatic, or unready for translation.
- **Candidate implication:** Review expressive candidates for target and collateral interpretations, body/food/health shame, identity stereotypes, trauma and stress contexts, cultural prerequisite, literal translation, humor target, power relationship, and safe fallback. Fail closed for ridicule or blame directed at the person.
- **Boundary:** Team members cannot determine empathy or offensiveness solely by intention, and avoiding every possible negative reading is impossible. Use affected-audience and locale evidence, severity, likelihood, rights, and reversibility rather than subjective veto or majority taste.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-expressive-risk-review-candidate; affected-audience-evidence-required`.

### `WID-C76` — writing one semantic message in several tones exposes mismatch and invariant meaning

- **Locator:** Chapter 7, PDF pages 172–175.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The authors rewrite a forced-password-reset message as encouraging, informational, trustworthy, and sympathetic. Comparing the variants makes excessive familiarity or emotional intensity visible and suggests that informational or security-oriented delivery better fits the inconvenience.
- **Candidate implication:** Generate controlled tone variants from one locked semantic contract, preserving cause, requirement, action, constraints, consequence, and security truth. Compare tone fit, comprehension, trust, blame, urgency, localization, accessibility, and task behavior without allowing variants to change material meaning.
- **Boundary:** Author readback cannot establish which variant users prefer or understand, and the underlying ninety-day password policy may itself be outdated or inappropriate. Tone exploration must not legitimize an unsupported policy or security claim.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-controlled-tone-variation-candidate; semantics-and-policy-held-separate`.

### `WID-C77` — tone patterns are hypotheses until linked to contextual outcomes

- **Locator:** Chapter 7, PDF pages 175–177.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Looking across a workflow may reveal repeated confusion, relief after completion, or situations where an assumed mindset produced a poor interpretation. The authors propose mapping recurring profiles such as motivational, useful, reassuring, supportive, and predominantly informational, then translating them into tactical guidance.
- **Candidate implication:** For each proposed pattern, preserve source instances, coverage, counterexamples, actor and state, observed versus inferred response, intended outcome, candidate rule, test, result, and disposition. Keep informational delivery as a common baseline while allowing product-specific profiles.
- **Boundary:** Similar messages do not prove a stable psychological segment, and “happy,” “stressed,” or “new” users are not fixed personas. A tone taxonomy must not become emotional classification, discriminatory personalization, or a substitute for correcting confusing behavior.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-tone-pattern-candidate; no-psychological-profiling`.

### `WID-C78` — tone frameworks trade specificity for adaptability and require lifecycle governance

- **Locator:** Chapter 7, PDF pages 176–178.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Detailed frameworks can become rigid around stress cases and outliers, while broad frameworks invite inconsistent interpretation. The chapter recommends regular review for new features, missing contexts, obsolete profiles, and actual usefulness rather than assuming one visualization or cadence fits every organization.
- **Candidate implication:** Version each framework with scope, coverage matrix, ambiguity tests, exception path, adoption evidence, owner, review trigger, deprecated profiles, migration notes, and unresolved gaps. Prefer event-driven review after product, audience, locale, policy, incident, or evidence change over a calendar-only cadence.
- **Boundary:** Quarterly review is an example, not a universal interval, and framework use does not prove better communication. Governance should be proportional to risk and must permit abstention when no profile fits safely.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-tone-lifecycle-candidate; event-driven-review-and-abstention-required`.

### `WID-C79` — content work creates user value only when implemented in the resolved product

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 180–181; conclusion, PDF page 209.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Writing deliverables, professional recognition, and sound advice have no direct value to users unless the resulting decisions are implemented in the experience. The authors ask practitioners to design their role and process around effects on the final product rather than artifact production.
- **Candidate implication:** Trace every accepted content decision through implementation owner, canonical resource, locale, component/state binding, build, release, observed runtime, and outcome evidence. Track unimplemented, altered, stale, and superseded decisions explicitly.
- **Boundary:** Implementation does not establish correctness, accessibility, usefulness, responsibility, or causal benefit. A harmful or unsupported recommendation should not ship merely to demonstrate content-team impact.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-decision-to-runtime-lineage-candidate; implementation-is-not-validation`.

### `WID-C80` — role and operating model should fit organizational need without hiding authority

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 180–184 and 189–190.
- **Documented practitioner position:** UX writing may be a specialist role or one responsibility among several, and organizations may support one practitioner, a large team, or a distributed community. The chapter rejects one correct title or contribution model and encourages practitioners to shape the role around local needs.
- **Candidate implication:** Model responsibilities per artifact and decision: authoring, research, factual verification, terminology, accessibility, localization, legal/domain review, implementation, approval, release, and maintenance. Allow centralized, embedded, federated, and hybrid operating views while retaining one accountable owner for each decision.
- **Boundary:** Local fit cannot justify missing competencies, segregation of duties, or ambiguous accountability. Professional self-definition does not grant legal, research, domain, safety, accessibility, engineering, or release authority.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-contextual-operating-model-candidate; explicit-decision-rights-required`.

### `WID-C81` — meeting team needs can build trust but must not displace affected-user needs

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 181–183.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Contributing to immediate writing needs and learning teammates' pressures can establish common ground for earlier, more strategic participation. The authors distinguish useful contribution from producing an impressive deliverable no one needs.
- **Candidate implication:** Intake should capture requestor need, affected-user need, urgency, current burden, strategic opportunity, risk, minimum safe assistance, and follow-up. Measure resolved problems and reduced duplication rather than artifact count, responsiveness, or stakeholder goodwill alone.
- **Boundary:** Trust-building can normalize a copy-only service model or pressure practitioners to ship unsafe shortcuts. Team satisfaction and internal urgency cannot override user evidence, truth, consent, accessibility, safety, or accountable review.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-trust-building-intake; noncompensatory-user-controls`.

### `WID-C82` — consultation demand can reveal systemic problems without proving their prevalence

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 183–184.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Open office hours gave one practitioner broad exposure to recurring product-language problems, duplicated effort, and potential priorities, while short sessions rarely resolved the immediate problem fully. The practice also built relationships and support for team growth.
- **Candidate implication:** Record consultations as intake evidence with requester, product/state, issue class, risk, resolution depth, recurrence key, referral, follow-up, and coverage limits. Aggregate repeated needs into candidate systemic work while preserving distinct local contexts.
- **Boundary:** Self-selected requests reflect awareness, access, and organizational networks, not the full product landscape or user population. Request volume, relationships, and resulting headcount do not prove user benefit or severity.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-consultation-signal; sampling-and-outcome-limits-required`.

### `WID-C83` — writing workflow includes discovery, dependencies, review, and decision latency

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 184–185.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Planning, unresolved facts, stakeholder and legal review, research, meetings, and other dependencies are part of content design, not overhead outside “the writing.” Exposing blockers and accounting for this work can prevent late, reactionary changes.
- **Candidate implication:** A content-work item should declare inputs, unknowns, dependencies, research, reviewers and authority, decision deadlines, accessibility/localization work, implementation, validation, and release path. Block factual or consequential assertions when required evidence or decisions are absent.
- **Boundary:** More process does not necessarily improve outcomes, and early planning cannot eliminate change. Workflow should be proportional to risk and support expedited paths with explicit residual risk, authority, rollback, and retrospective review.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-content-workflow-candidate; proportional-gates-required`.

### `WID-C84` — early participation should be justified by decision relevance, not meeting access

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 185–187.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Practitioners can request to observe, explain downstream rework risk, or offer facilitation when language-relevant decisions occur before they are included. The tactics aim to make the contribution legible and useful rather than wait for automatic invitation.
- **Candidate implication:** Map decision events to required competencies and notify or invite roles based on affected contracts, risk, and authority. Preserve agenda, requested contribution, decision, dissent, follow-up, and any reason participation was constrained.
- **Boundary:** Meeting attendance is a weak proxy for influence or quality, and self-invitation can conflict with confidentiality, privacy, labor boundaries, safety, or efficient decision-making. No discipline needs presence in every meeting.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-decision-relevance-routing; attendance-is-not-outcome`.

### `WID-C85` — unresolved questions should be visible, typed, owned, and consequential

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 186–189.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Publishing questions and marking work blocked can show teammates that writing depends on product goals, prior state, control behavior, user understanding, business outcomes, measurement, and intended behavior. Questions expose ambiguity that a quick “wordsmithing” request conceals.
- **Candidate implication:** Store each question with subject, assertion type, why it matters, source needed, accountable answer owner, decision affected, due state, response and evidence, confidence, and resolution. Prevent an unanswered question from silently becoming model-generated product fact.
- **Boundary:** The number of questions is not evidence of rigor, and a practitioner should not block unrelated work without delegated authority and proportional risk. Some ambiguity is irreducible and should produce bounded language, safe fallback, or abstention rather than endless review.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-question-and-unknown-governance-candidate; authority-and-proportionality-required`.

### `WID-C86` — communities of practice support learning but are not approval bodies by default

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 189–191.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Physical or digital spaces can connect distributed people who care about product writing, enable questions and peer support, and reduce isolation even when few specialists exist. The authors recommend designing these spaces for accessible participation and practical value.
- **Candidate implication:** Define community purpose, membership, accessibility, moderation, confidentiality, artifact retention, response expectations, escalation, and relationship to canonical governance. Capture reusable learning with provenance and route decisions to authorized owners.
- **Boundary:** A chat channel, office hour, or enthusiastic community does not create consensus, review coverage, representation, or decision authority. Informal spaces can expose confidential work, amplify dominant voices, and exclude people across time zones, languages, disabilities, or employment status.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-community-of-practice; noncanonical-by-default`.

### `WID-C87` — facilitation separates problem agreement, objectives, and solution design

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 190–194.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The chapter proposes documenting an evidenced problem, surfacing differing interpretations, agreeing on shared objectives, and then designing against them. Worksheets and visible artifacts can reduce defensiveness and make incomplete discovery apparent.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve proposed problem, evidence, stakeholder interpretations, affected parties, disagreement, agreed objective, exclusions, decision authority, candidate solutions, and evaluation. Treat a canvas as a projection over these records rather than the canonical truth.
- **Boundary:** Agreement improves coordination but cannot prove the problem, user need, ethical legitimacy, or solution effectiveness. Facilitation can conceal agendas or power asymmetry, especially when the facilitator's private goal shapes the activity without transparency.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-facilitated-decision-workflow-candidate; consensus-is-not-evidence`.

### `WID-C88` — maps, flows, and interface mockups are complementary context views

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 194–197.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Object maps expose entities and non-linear state visibility, flows expose branching and turn-taking, and interface designs show words with visual and interactive context. Visualizing work helps collaborators orient to systems and inspect language beyond isolated strings.
- **Candidate implication:** Generate linked views from shared canonical entities, states, transitions, messages, actors, surfaces, and evidence. Version each view and record coverage, omissions, assumptions, and source snapshot so it cannot silently diverge from implementation.
- **Boundary:** Visual clarity and stakeholder recall do not establish model completeness or runtime truth. Static mockups omit timing, permissions, async behavior, personalization, accessibility trees, responsive states, localization, failure, and real data unless explicitly represented and tested.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-multiview-artifact-candidate; canonical-model-remains-separate`.

### `WID-C89` — critique should be early, goal-bound, scoped, and psychologically safe

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 197–198.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Useful critique focuses on user and organizational objectives rather than taste, happens while change remains affordable, lets creators request a feedback scope, and treats the work rather than the person as the object of evaluation. Asynchronous comments can widen participation.
- **Candidate implication:** A critique record should include artifact/version, readiness, requested scope, criteria, participants and roles, accessibility/accommodation, comments classified as evidence/question/preference/risk, creator response, disposition, decision owner, and unresolved dissent.
- **Boundary:** Creator-defined scope cannot exclude mandatory safety, accessibility, legal, privacy, or domain issues. Psychological safety, asynchronous participation, and diverse perspectives require active facilitation and cannot be inferred from tool availability.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-critique-governance-candidate; mandatory-risk-review-preserved`.

### `WID-C90` — consistency is beneficial only after strategy and pattern validity

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 198–200.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Reusing effective approaches can reduce duplicated effort and recognition cost, but consistency without strategy scales the wrong solution more quickly. The chapter positions consistency as a conditional consequence of validated purpose rather than an absolute virtue.
- **Candidate implication:** Require concept, purpose, user/task evidence, behavior equivalence, applicable states, accessibility, locale, and success/guardrail evidence before promoting a repeated solution. Track exceptions and fork rationale instead of forcing lexical sameness.
- **Boundary:** Consistency can conflict with role, platform, modality, locale, expertise, or changing evidence. Repetition itself does not prove a pattern is correct, and inconsistency may expose a legitimate semantic difference.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-conditional-consistency-candidate; semantic-equivalence-over-string-match`.

### `WID-C91` — reusable patterns encode design problems and decisions, not frozen strings

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 199–202.
- **Documented practitioner position:** A conversational intent-recognition pattern became more reusable when documented around low-confidence understanding and user correction rather than one prompt, button label, or component. Different answer length and branch complexity required different expressions and controls.
- **Candidate implication:** Pattern records should define problem, intent, prerequisites, state/confidence, semantic obligations, supported variants, selection logic, behavior, recovery, accessibility, locales, evidence, anti-patterns, and exceptions. Expressions remain versioned examples or generated projections.
- **Boundary:** Abstract patterns can become vague and may conceal model calibration, disclosure, security, accessibility, or component constraints. Confidence language and correction paths require runtime evidence and must not imply learning or understanding beyond capability.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-semantic-pattern-candidate; runtime-and-component-bindings-required`.

### `WID-C92` — style guides resolve mechanics but cannot govern whole interactions

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 202–203.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Adopting an existing style guide can settle recurring capitalization, punctuation, spelling, and word-choice questions more efficiently than authoring one from scratch. Interface decisions nevertheless exceed traditional editorial mechanics because words participate in components and behavior.
- **Candidate implication:** Treat style guides as versioned mechanics and terminology sources with scope, locale, exceptions, precedence, and provenance. Apply them after semantic, factual, behavioral, accessibility, safety, and domain obligations are satisfied.
- **Boundary:** The named digital guide and edition recommendations are time-bound, and “pick one and stick to it” cannot override current platform, locale, legal, accessibility, or product requirements. A guide's authority depends on explicit adoption.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-style-source-boundary; mechanics-subordinate-to-contract`.

### `WID-C93` — design-system components need purpose, content, behavior, and implementation together

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 203–206.
- **Documented practitioner position:** A design system combines strategic direction, verbal and visual style, reusable patterns, assets, and code. Component purpose should determine structure, content, presentation, and permissible modification; words give controls and states meaning rather than decorating completed visual blocks.
- **Candidate implication:** A component contract should include purpose/anti-purpose, semantic roles, content slots and obligations, states/transitions, actions/consequences, variables, accessible name/description and announcements, visual/interaction/code implementation, locale behavior, examples, tests, ownership, version, and deprecation.
- **Boundary:** Co-location in one website does not guarantee parity among design, content, code, documentation, and runtime. “Single source of truth” should mean governed identities and verified transformations, not one tool claiming authority over every layer.

### `WID-C94` — a design system is an internal product whose adoption requires user evidence

- **Locator:** Chapter 8, PDF pages 205–208.
- **Documented practitioner position:** Shopify reorganized component documentation after testing showed that developers needed visual and code information sooner while retaining deeper UX and content guidance. The chapter recommends starting with high-value inconsistencies, listening to contributor needs, building shared cross-disciplinary terminology, and expanding only when the system proves useful.
- **Candidate implication:** Govern the system with contributor populations and jobs, research, information architecture, search/findability, contribution and review workflow, ownership, releases, changelog, deprecation/migration, support, telemetry, adoption depth, exception burden, parity tests, and user/product outcomes. Pilot a bounded problem before expansion.
- **Boundary:** Adoption, visits, component reuse, and workshop agreement do not prove end-user quality; teams may adopt a system because it is mandatory. Internal research must include content, design, engineering, accessibility, localization, product, and downstream maintainers without treating any one role as universal.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-design-system-as-product-candidate; adoption-and-outcome-separated`.

### `WID-C95` — the book offers adaptable practitioner ideas, not strict executable rules

- **Locator:** Conclusion, PDF page 209; Introduction, PDF pages 13–17.
- **Documented practitioner position:** The authors close by asking readers to adapt the methods to their teams and people rather than treat the book as a fixed rule set. They frame product-language decisions as capable of supporting clarity and usefulness or enabling deceit and manipulation.
- **Candidate implication:** Retain all book-derived rules as source-scoped candidates with applicability, evidence, conflicts, exceptions, authority, and abstention. Cross-book and repository synthesis must preserve ethical alternatives and affected-party consequences.
- **Boundary:** Adaptability cannot waive factual, legal, safety, privacy, accessibility, localization, domain, or release requirements. “User first” is not sufficient when purchasers, workers, bystanders, subjects, or other affected people have conflicting rights and needs.

## Book-level synthesis

The complete book contributes a coherent practitioner model rather than a universal standard:

1. Writing participates in problem definition, strategy, research, behavior, expression, evaluation, implementation, and maintenance rather than beginning with an assigned string.
2. Usability, usefulness, and responsibility remain distinct; truth, agency, inclusion, consent, and foreseeable misuse cannot be traded for stylistic success.
3. Clarity is a contextual claim about a particular audience, task, product state, and consequence—not a word-count, reading-time, plain-language, or consistency score.
4. Error handling begins with the interrupted goal and separates prevention, explanation, recovery, retry, escalation, preserved work, and stress-case policy defects.
5. Inclusion requires affected participation, identity and sensitive-data governance, evolving language, and disability-led evidence; accessible prose cannot prove accessible implementation.
6. Voice is a governed product identity, while tone adapts delivery to known state without claiming emotional knowledge or a human relationship.
7. Audits, inventories, profiles, maps, flows, critiques, and guidelines are versioned views and collaboration artifacts; none is automatically canonical product truth.
8. Reusable patterns and design systems must bind semantic purpose, content, behavior, components, accessibility, locale, code, governance, adoption, and outcomes.
9. Role and organizational form may vary, but decision authority, evidence types, runtime lineage, maintenance, and noncompensatory controls must remain explicit.

The book's distinctive value is its sustained insistence that words are a design material and that copy problems often expose product, policy, data, or organizational problems. Its limitations include practitioner anecdotes presented without full study detail, company examples that are now historical, English- and U.S.-centered guidance, simplified legal and technical summaries, broad emotional and business-effect claims, and interpersonal metaphors that can overstate what software knows or is. Those limitations are retained rather than silently promoted.

## Comparison with `BOOK-UXW-001`

The opening thesis strongly corroborates the completed *Strategic Writing for UX* synthesis: UX writing begins before strings, can expose behavior and product-strategy problems, requires separate user and organizational goals, and participates in research, interaction, evaluation, and cross-functional decisions. Both books reject title-based ownership and copy-only repair.

This book adds sharper early emphasis on usefulness, responsibility, separable consent choices, foreseeable misuse, and the quiet persuasive power of product categories. Its “usable, useful, responsible” triad is a useful human-facing review projection, but the repository model must retain more granular noncompensatory controls for truth, accessibility, safety, privacy, autonomy, localization, domain authority, and runtime behavior.

The comparison also reveals a terminology difference worth preserving: *Writing Is Designing* uses “user” throughout its opening, while the first book often says “person.” Neither label should become an automatic canonical audience term. Adopted terminology should follow product context, audience preference, domain norms, and inclusive research.

Chapter 2 closely corroborates `BOOK-UXW-001` on separate user and organizational goals, collaborative strategy, research questions, interviews, contextual observation, usability tests, and evidence-bound terminology. It adds a compact strategy-statement workshop and an especially clear warning against substituting internal opinions for user evidence. The stronger combined model keeps workshop outputs as assertions, treats research plans and methods as governed artifacts, preserves participant-language provenance, and separates isolated content diagnostics from whole-experience and causal evidence.

Chapter 3 corroborates the first book's distinction between semantic completeness and surface economy: brevity, consistency, plain language, and familiar metaphors are conditional techniques rather than independent proof of clarity. It adds a concrete path from message decomposition through flow inspection, consequence prioritization, question/control alignment, and user testing. The combined model therefore treats clarity as a versioned claim about an audience performing a task in a resolved state, while keeping truth, behavior, accessibility, localization, privacy, harm, and authority as separate constraints.

Chapter 4 strongly corroborates `BOOK-UXW-001` on state/cause/action/recovery structure, nonblaming language, preserving people's goals, treating content defects as possible product or policy defects, and testing adverse states rather than only happy paths. Its memorable `avoid / explain / resolve` frame becomes a useful review projection, while the combined model retains distinct runtime cause confidence, security disclosure, preserved work, retry side effects, escalation availability, accessible association and announcement, success confirmation, and policy disposition. External implementation documentation reviewed for this slice further constrains the book's examples: visual cues and helpful prose do not prove that an error is programmatically associated, announced at the right time, reachable by focus, or recoverable with assistive technology.

Chapter 5 corroborates `BOOK-UXW-001` on accessibility as an implementation property, inclusive research, identity-safe language, sensitive-data purpose, multimodal meaning, semantic order, and continuous governance. It adds especially useful prompts for separating pronouns, gender, and sex; interrogating demographic collection; and avoiding outsider-assigned tragedy or heroism. The combined model is stricter than either chapter-level checklist: it separates broad inclusive intent from WCAG conformance, individual self-identification from group guidance, adjacent situational benefits from disability evidence, and readable prose from implemented semantics and assistive-technology behavior. Time-bound legal, population, language, and technology claims remain research inputs rather than canonical rules.

Chapter 6 corroborates `BOOK-UXW-001` on voice as a governed, evolving system tied to strategy, audience, product state, and evidence rather than a static list of adjectives. It adds practical attribute formats, brand/product scope separation, and a strong illustration of brand recession under urgency. The combined model preserves its `clear / concise / human` priority as a review view but replaces “human” with more observable relational qualities, prevents brand fit from compensating for semantic or safety failures, versions every rule and example, and requires localization, accessibility, privacy, and affected-user evidence before voice patterns become reusable.

Chapter 7 corroborates `BOOK-UXW-001` on tone as contextual behavior generated from state, actor, message purpose, and risk rather than sentiment decoration. It adds a useful audit-to-inventory-to-profile workflow and controlled rewriting exercise. The combined model is more conservative about the chapter's interpersonal analogy: software may adapt delivery to known circumstances but cannot know emotion, mirror a relationship, or claim empathy. It keeps semantic content invariant across tone variants, distinguishes observed product state from hypothesized interpretation, includes all service actors, and binds every profile to accessibility, localization, evidence, exception, and lifecycle controls.

Chapter 8 corroborates `BOOK-UXW-001` on early content participation, visible unknowns, explicit workflow, critique, reusable semantic patterns, content-aware components, and design systems treated as maintained products. It adds grounded tactics for building trust and participation, but the combined model does not treat goodwill, meeting access, consensus, community activity, or adoption as proof. It requires typed decision authority, immutable inventories, canonical cross-layer identities, contribution and release governance, deprecation and migration, runtime parity, and separate internal-adoption and affected-user outcome evidence.

No implementation change is authorized by this completed ledger.

## Open questions

1. What minimum semantic and behavioral evidence lets the system determine that a problem cannot be solved safely with wording alone?
2. How should `usable`, `useful`, and `responsible` project onto smaller typed criteria without becoming a compensatory score?
3. Which product choices must always be represented as separate consent or preference decisions even when a legacy implementation bundles them?
4. How should foreseeable content misuse be threat-modeled alongside prompt/model, data, component, and business-model risks?
5. What evidence demonstrates that a name or category exploits trusted language rather than merely borrowing familiar vocabulary?
6. How should title-agnostic contribution coexist with competency requirements and explicit accountable decision owners?
7. What strategy representation lets teams communicate one concise direction while preserving conflicting goals, exclusions, evidence, and uncertainty underneath it?
8. Which minimum research-governance fields should be mandatory even for lightweight interviews or content tests?
9. How should vocabulary observations be aggregated across roles, expertise levels, locales, disability contexts, and conflicting conceptual models?
10. When does field research that changes the target actor require a fresh product-risk, power, privacy, and affected-party review?
11. Which conclusions may safely come from isolated content testing, and which always require the resolved interface or production behavior?
12. What evidence threshold distinguishes harmless terminology variation from concept drift that changes expected behavior?
13. How should a clarity test represent prior knowledge and immediately preceding interface context without overfitting to one path?
14. Which sensitive-state scenarios are mandatory by product domain, and who has authority to define or waive them?
15. When is a domain term clearer than a plain-language substitute across novice, expert, multilingual, and assistive-technology audiences?
16. Which confirmation consequences require explicit action labels rather than question-aligned “Yes” and “No” controls?
17. What runtime confidence is required before a message may name a cause rather than only the detected condition?
18. Which error distinctions must be deliberately collapsed to prevent account enumeration, fraud leakage, or privacy disclosure?
19. How should retries model idempotency, duplicate effects, progressive lockout, and diminishing likelihood of success?
20. Which error states require success confirmation, restored focus, preserved input, or an explicit human handoff?
21. What severity and rights criteria prevent low-prevalence stress cases from being deprioritized solely by volume or revenue?
22. Which sensitive personal-data purposes justify collection, and which should force omission even when an optional field is possible?
23. How should identity terminology governance capture community disagreement without converting a majority preference into an individual label?
24. What locale-specific data model supports pronouns and grammatical agreement without exposing identity unnecessarily?
25. Which accessibility claims can be verified automatically, which require expert manual inspection, and which require disabled-user participation?
26. How should semantic and focus order be validated when visual order, DOM order, bidirectional text, and responsive layouts diverge?
27. Which voice characteristics are stable across brand and products, and which should be explicitly overridden by task, role, channel, or risk state?
28. How can inter-reviewer agreement test whether a voice attribute is operational rather than an attractive but ambiguous adjective?
29. What evidence demonstrates that personalization increases useful connection without increasing privacy, identity, or surveillance harm?
30. Which cultural references, idioms, humor patterns, and sentence-level cues require transcreation or suppression by locale?
31. What state taxonomy should govern when brand expression recedes and which content elements are prohibited under urgency or distress?
32. Which tone dimensions are independently controllable, and which combinations create contradictions such as urgent-but-playful or reassuring-but-uncertain?
33. What product events justify a tone change without collecting or inferring sensitive emotional data?
34. How should audit coverage prove inclusion of rare, asynchronous, personalized, permission-dependent, and inaccessible states?
35. Which semantic fields must remain invariant when generating or testing tone variants?
36. What governance prevents a tone profile from becoming a psychological label attached to a person?
37. Which unanswered content questions should block release, and who has authority to accept residual uncertainty for each risk class?
38. How should community guidance be promoted into canonical policy without converting popularity or seniority into evidence?
39. What representation keeps maps, flows, prototypes, localized resources, code, and runtime observations synchronized without declaring one tool universally canonical?
40. Which critique feedback must override creator-requested scope because it concerns safety, rights, accessibility, privacy, or factual accuracy?
41. What evidence threshold promotes a local solution into a reusable semantic pattern, and what events trigger deprecation or migration?
42. Which adoption and parity measures show that a content-aware design system is used correctly rather than merely referenced or mandated?
