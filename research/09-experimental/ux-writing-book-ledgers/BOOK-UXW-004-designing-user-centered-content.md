## Source-position records

These records preserve separately attributed foreword and author positions. They are not universal findings or approved `content.md` behavior.

### `UXDUC-C01` — conditioning, context, and content are proposed as interacting usability factors

- **Locator:** Foreword, printed pages xi–xiii; PDF pages 12–14.
- **Documented source position:** Foreword author Kirk St.Amant proposes a “3Cs” frame: people learn interaction through instruction, observation, or independent use; use occurs in situated contexts; and content helps people accomplish objectives within those contexts. He presents UX writing as a synthesis that can map these factors.
- **Candidate implication:** Model prior learning, familiarity, environment, task, device, social conditions, and content/interaction contract as distinct contextual evidence. Treat instruction, observation, and exploration as possible learning paths to test rather than a fixed user classification.
- **Boundary:** This is the foreword author's framing, not the textbook authors' validated taxonomy. Three categories do not exhaust cognition, culture, disability, language, power, motivation, or situated use, and “cognitive dynamics” require qualified research rather than persona inference.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-situated-usability-frame; source-attribution-and-research-required`.

### `UXDUC-C02` — the textbook deliberately integrates academic, industry, and pedagogical methods

- **Locator:** Foreword, printed pages xii–xiii; Preface, printed pages xv–xvi; PDF pages 13–17.
- **Documented source position:** The book combines technical communication, design thinking, content strategy, UX research, interface design, analytics, and later AI chapters into a teachable process. It is intended as both an introductory textbook and an adaptable practitioner playbook.
- **Candidate implication:** Preserve theory, empirical citation, practitioner snapshot, illustrative example, checklist, discussion prompt, and learning activity as different source classes. Compare the textbook's integrated curriculum with practitioner workflows without treating pedagogical sequence as production governance.
- **Boundary:** Breadth and citation do not prove each method, claim, or example is current, transferable, or sufficiently evidenced. Educational simplification, historical examples, and exercises need separate scrutiny before operational use.
- **Promotion status:** `source-method-frame; evidence-types-separated`.

### `UXDUC-C03` — UX spans the whole interaction and is not limited to screens

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 3–7.
- **Documented source position:** The authors define UX broadly across interactions with an organization, product, service, and content, including digital and physical settings. They reject reducing design to visual attractiveness and emphasize people, technology, society, business, underlying problems, and environment.
- **Candidate implication:** Scope a UX-writing request across actors, service moments, channels, physical/digital contexts, dependencies, policy, support, and downstream effects. Permit the output to identify a product, service, environment, or behavior problem that wording cannot solve.
- **Boundary:** “All aspects” is too broad for one practitioner or model to observe or control. Research scope, accountable ownership, affected parties, and material exclusions must be explicit; pleasantness and invisibility are not universal UX goals.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-whole-experience-scope-candidate; ownership-and-coverage-required`.

### `UXDUC-C04` — experience design is intentional, researched, and cross-disciplinary

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 2–7.
- **Documented source position:** Modern writers participate before interface completion, working with engineers and designers to investigate needs and shape the experience. The chapter argues that good UX is produced by deliberate decisions even when users do not consciously notice them.
- **Candidate implication:** Include content work in discovery, product definition, behavior, prototyping, implementation, and evaluation. Trace intentional decisions to evidence and runtime rather than equating a seamless or unnoticed interaction with success.
- **Boundary:** Intention does not prove benefit, and “invisible” UX can hide defaults, data collection, persuasion, labor, exclusion, or lost control. Some users need explicit state, provenance, instructions, and confirmation rather than frictionless operation.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-early-design-participation-candidate; transparency-and-outcome-controls`.

### `UXDUC-C05` — content-first design begins with the needed meaning rather than an empty interface

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 7–8.
- **Documented source position:** The authors describe content-first design as determining what people need before designing an interface, rather than creating a layout and filling it afterward. They associate early content integration with user relevance and organizational efficiency.
- **Candidate implication:** Draft a semantic content and behavior contract before surface expression where useful, then co-evolve it with interaction, visual, accessibility, technical, locale, and domain constraints. Record unresolved facts instead of inventing copy to fit a completed frame.
- **Boundary:** Content-first is an entry strategy, not a universal phase order or proof of return on investment. Some spatial, embodied, visual, safety, or technical constraints must be explored concurrently or earlier.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-content-first-entry-candidate; reciprocal-design-and-evidence-required`.

### `UXDUC-C06` — UX writing includes multimodal content and an iterative lifecycle

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 8–10 and 19–20.
- **Documented source position:** The chapter treats text, images, animation, sound, haptics, and combinations as content mediating an experience. UX writing is also described as a process of research, writing, design, iteration, strategy, development, deployment, and management across channels.
- **Candidate implication:** Give one semantic message stable identity across visual, textual, audio, haptic, and interaction expressions while recording modality-specific timing, priority, alternatives, accessibility, and behavior. Model lifecycle state and provenance beyond the initial string.
- **Boundary:** Calling all modalities “writing” does not grant a writer competency or authority over illustration, sound, motion, haptics, accessibility engineering, or interaction implementation. Modalities are not interchangeable and require specialized evidence.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-multimodal-lifecycle-candidate; specialist-authority-required`.

### `UXDUC-C07` — interdisciplinary range does not make one person a universal specialist

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 7–12 and 16–17.
- **Documented source position:** The “UX writing compass” connects research, content strategy, design thinking, interface design, technical communication, marketing, analytics, and technology. The authors use “UX writer” as an umbrella for varied work and note that practitioners cannot perform every competency perfectly.
- **Candidate implication:** Maintain a capability map with task, required competency, evidence, accountable role, contributor, reviewer, and escalation rather than assigning authority from the umbrella title. Compose cross-functional teams according to project and risk.
- **Boundary:** The chapter sometimes shifts from “may perform” to broad statements about what writers do. Role flexibility must not erase research ethics, statistical, accessibility, privacy, legal, domain, marketing, engineering, security, or release expertise.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-competency-routing-candidate; title-does-not-confer-authority`.

### `UXDUC-C08` — user and organizational goals coexist but must remain independently inspectable

- **Locator:** Preface, printed page xvi; Chapter 1, printed pages 8–15 and 19–20.
- **Documented source position:** The authors repeatedly define UX writing as helping people perform desired tasks while serving marketing, sales, engagement, retention, and growth objectives. They position content strategy at the intersection of user needs, organizational context, and business goals.
- **Candidate implication:** Represent user task/outcome, organization goal, purchaser goal, affected-party interests, evidence, conflicts, tradeoffs, hard constraints, and decision authority separately. Prevent business success from compensating for deception, harm, exclusion, or loss of agency.
- **Boundary:** “Balance” is not an operational or ethical decision rule. Users, customers, purchasers, subjects, workers, and bystanders may differ, and legitimate rights or safety constraints do not become negotiable because a growth metric improves.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-goal-separation-candidate; noncompensatory-rights-controls-required`.

### `UXDUC-C09` — attention and continued use are business outcomes, not intrinsic user benefits

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 3–4 and 12–14.
- **Documented source position:** The chapter invokes an attention economy in which products compete for limited time and describes attracting attention, encouraging continued use, and creating memorable experiences as UX-writing goals.
- **Candidate implication:** Classify attention, time spent, return frequency, retention, and engagement as organization-facing or ambiguous metrics. Pair them with task success, voluntary value, effort, agency, wellbeing, accessibility, and harm guardrails; document whether less use is a successful outcome.
- **Boundary:** More attention or continued use can indicate confusion, compulsion, obstruction, or lack of alternatives. The chapter's restaurant and product analogies do not establish that engagement causes satisfaction or that a person should be persuaded to remain.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-engagement-metric-boundary; user-value-evidence-required`.

### `UXDUC-C10` — personalized intelligent content requires structured meaning and governed context

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 10 and 13–16.
- **Documented source position:** The authors describe reusable, machine-readable content separated from interface layout and delivered according to location, query, prior ratings, channel, and context. They connect structured content, voice recognition, natural-language processing, analytics, and AI in a restaurant-recommendation example.
- **Candidate implication:** Model semantic content independently from presentation with source, schema, context inputs, eligibility, freshness, locale, ranking rationale, uncertainty, accessible alternatives, data authority, and user control. Permit deterministic, retrieval, ranking, or generative implementations only after problem-specific evaluation.
- **Boundary:** People do not universally expect or want personalization, and the example compresses distinct systems into an apparently seamless response. Location, history, inferred preference, rankings, and voice data raise privacy, bias, safety, accuracy, consent, and driving-distraction risks.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-structured-contextual-content-candidate; privacy-and-system-evidence-required`.

### `UXDUC-C11` — shareable content creates multi-actor and downstream-context obligations

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed page 13.
- **Documented source position:** The chapter notes that content moves among social, web, and app channels and can be commented on, liked, redistributed, and used to coordinate group activity. Organizations consequently care about how an experience performs across networks and reviews.
- **Candidate implication:** Record sharing audience, recipient roles, context loss, permissions, preview, attribution, expiry, revocation, privacy, abuse, accessibility, locale, and downstream edits. Distinguish original task success from virality or reputation management.
- **Boundary:** Shareability is not inherently beneficial and can expose location, identity, private status, harassment targets, misinformation, or content outside its valid context. Negative reviews are stakeholder evidence, not a reason to suppress criticism or optimize manipulation.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-shareability-context-candidate; downstream-harm-controls-required`.

### `UXDUC-C12` — behavioral influence requires autonomy and manipulation review

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 13–14.
- **Documented source position:** The chapter identifies nudges, social proof, reviews, humor, and interface design as techniques intended to alter behavior, encourage return, increase purchase confidence, or change a negative experience into a positive one.
- **Candidate implication:** For any influence intervention, record actor, target behavior, user and organization benefit, evidence, disclosure, freedom to refuse, reversibility, targeting data, vulnerable populations, competing choices, dark-pattern risks, guardrails, and accountable approval.
- **Boundary:** A review count does not ensure purchase confidence, and humor cannot ensure reassurance. Influence may become coercion, deception, addiction, discriminatory targeting, or concealment of an actual error; conversion and positive affect do not prove informed autonomous choice.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-influence-governance-candidate; autonomy-and-harm-gates-required`.

### `UXDUC-C13` — behavioral analytics are ambiguous observations with privacy costs

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 13–14 and 15–16.
- **Documented source position:** The authors recommend examining interaction before, during, and after deployment and explicitly note that time on a page may reflect either success or confusion. They introduce time-on-task, evaluation scales, analytics, and data visualization as part of the UX-writing toolkit.
- **Candidate implication:** Define event, purpose, lawful/organizational authority, data minimization, consent where applicable, retention/access, identity risk, metric construct, alternative explanations, baseline, uncertainty, guardrails, and decision use. Triangulate analytics with qualitative and task evidence.
- **Boundary:** Observable behavior does not reveal what a person thinks or feels, and surveillance can change or harm the experience it purports to improve. Writers do not acquire analytics or causal-inference competence from role breadth alone.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-analytics-evidence-boundary; privacy-and-inference-controls-required`.

### `UXDUC-C14` — usable error content is bound to placement, context, and recovery

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 14–15.
- **Documented source position:** A form comparison shows the same basic information becoming more usable when an error appears beside the relevant field with corrective guidance. The example presents writing, placement, and recovery as one design problem.
- **Candidate implication:** Bind error meaning to affected control, visible placement, programmatic association, state, cause confidence, correction, focus and announcement, preserved input, resubmission, and success confirmation. Evaluate the implemented form rather than the text alone.
- **Boundary:** A screenshot cannot establish accessibility, runtime validation, reading order, localization, or successful recovery. “Best practice” status requires current component, platform, standards, and user evidence.
- **Promotion status:** `corroborates-resolved-error-contract; implementation-evidence-required`.

### `UXDUC-C15` — marketing expression and task guidance need explicit scope boundaries

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 14–15 and 19–20.
- **Documented source position:** The authors place UX writing between technical communication and creative copywriting and include selling, desirability, sales promotion, and customer attraction within its objectives.
- **Candidate implication:** Classify each message by task guidance, factual explanation, marketing persuasion, legal disclosure, support, or mixed purpose. Assign specialized owner/reviewer, prohibit disguised promotion in required workflows, and separate conversion evidence from usability and user benefit.
- **Boundary:** “Poetically weaving” technical facts with sales language can obscure qualifications, exaggerate capability, or exploit trust. Marketing goals must not control safety, consent, privacy, error, account, payment, cancellation, or other consequential content.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-purpose-and-role-separation-candidate; no-disguised-persuasion`.

### `UXDUC-C16` — technology awareness supports collaboration but not automatic tool authority

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 15–17.
- **Documented source position:** The chapter surveys location, speech recognition, natural-language processing, analytics, AI, cloud collaboration, graphics, component content management, structured authoring, visualization, prototyping, and social-media tooling. It presents technology selection as part of dynamic content work.
- **Candidate implication:** Maintain a tool/dependency record with bounded purpose, data handled, provider/version, permissions, output contract, evidence, accessibility, security/privacy, locale, owner, monitoring, fallback, and retirement. Route selection and operation to qualified roles.
- **Boundary:** The simplified pipeline does not establish how current systems work, and a list of tools is not a competency model. Technology novelty or dynamic delivery cannot justify unnecessary data, probabilistic output, vendor dependence, or unverified automation.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-technology-awareness; provider-and-competency-governance-required`.

### `UXDUC-C17` — career and salary evidence is time-bound and distinct from capability value

- **Locator:** Chapter 1, printed pages 17–19.
- **Documented source position:** The authors cite 2019 and 2021 surveys and an archived job posting to show role growth, salary variation by title, demographics, experience, and demand for broad skills. They use this evidence to motivate students and portfolio development.
- **Candidate implication:** Keep labor-market claims with date, geography, currency, sample, recruitment, title definition, employment type, compensation basis, source quality, and uncertainty. Separate learner/career guidance from evidence that a UX-writing capability improves products.
- **Boundary:** Maximum salaries, voluntary surveys, job titles, and archived postings are not current representative compensation estimates or proof of field growth, equity, competence, or user benefit. These figures require fresh labor-market verification before reuse.
- **Promotion status:** `career-context-only; current-market-verification-required`.

### `UXDUC-C18` — UX content is both a deployed product and an evolving process

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed pages 23–25 and 39–40.
- **Documented source position:** The authors describe content as an experience-bearing deliverable and UX writing as the recursive work that researches, plans, creates, implements, evaluates, and refines it across devices, contexts, and user groups. They associate the process with satisfaction, brand loyalty, equity, ethics, and justice.
- **Candidate implication:** Separate content identity, semantic version, expression, deployment, performance observation, maintenance, and retirement from the workflow and decisions that create them. Track each claimed user, organization, equity, and ethical outcome independently.
- **Boundary:** A content artifact is not a self-contained product when behavior, policy, interface, data, and service determine the experience. Iteration does not itself produce justice or equity, and broad outcome claims require affected-population evidence and governance.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-content-product-process-separation; outcome-evidence-required`.

### `UXDUC-C19` — the three continuums are comparative lenses rather than exclusive categories

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed pages 25–28 and 39–40.
- **Documented source position:** The chapter proposes narrow/wide, machine-/human-focused, and content-/people-oriented continuums to discuss UX-writing purpose, method, and outcome. It repeatedly notes that the poles overlap and are not mutually exclusive.
- **Candidate implication:** Treat the continuums as configurable analysis views over smaller typed attributes, allowing one artifact or decision to occupy several positions with rationale and evidence. Do not persist a single categorical label as canonical identity.
- **Boundary:** Three axes do not exhaust risk, modality, lifecycle, locale, authority, audience, channel, or semantic purpose, and the endpoints combine unlike constructs. Their pedagogical usefulness is not evidence of psychometric validity or complete taxonomy coverage.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-taxonomy-projection; nonexclusive-and-noncanonical`.

### `UXDUC-C20` — local interaction details and system-wide obligations require different evidence

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed pages 25–26.
- **Documented source position:** “Narrow” UX writing covers controls, assistance, forms, logins, accuracy, efficiency, and localized conventions, while “wide” concerns include standards, culture, accessibility, privacy, ethics, sustainability, health, and harm across populations and contexts.
- **Candidate implication:** Link local message/component evidence to applicable organization-, product-, jurisdiction-, population-, and ecosystem-level constraints. Require local expressions to inherit hard external obligations while preserving contextual exceptions and scope.
- **Boundary:** The chapter's wide-UX examples are aspirations rather than operational criteria: accessibility “to everyone from anywhere,” doing no unintended harm, and universal generalization cannot be proven. Standards and regional cultures are also context-specific and versioned.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-local-system-scope-candidate; universal-claims-rejected`.

### `UXDUC-C21` — machine-focused content raises audience, agency, and consequential-decision risks

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed pages 26–28.
- **Documented source position:** The chapter expands audience awareness to software that parses, ranks, or generates language, using applicant tracking, automated recruiting, and chatbots as examples. Writers are asked to account for how machines weight information and to make chatbot interaction feel natural and responsive to human needs.
- **Candidate implication:** Identify every machine consumer or producer, its provider/version, task, input schema, ranking or transformation, authority, affected people, explainability, contestability, bias evidence, audit, fallback, and human review. Keep machine-readable semantics distinct from human-facing expression.
- **Boundary:** Optimizing applications for opaque screening can legitimize discriminatory or invalid systems, and “humanizing” a bot can deceive people about agency, emotion, competence, or accountability. Writers cannot infer or guarantee emotional understanding.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-machine-content-governance-candidate; consequential-and-deception-review-required`.

### `UXDUC-C22` — content substance and organizational governance are interdependent dimensions

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed page 28.
- **Documented source position:** The cited content-strategy quad separates goals, audience, messaging, voice, structure, and access from roles, workflow, policies, standards, and lifecycle maintenance. The authors use it to show that content and the people/system managing it cannot be designed independently.
- **Candidate implication:** Maintain separate but linked semantic/content, structure, workflow, role, policy, standard, and governance records. A content decision is incomplete without accountable ownership and maintenance; governance is incomplete without the content purpose it protects.
- **Boundary:** “Content-oriented” and “people-oriented” can obscure technology, affected noncontributors, labor, enforcement, and external authority. The cited government summary and 2011 framework require source/version verification before adoption.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-content-governance-link-candidate; expanded-actor-and-system-model-required`.

### `UXDUC-C23` — the lifecycle separates requirements, strategy, planning, development, management, delivery, evaluation, and optimization

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed pages 28–30 and 39–40.
- **Documented source position:** The authors adapt a five-stage government lifecycle into a larger circuit beginning with research and requirements, then strategy, planning, development and management, coordinated delivery, implemented evaluation, performance tracking, and optimization. They distinguish interdependent stages that practitioners often conflate.
- **Candidate implication:** Give each lifecycle state defined inputs, outputs, evidence, owner, authority, dependencies, exit/return criteria, version, and status. Preserve differences among researched need, strategic direction, plan, draft, managed asset, approved expression, build, release, observed runtime, finding, and optimization proposal.
- **Boundary:** The lifecycle is a conceptual map rather than a universal production sequence. Publication does not always precede meaningful testing, “ready” requires explicit gates, and optimization must not imply that all tracked metrics should increase.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-content-lifecycle-candidate; explicit-state-and-gate-semantics-required`.

### `UXDUC-C24` — post-release learning is part of design but cannot bypass release governance

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed pages 29–30 and 38–39.
- **Documented source position:** The textbook extends delivery into testing implemented content, tracking context-appropriate indicators, interpreting reactions and use, and planning further optimization. New evidence can reopen research, strategy, planning, or creation.
- **Candidate implication:** Link release version to instrumentation, exposure, observation window, metrics and guardrails, qualitative feedback, incidents, uncertainty, decision rule, rollback, and the exact lifecycle states reopened. Separate maintenance fixes from experiments and strategic redesign.
- **Boundary:** Production users are not automatically research participants, and “continuous improvement” can become continuous surveillance or uncontrolled experimentation. Consent/privacy, experiment integrity, accessibility, safety, incident response, and release authority remain required.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-post-release-learning-candidate; monitoring-and-experiment-governance-required`.

### `UXDUC-C25` — theories from adjacent fields are sources of hypotheses, not interchangeable laws

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed pages 30–33.
- **Documented source position:** The authors identify psychology, human factors, rhetoric/philosophy, and business/project management as major influences on UX writing, alongside design studies. Each supplies different concepts for perception, interaction, persuasion, ethics, feasibility, collaboration, and reuse.
- **Candidate implication:** Record every imported theory with discipline, construct, source, population, method, intended inference, applicability, criticism, and qualified reviewer. Prevent a named theory or familiar law from automatically generating a content rule.
- **Boundary:** The selected disciplines are not exhaustive, and borrowing across them can strip context or overstate consensus. Sociology, disability studies, linguistics, anthropology, law, critical race/gender studies, safety, security, and domain science may be essential.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-cross-disciplinary-evidence-boundary; construct-validity-required`.

### `UXDUC-C26` — cognitive-load and memory guidance must not become universal simplification formulas

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed page 31.
- **Documented source position:** The chapter links working memory, effort conservation, chunking, categorization, and aging to presentation choices, arguing that reduced unnecessary effort and structured information can support task performance and retention.
- **Candidate implication:** Treat load as a contextual interaction hypothesis involving intrinsic task complexity, prior knowledge, structure, choices, timing, modality, device, language, stress, and accessibility. Evaluate comprehension and task performance rather than applying fixed chunk counts or brevity thresholds.
- **Boundary:** Human brains do not uniformly “prefer” one presentation, aging does not imply one memory profile, and chunking can hide relationships or increase navigation effort. Cognitive and disability claims require current qualified evidence and representative testing.
- **Promotion status:** `qualified-cognitive-load-candidate; no-universal-formula-or-age-proxy`.

### `UXDUC-C27` — human factors connects content to physical action, safety, and error prevention

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed page 31.
- **Documented source position:** The authors connect physiological and psychomotor factors with productivity, safety, error prevention, control display, typography, color, paragraph length, and interfaces across sizes.
- **Candidate implication:** Bind wording to target size, input method, reach/motor demand, focus, timing, contrast, reflow, error tolerance, device/environment, fatigue, and safety consequences. Route ergonomic and accessibility claims to qualified evaluation.
- **Boundary:** Font, color, paragraph length, and button display are not solely writing decisions, and productivity may conflict with safety or access. The brief overview cannot establish conformance or population-specific ergonomics.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-content-human-factors-link; implementation-and-specialist-evidence-required`.

### `UXDUC-C28` — rhetoric makes influence visible and therefore requires ethical limits

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed pages 31–32.
- **Documented source position:** Rhetoric is presented as a tradition for identifying available means of informing or motivating people in a situation, paired with philosophical questions of ethics and virtue. The authors connect persuasive effectiveness, engagement, and social good.
- **Candidate implication:** Represent rhetorical purpose, audience, speaker/agent, power relationship, appeal, target behavior, alternatives, disclosure, evidence, autonomy, vulnerable groups, and ethical reviewer. Keep information, recommendation, persuasion, and coercion distinguishable.
- **Boundary:** Ethical intent cannot make all influence acceptable, and “social good” is contested. Rhetorical effectiveness, engagement, or organizational approval does not prove truth, consent, fairness, or user benefit.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-rhetorical-purpose-candidate; autonomy-and-power-review-required`.

### `UXDUC-C29` — commercial and project-management constraints shape workflow but do not define success

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed page 32.
- **Documented source position:** The authors acknowledge that UX primarily serves commercial needs and adopts Agile, Scrum, Lean, and structured-authoring practices to support feasible, profitable, collaborative work and resource efficiency.
- **Candidate implication:** Record budget, time, staffing, release, profitability, procurement, and workflow constraints separately from user, affected-party, quality, and hard-control outcomes. Adapt process to context without allowing delivery cadence or commercial priority to become evidence.
- **Boundary:** Named methods vary by implementation and can create speed pressure, fragmented research, labor harms, or performative collaboration. Profitability and efficiency cannot compensate for safety, rights, accessibility, privacy, truth, or valid consent.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-commercial-constraint-separation; methods-not-success-proxies`.

### `UXDUC-C30` — structured authoring separates reusable semantics from presentation

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed pages 32–33.
- **Documented source position:** A fictional documentation case illustrates semantic markup and single sourcing: common content can be updated once and republished across many outputs, improving consistency and reducing repeated labor while formatting remains separate.
- **Candidate implication:** Give reusable content stable semantic identity, schema/type, source, applicability, variables, locale, dependency graph, transformations, validation, version, owner, and propagation preview. Require impact analysis, approval, tests, migration, and rollback for shared changes.
- **Boundary:** Reuse is unsafe when contexts differ in actor, state, jurisdiction, product version, consequence, or terminology. XML is one historical implementation option, and a fictional efficiency example does not prove lower cost or semantic correctness.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-structured-content-candidate; context-and-propagation-controls-required`.

### `UXDUC-C31` — design thinking is presented as socially responsive, participatory, and recursive

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed pages 33–35.
- **Documented source position:** The chapter contrasts efficiency-centered design science with a later social turn and participatory methods, then presents empathize, define, ideate, prototype, and test as a cycle intended to incorporate lived experience and collective expectations.
- **Candidate implication:** Use the five phases as a navigational projection over research, synthesis, candidate generation, materialization, and evaluation records. Allow loops, parallel work, compression, and reopening while preserving why a phase was entered and what evidence changed.
- **Boundary:** The compressed history is selective, and the branded five-phase model is not the origin or universal form of participatory design. A cycle diagram cannot ensure participation, social responsiveness, innovation, or ethical outcomes.
- **Promotion status:** `strong-iterative-scaffolding-candidate; historical-and-participatory-limits-retained`.

### `UXDUC-C32` — design-thinking mindsets are facilitation heuristics, not release criteria

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed pages 35–36.
- **Documented source position:** The cited mindset encourages showing ideas, centering human values, clarifying messy problems, experimenting through prototypes, tracking process, favoring action, and collaborating across backgrounds.
- **Candidate implication:** Translate each mindset into optional practices with intended purpose, applicability, risks, evidence, and counterbalance. For example, pair action bias with safety review, “show” with accessible alternatives, and collaboration with explicit decision rights.
- **Boundary:** “Bias toward action” can cause premature building; “radical collaboration” can tokenize; “craft clarity” can erase disagreement; and “human values” is undefined. None proves readiness, inclusion, validity, or approval.
- **Promotion status:** `candidate-design-mindset-view; non-gating-and-counterbalanced`.

### `UXDUC-C33` — empathy requires inquiry, positional reflexivity, and limits on inference

- **Locator:** Chapter 2, printed page 36.
- **Documented source position:** The first phase asks writers to delay solution selection, listen and observe, interact with people about tasks and struggles, and examine how their own positionality, power, and privilege shape interpretation. The chapter favors firsthand accounts over guessing.

- **Boundary:** Age, student status, activity, device ownership, search speed, cooking interests, and screen comfort are not a coherent user type. A scenario can bias behavior and exclude nonvisual or non-touch interaction.

## Book-level synthesis

The complete textbook contributes a broad instructional map rather than a validated universal standard:

1. UX writing participates across research, strategy, synthesis, ideation, prototyping, evaluation, structured delivery, analytics, maintenance, career presentation, and machine-assisted work.
2. Every transition must preserve evidence type and authority: client assertions, participant observations, analyst interpretations, generated hypotheses, team decisions, approvals, implementations, releases, and production signals are not interchangeable.
3. Personas, journeys, inventories, audits, maps, metrics, portfolios, prompts, and AI outputs are purpose-specific projections with lineage, uncertainty, scope, version, access, and expiry—not canonical truth.
4. Output genres require distinct behavioral contracts: onboarding, help, errors, forms, legal notices, settings/specifications, video, and voice each bind meaning to state, implementation, access, recovery, privacy, and user control.
5. Research validity depends on method, population, construct, consent, protocol, analysis, negative cases, and decision scope; “empathy,” stakeholder agreement, eye movement, votes, or generated simulations cannot substitute for those controls.
6. Measurement separates events, metrics, indicators, targets, benchmarks, attribution models, qualitative interpretation, user outcomes, organization outcomes, guardrails, and incident triggers; optimization is never self-authorizing.
7. Portfolio narratives can communicate evidence but cannot independently prove authorship, capability, causal impact, confidentiality compliance, or fair employability.
8. Generative AI adds candidate-producing, transformation, inference, and execution layers. It requires task typing, data and rights authority, model/prompt/run provenance, evaluation, meaningful human control, monitoring, correction, rollback, labor review, and an explicit nonuse option.
9. No-code automation remains executable production software with credentials, data flows, failure modes, approval gates, observability, recovery, and accountable ownership.
10. Brand, conversationality, positivity, engagement, speed, polish, popularity, and professional advocacy remain subordinate to truth, safety, accessibility, autonomy, privacy, nondiscrimination, legal/domain authority, and user remedy.

The book's distinctive value is curricular integration: it connects technical communication, design thinking, content strategy, research, structured authoring, analytics, recognizable genres, professional portfolios, and early generative-AI practice in one teachable sequence. Its principal limitations are broad umbrella terminology, pedagogical simplification, U.S.- and English-centered examples, time-bound labor and technology claims, weakly supported numeric heuristics, commercial-growth framing, conflation of empathy with evidence, simplified accessibility and legal treatment, and 2023-era AI/product descriptions. These limitations are retained rather than silently promoted.
